# Chief Communications Officer Coordination Pattern — Head Owns the Substance, CCO Owns the Delivery

## Motivation

The Chief Communications Officer owns the enterprise's public
voice — media relations, executive communications, internal
communications, brand and reputation management, and the
crisis-communications discipline at parent scope. The head of
AI governance owns the *AI-substantive* content that the CCO's
function delivers: the public trust posture on AI, the
technical and governance framing of AI-incident disclosure, the
positioning language for regulator-facing statements, and the
substance of media-facing collateral (executive interviews,
op-eds, keynote positions, transparency-report AI chapters).

Neither of these officers can carry the other's craft. A head
who tries to run public communications independently — placing
op-eds without CCO involvement, briefing journalists without
CCO on the call, drafting crisis statements the CCO does not
sign — damages the enterprise's public voice and eventually
becomes a public-relations story in their own right. A CCO who
tries to draft AI-substantive content without the head — using
generic AI-governance language, mischaracterising the AIA
process, understating the technical severity of a live
incident, overstating the AI-safety posture — creates the two
failure modes the AI-governance function cannot survive: the
regulator-facing statement that later reads as materially
misleading, and the incident disclosure that the CISO's
counsel and the GC's litigation posture cannot defend.

The coordination pattern in this chapter is the division-of-
labour discipline that keeps the head on substance and the
CCO on delivery, with joint sign-off on every AI-facing
external statement. It composes with the CISO coordination
from chapter [[01-ciso-coordination]] (the joint incident
response Surface 4, where communications is a joint sign-off
gate), with the General Counsel coordination from chapter
[[06-general-counsel-coordination]] (legal review sits
alongside CCO delivery on every regulator-facing statement),
and with the external-positioning discipline from
[[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]] where the
public trust posture is authored.

The pattern draws on the FTC's guidance on misleading AI
claims (2023 "Keep your AI claims in check" and 2024 "AI
(and other) companies: Quietly changing your terms of service
could be unfair or deceptive"), SEC guidance on cybersecurity-
incident and material-event disclosure (Item 1.05 Form 8-K
under the 2023 Cybersecurity Disclosure Rule), the NIST
CSF 2.0 GOVERN and RESPOND functions on communications, the
IIA / ISACA practice on communicating audit findings and
incidents, and the IABC (International Association of Business
Communicators) and PRSA (Public Relations Society of America)
professional-body standards on crisis communications.

## Core concept — the substance / delivery split

The load-bearing move is the same substance/delivery split
this module has applied to every other adjacency. The head is
substantive author on any AI-facing statement — the words that
describe what the enterprise did, what it did not do, what
the AIA concluded, what the evaluation surfaced, what the
incident scope is, what remediation is in flight. The CCO is
accountable executive for the delivery — the audience choice,
the channel, the timing, the framing that lands with the
intended reader, the coordination with executive
spokespeople, and the internal communications cascade.

Every AI-facing external statement runs through a two-sign-off
gate: substance sign-off by the head, delivery sign-off by
the CCO. Where the statement is regulator-facing, GC's legal
review is a third sign-off (chapter [[06-general-counsel-
coordination]]). Where the statement is incident-related and
touches security, the CISO's sign-off is a fourth
(chapter [[01-ciso-coordination]]). Where the statement
touches privacy, the DPO's sign-off is a further gate
(chapter [[03-cpo-dpo-coordination]]). The multi-sign-off
discipline slows the release of the statement by hours or
days; it is the discipline that prevents the statement from
becoming the next crisis in its own right.

## The four coordination surfaces

### Surface 1 — public trust posture and standing narrative

The enterprise's public trust posture on AI is the standing
narrative the CCO's function carries into every media
engagement, every earnings call AI section, every annual
transparency report, every voluntary-code public statement,
every board-facing all-hands. The head is the substantive
author of the posture; the CCO's function operationalises it
across channels.

The interface at this surface:

- **Standing narrative document.** A 4- to 8-page document
  co-authored by the head and the CCO that names the
  enterprise's AI-governance posture, the specific
  commitments the enterprise has made (voluntary codes
  signed, standards conformance claimed, transparency-report
  cadences established), the current AI system portfolio at
  the granularity the enterprise is willing to disclose
  publicly, and the language the CCO's function uses
  consistently across channels. The document composes with
  the external-positioning artefacts from
  [[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]].
- **Approved-language register.** A short register — a page
  or two — of specific phrasings the head has cleared for
  external use ("we conduct pre-deployment evaluation on
  fairness, safety, and security for high-risk systems"; "we
  operate an AI management system aligned to ISO/IEC 42001";
  "we participate in the EU General-Purpose AI Code of
  Practice") with the substantive basis for each phrasing
  cross-referenced to the AIMS artefacts. The register is
  what the CCO's spokespeople use verbatim; it prevents
  drift and prevents overclaim under the FTC's misleading-
  AI-claims discipline.
- **Prohibited-language register.** The mirror of the
  approved register — phrasings the head has explicitly
  ruled out because they overstate the enterprise's posture
  ("our AI is unbiased"; "our models are safe"; "we have
  eliminated hallucination"; and other categorical claims
  that will not survive first-principles scrutiny). The
  register is the head's protection against the CCO's
  spokesperson under pressure reaching for a phrase that
  cannot be defended.

Cadence: annual joint review at the CCO's brand-and-
positioning calendar; ad-hoc when a new voluntary-code
commitment is signed, a new regulatory obligation is taken
on, or a material AI system enters production with a public
narrative dimension. Joint owner: the head's level-50
architect on substance, the CCO's brand-and-executive-
communications lead on delivery.

### Surface 2 — proactive external communications

Proactive external communications are the enterprise-initiated
statements: transparency-report AI chapters, executive op-eds
on AI-governance topics, keynote positions at industry
conferences, blog-post series on the AI-governance program,
research publications co-authored by the head's function,
regulator-facing voluntary submissions (comment letters on
proposed rules, participation in code-of-practice authoring).

The interface at this surface:

- **Editorial calendar.** A rolling 12-month editorial
  calendar owned by the CCO's function, with the AI-
  governance dimension co-authored with the head. The
  calendar names the piece, the channel, the substantive
  author (head's level-50 architect or head personally for
  senior-visibility pieces), the CCO's delivery owner
  (executive communications lead, brand-communications lead,
  media-relations lead as applicable), the timing, and the
  legal-review requirement (chapter [[06-general-counsel-
  coordination]]).
- **Substantive-drafting workflow.** For each proactive piece,
  the head's function drafts, GC reviews for legal exposure,
  CCO's function reviews for delivery fit (audience, framing,
  timing, channel appropriateness), and the CEO or CAIO
  reviews for enterprise-strategic alignment where the piece
  is under the CEO's or CAIO's byline. The workflow is named
  in the interface document; the sequence is not negotiable
  in the moment.
- **Speaker preparation.** Where the head or another executive
  is presenting on AI governance externally (a keynote, a
  panel, a regulator-facing appearance under
  [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]], an audit-committee-
  visible board briefing), the CCO's function runs media
  training and pre-briefing on the delivery dimension; the
  head runs substantive preparation on the AI-content
  dimension. The head does not step into an external speaking
  slot without CCO pre-briefing on the audience, the room,
  the likely questions, and the delivery norms of the forum.
- **Transparency report AI chapter.** Where the enterprise
  publishes an annual transparency report, ESG report, or
  responsible-AI report, the AI chapter is authored by the
  head's function, edited by the CCO's function for
  audience-fit, reviewed by GC for exposure, and signed off
  jointly. The transparency-report AI chapter composes with
  the audit-committee reporting cluster from
  [[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] and the
  external-positioning discipline from
  [[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]].

Cadence: rolling editorial calendar; per-piece workflow with
lead times matched to the CCO's function's standard editorial
lead times. Joint owner: head's level-50 architect and CCO's
executive-communications lead.

### Surface 3 — reactive external communications on AI news

Reactive external communications are the statements the
enterprise makes in response to external events — a competitor
incident that raises questions about the enterprise's own
posture, a regulator statement or investigation with sector
scope, a journalist inquiry about the enterprise's AI
practices, a civil-society or research publication that names
the enterprise, a viral social-media claim about an AI system
the enterprise operates. These are not the enterprise's own
incidents (that is Surface 4); they are the news cycle
touching the enterprise from outside.

The interface at this surface:

- **Standing response templates.** A small library of
  standing response templates for the recurring reactive
  scenarios — competitor-incident inquiries, regulator-
  statement inquiries, journalist background inquiries, civil-
  society-publication inquiries, social-media-claim
  inquiries. Each template has an approved-language skeleton
  and the substantive-fill-in guidance from the head's
  function. Templates prevent the CCO's function from
  drafting from scratch under time pressure with the risk of
  reaching for prohibited language.
- **Inbound routing.** All AI-substantive external inquiries
  route into the CCO's function's inbound intake (the media-
  relations desk, the executive-communications intake, the
  investor-relations intake as applicable), and the CCO's
  function routes them to the head's function for
  substantive input within a named SLA (typically same-day
  for a journalist inquiry with a deadline; next-business-day
  for background inquiries). The head does not respond to
  inbound inquiries independently.
- **Named-executive-spokesperson protocol.** Where an inbound
  inquiry names the head personally as a requested
  spokesperson (a keynote invitation, a podcast interview
  request, an on-the-record technical question), the CCO's
  function evaluates the fit and the head decides whether to
  accept. The head does not accept independently. The
  discipline is a load-bearing safeguard on the head's
  external voice — the head appears where the CCO has
  concluded the appearance strengthens the enterprise's
  posture, not where the head's personal profile is served.
- **No-comment discipline.** For inquiries where the
  enterprise's posture is "no comment", "we do not comment
  on speculation", or "we will provide a substantive response
  when we have completed our review", the CCO's function
  delivers the no-comment; the head does not fill the silence
  with a substantive holding statement. The head's silence is
  a CCO-authored silence, not an accidental one.

Cadence: continuous inbound; response SLAs matched to the
CCO's function's standard media-relations SLAs. Joint owner:
CCO's media-relations lead and head's level-50 architect.

### Surface 4 — AI-incident crisis communications runbook

The load-bearing surface. When the enterprise has an AI
incident — a production model behaving in ways that produce
customer harm, an evaluation surfacing a serious safety
concern in a shipped system, a jailbreak or prompt-injection
attack that leads to public exposure, a fairness or bias
finding that becomes a story, a regulator opening an
investigation — the CCO's crisis-communications runbook
governs the external voice, and the head is a co-owner on the
AI-substantive side.

The interface at this surface, at incident time:

- **Single crisis-communications bridge.** The CCO's crisis-
  communications bridge is the operational bridge for
  external voice. The head joins; the head does not run a
  parallel comms track. This is the mirror of the CISO's
  single incident bridge from chapter [[01-ciso-coordination]]
  Surface 4; the discipline is the same.
- **Head is substantive lead on AI classification.** The
  first question the crisis bridge asks the head is: what is
  this. Is this a genuine AI-specific failure mode, an
  application-layer failure that surfaced through an AI
  system, an operational failure with an AI dimension, or a
  user-side misuse the enterprise's system did not prevent
  when it could have. The AI classification decides the
  framing. The head owns the answer; the CCO does not
  characterise the incident's AI dimension without the head's
  input.
- **Statement drafting workflow.** The head's function drafts
  the AI-substantive core of the statement (what happened,
  what the enterprise did in response, what the current
  posture is, what the customer or user should do). The CCO's
  function drafts the framing and delivery. GC reviews for
  litigation and regulatory exposure. The CISO (chapter
  [[01-ciso-coordination]]) reviews for security-substantive
  accuracy. The DPO (chapter [[03-cpo-dpo-coordination]])
  reviews where personal data is in scope. The CFO (chapter
  [[07-cfo-coordination]]) reviews where material financial
  exposure is in scope and disclosure obligations may apply.
  The named-executive spokesperson (CEO, CAIO, or head
  depending on scope) delivers. The workflow is written down
  before the incident, rehearsed at least annually, and
  followed under time pressure.
- **Sequencing discipline.** External statements sequence
  after: incident containment is in flight, regulator
  notification obligations (chapter [[06-general-counsel-
  coordination]] and [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]]) have
  been evaluated by GC and the head, and the internal
  communications cascade has begun. Public disclosure that
  precedes regulator notification where notification is
  required is a compounding failure the head refuses.
- **Multi-audience discipline.** The CCO's function
  coordinates the parallel statements for different
  audiences — regulator statement, customer statement,
  employee statement, investor statement, media statement,
  civil-society-stakeholder statement — with the head as
  substantive-consistency owner across all of them. The
  substantive core of each statement is consistent (same
  facts, same causal account, same remediation posture); the
  framing and delivery are audience-adapted.
- **Post-incident narrative arc.** The CCO's function owns
  the multi-day narrative arc — the initial disclosure, the
  progressive updates, the after-action public summary, the
  post-mortem publication where the enterprise's transparency
  posture warrants it. The head co-authors the substantive
  content across the arc. The head does not disappear from
  the narrative after the initial statement — the sustained
  presence is the trust signal.
- **After-action review.** The head and the CCO jointly run
  the after-action review on the communications dimension,
  in parallel with the CISO-side after-action from chapter
  [[01-ciso-coordination]] Surface 4. Both feed the joint
  report to the audit committee.

Cadence: continuous readiness (runbook current, spokespeople
media-trained, standing statements in the library); live
under incident conditions with the CCO's function's standard
incident tempo (statements measured in hours, not days, for
severe incidents). Joint owner: CCO's crisis-communications
lead and head personally (this surface is not delegable to
the level-50 architect at the accountability layer, though
the architect is the substantive author on the drafts).

The crisis-communications runbook composes with the incident-
leadership discipline in [[mod-110-incident-leadership]]. The
incident-leadership module is where the runbook becomes
operational under time pressure across every incident
dimension; this chapter is the *communications-specific*
coordination pattern the head runs with the CCO.

## The interface document

Consolidated in a single interface document co-signed by the
head and the CCO, ratified in the CxO adjacency map. The
document has, at minimum:

1. **The substance-delivery split.** The head is substantive
   author; the CCO is delivery accountable executive; the
   two-sign-off gate governs every AI-facing external
   statement. Named sign-off additions for regulator-facing
   (GC), security-related (CISO), privacy-related (DPO),
   and materially financial (CFO) statements.
2. **The four coordination surfaces.** Public trust posture
   and standing narrative, proactive external communications,
   reactive external communications, and AI-incident crisis
   communications — each with the artefacts, the cadence, and
   the joint owners.
3. **The approved-language and prohibited-language registers.**
   The named phrasings the CCO's spokespeople may use verbatim
   and the categorical claims they may not.
4. **The named-executive spokesperson protocol.** Which
   executive speaks in which forum on AI matters, and the
   process for spokesperson selection on new inbound
   inquiries.
5. **The AI-incident crisis-communications runbook.** The
   named steps, the named owners, the sequencing discipline,
   the multi-audience coordination, and the after-action
   review. The runbook is referenced from the incident-
   leadership discipline in [[mod-110-incident-leadership]].
6. **The escalation ladder.** When the interface breaks — a
   proactive piece placed without head substantive sign-off;
   a reactive statement drafted with prohibited language; a
   crisis-communications statement released before regulator
   notification is evaluated — how the head and the CCO
   escalate. Typically: peer conversation first, then the CEO,
   then the audit-committee chair.
7. **The joint audit-committee appearance.** Once a year, the
   head and the CCO appear jointly at the audit committee on
   the external-communications posture review, presenting
   the standing narrative, the year's communications activity,
   any AI-incident communications after-action, and the
   forward-year editorial calendar.
8. **The review cadence.** Annual review of the interface
   document; ad-hoc on material events (CCO transition, head
   transition, major AI system launch with a public narrative
   dimension, live crisis).

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**The unsigned op-ed.** The head places an op-ed, a LinkedIn
post, a blog piece, or a keynote position externally without
the CCO's function reviewing it for delivery-fit and GC
reviewing it for exposure. The piece contradicts standing
language, understates a known limitation, or overstates the
posture. The CCO's function only learns about it when the
media picks it up. The remedy is: every AI-substantive
external statement runs through the two-sign-off gate; the
head's personal external voice is not exempt.

**The categorical claim under pressure.** Under a journalist's
question, a hostile panel exchange, or an incident-time press
briefing, the head or the CEO reaches for a categorical claim
("our models are unbiased", "we have eliminated hallucination",
"our AI cannot cause harm") that the enterprise's evidence
does not support. The claim becomes the FTC's next
misleading-AI-claims case study. The remedy is: the
approved-language register is memorised; the prohibited-
language register is memorised; media training rehearses the
categorical-claim substitutes ("we test for bias against the
following benchmarks, and here is what we found"; "we have
reduced hallucination on this evaluation set by this measure,
and here is where it still occurs").

**The parallel crisis-comms bridge.** During an AI incident,
the head runs an independent communications track — technical
statement on the head's function's own blog, tweets from the
head's personal account, background briefings the head places
with sympathetic journalists — parallel to the CCO's crisis
bridge. The CCO's function learns about the head's statements
after they land. The remedy is: one bridge, the CCO's
convention, the head joins and co-leads on AI substance.

**The disclosure that precedes obligation evaluation.** The
enterprise publishes an AI-incident statement before GC and
the head have evaluated the regulator-notification obligations
under EU AI Act incident-reporting timelines, sector-specific
disclosure rules, and SEC material-event obligations where
applicable. The disclosure triggers a regulator response the
enterprise was not prepared for. The remedy is: the
sequencing discipline in Surface 4 is not compressed under
time pressure; the CCO's function accepts that the external
statement waits for the obligation evaluation to complete.

**The disappearance after the first statement.** The head
issues the initial substantive statement on an incident and
then goes silent while the CCO's function handles the
progressive updates alone. The narrative arc loses its
substantive anchor; the CCO's function reaches for generic
language in the follow-on updates; the substance drifts. The
remedy is: the head is present across the multi-day arc, at
the level appropriate to the incident's severity, with
substantive co-authorship of every update.

**The internal-external mismatch.** The CCO's function's
external statements say one thing; the head's function's
internal all-hands, employee communications, and internal
FAQ say another. Employees screenshot the internal messages
and post them; the mismatch becomes the story. The remedy is:
the internal communications cascade is co-authored by the CCO
and the head, with the substantive core matching the external
statement.

## Where the discipline earns its keep — the incident-time
## sequencing

The load-bearing test of the coordination pattern is the live
AI incident. At hour one, the CISO's SOC has confirmed a
production AI system is behaving in a way that produced
customer-visible harm — an agentic tool call that exfiltrated
customer-identifying data, an image-generation model producing
content that violated the sanctioned-use policy at scale, a
recommendation system amplifying a demographically skewed
pattern to a customer segment that noticed.

If the coordination pattern is working, the following happens
in the first six to twelve hours: the head and the CCO join
the CISO's incident bridge (Surface 4 in this chapter and
chapter [[01-ciso-coordination]]). The head runs the AI
classification. The CCO's function drafts the initial customer
statement using the approved-language register. GC evaluates
regulator-notification obligations. The head drafts the AI-
substantive core of the statement. The CISO reviews for
security-substantive accuracy. The DPO reviews for personal-
data scope. The CFO reviews for material-financial-exposure
and disclosure obligations. The named-executive spokesperson
is briefed. The statement is released after regulator
notification obligations have been evaluated. The internal
communications cascade begins in parallel. The multi-audience
statements go out with a consistent substantive core.

If the coordination pattern is not working, one of the anti-
patterns above surfaces. The head tweets an initial statement
from a personal account before the CCO's function has
prepared the customer statement; the head's statement uses
"unbiased" or "safe" categorically because it was drafted
without media training's guardrails; the customer statement
lands before the regulator notification, and the regulator's
first read of the incident is on the customer statement; the
internal all-hands the next morning contradicts the customer
statement in three particulars, and screenshots reach the
press by lunchtime. The audit committee reads about the
incident and about the communications failure as a compounded
story; the head's next appearance at the audit committee is
against a very different backdrop.

## Summary

- The head is substantive author on any AI-facing external
  statement; the CCO is accountable executive for delivery.
  The two-sign-off gate governs every statement. Regulator-
  facing, security-related, privacy-related, and materially-
  financial statements add GC, CISO, DPO, and CFO sign-offs.
- Four coordination surfaces carry the work: public trust
  posture and standing narrative, proactive external
  communications, reactive external communications, and AI-
  incident crisis communications.
- The approved-language and prohibited-language registers are
  the load-bearing artefacts against the FTC's misleading-
  AI-claims discipline. Media training rehearses the
  substitutes for categorical claims.
- The AI-incident crisis-communications runbook governs
  Surface 4: one bridge (the CCO's), single-substantive-
  anchor (the head), sequencing discipline (regulator
  notification before public disclosure), multi-audience
  consistency, sustained narrative arc, joint after-action.
- Six anti-patterns to refuse: the unsigned op-ed, the
  categorical claim under pressure, the parallel crisis-
  comms bridge, disclosure that precedes obligation
  evaluation, the disappearance after the first statement,
  the internal-external mismatch.
- The interface document — co-signed by head and CCO — is
  ratified in the CxO adjacency map, reviewed annually, and
  the head and CCO appear jointly at the audit committee on
  the external-communications posture review once a year.
- Chapter [[11-chairing-the-governance-council]] turns
  *inward* — from the outbound CxO adjacencies of chapters
  01–10 to the AI Governance Council the head chairs, and
  the discipline of chairing it well.
