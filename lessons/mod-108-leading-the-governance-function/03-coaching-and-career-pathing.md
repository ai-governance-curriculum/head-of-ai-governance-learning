# The Coaching, Mentoring, and Career-Pathing Cadence

## Motivation

Chapters [[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]] and
[[02-multi-year-hiring-plan]] built the function's shape and
filled it with people. That is the *structural* work. It is not
enough. A well-shaped function of well-selected hires will still
underperform if the head does not run a coaching and career-
pathing cadence that develops individuals over time and gives
them a visible path.

The failure mode here is not visible in a single quarter. It is
visible only in the trailing-twelve-month regretted-loss rate,
in the internal-promotion rate, in the difference between
where a level-15 analyst is at hire and where the same analyst
is eighteen months later. A function whose people are
technically stronger after eighteen months than at hire is a
function that is compounding capacity; a function whose people
are exactly as strong (or weaker, more likely — because talent
that isn't developed atrophies) is a function whose head is
being outpaced by the intake queue.

The cadence in this chapter is what compounds. Not one 1:1 —
a discipline of 1:1s. Not one career-development plan — a
career-development plan per team member, refreshed at cadence.
Not one shadow rotation — a rotation program with named hosts
and a debrief pattern. The compounding effect is what turns
a function of six-to-nine into a function that can genuinely
handle year-two and year-three complexity without the head
becoming the operational bottleneck.

The head is also the model. A head who runs 1:1s religiously
with the CRO and haphazardly with the level-50 architect is a
head whose team notices the asymmetry and reads it as the head
disinterested in their development. Coaching is a discipline
the head runs on themselves before they run it on the team.

## Core concept — the head's development portfolio

The head runs a *development portfolio* against the team, in the
same disciplined sense the head runs a KRI/KPI portfolio at
program scope. The portfolio has:

- **A 1:1 cadence per direct report.** Weekly for most direct
  reports at most levels; fortnightly for very senior direct
  reports where weekly cadence would be over-touch; explicit
  cadence per report, not a default.
- **A career-development plan per direct report.** Written down,
  reviewed at least quarterly, jointly authored, with named
  development actions and honest time-to-readiness reads.
- **A skip-level cadence.** The head talks to the level-15
  analyst and the level-25 engineer, not only to the level-50
  architect. Skip-level is not surveillance; it is the head's
  own signal on team health.
- **A rotation program.** Architect mentorship, evaluation-
  engineer shadowing, and product-team embed rotations, run at
  cadence, with hosts, debriefs, and outcomes documented.
- **A team-level ritual set.** Weekly standing team meeting,
  monthly all-hands with the extended function, quarterly team
  retrospective, annual off-site (where the enterprise's shape
  supports it).

The portfolio is not the head's private discipline. The head
publishes the portfolio's cadence and expectations to the team,
so that the team knows what they can expect from the head and
the head is on the hook for delivering it.

## The 1:1 discipline

The 1:1 is the head's single most important management ritual.
It is where career development happens, where blockers surface,
where the head hears what the team hears from the first-line
product engineers before the head hears it from the product
leadership, and where the head builds the trust that carries
the difficult conversations chapter
[[07-difficult-people-leadership-conversations]] runs.

A 1:1 discipline that works has five properties.

**Recurring, protected, and rarely rescheduled.** Weekly, same
day, same time, in the head's calendar as a standing hold. The
head does not reschedule a 1:1 for a "more important" meeting
absent an actual emergency; the moment the 1:1 becomes movable
in the team's perception, it becomes movable in fact, and the
cadence collapses over a quarter. When the head does have to
reschedule, the head reschedules within the same week and
apologises for the disruption.

**Owned by the direct report, not by the head.** The direct
report brings the agenda. The head's role is to listen first
and to press on the agenda items where the head has a specific
input. The head does have some standing items (the direct
report's development conversation, any specific escalations
from the last week, any coordination the head owes on cross-
function work), but the 1:1 is not the head's status meeting.
A 1:1 that runs as the head's status meeting is a 1:1 the
direct report will begin dreading, and eventually will begin
looking to move away from.

**Includes the development conversation on a rhythm.** Every
1:1 does not need to be a career-development conversation.
Every fourth or so should be — an explicit "how are you
developing against the plan we agreed" check-in that keeps the
development plan a live document rather than a shelf artefact.
The head schedules the development conversation as a specific
agenda item, not as an ambient hope.

**Has notes the head remembers between weeks.** The head takes
brief notes during or immediately after each 1:1 — the specific
items in flight, the specific commitments made by either
party, the specific development points discussed. The head
reviews the notes before the next 1:1. A head who cannot
remember what they discussed last week reads as disengaged, and
the direct report stops bringing the substantive material.

**Ends with commitments both parties can hold each other to.**
The 1:1 does not end at a general vibe. It ends at "you will do
X by next week; I will do Y by next week; we will pick up on Z
in the following 1:1". Both parties leave with a small,
concrete accountability, and both parties honour it. The
compounding trust from consistently honoured 1:1 commitments is
what carries the team through the hard moments.

The head does not delegate the 1:1 to their executive assistant
or to a deputy for direct reports; the 1:1 is the head's own
work. The head does delegate the 1:1 for indirect reports
(level-15 analysts embedded on a product team, for example, whose
solid-line report is a level-35 evaluator, not the head) to the
direct manager and holds the skip-level as the head's own signal.

## The career-development plan

Every direct report has a written career-development plan. It
is not a competency-inventory checkbox exercise; it is the
document that shapes what the direct report works on, what
development investments the head funds, and how progression
happens.

The plan has, at minimum:

- **Current level and role.** The concrete position on the
  ladder from chapter
  [[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]].
- **Next-level competence gate.** The specific competence gates
  the level ladder names for the next progression step, phrased
  concretely against this individual's craft and context.
- **The individual's own career direction.** The head asks; the
  individual answers. Not every level-25 engineer wants to
  progress to level-35 evaluator; some want to deepen as an
  individual-contributor engineer indefinitely. The head
  respects the honest answer and shapes the plan against it.
- **Named development actions.** The specific investments in
  flight — training the individual is doing, certifications the
  individual is pursuing, stretch assignments the individual has
  taken on, mentorship arrangements. Each with a target date
  and a specific expected outcome.
- **Named coaching relationships.** The specific people the
  individual is working with — the mentoring architect, the
  shadowing evaluator, the product-team embed host.
- **Time-to-readiness estimate.** The head's honest read on when
  the individual will meet the next-level competence gate.
  Sometimes that read is "twelve to eighteen months"; sometimes
  it is "unclear given current trajectory — reassess at the
  next quarterly review"; sometimes it is "this individual is
  ready-now for the next step but the enterprise does not have
  the headcount to promote". Each honest read shapes different
  subsequent conversations, and papering over the honest read
  loses the trust the plan is built on.
- **Refresh cadence.** Reviewed quarterly at minimum, and
  updated whenever material development actions land (a
  certification completed, a stretch assignment concluded, a
  competence gate met).

The plan is jointly authored — head and direct report — and
signed off by both. The head does not author a plan and hand
it down; a plan the individual has not helped author is a plan
the individual will not own.

The plan is not the same document as the performance-review
form the enterprise HR system produces. The performance review
is a look-back; the development plan is a look-forward. Both
matter, and both are the head's work.

## The rotation program — architect mentorship, evaluator shadowing, product embed

Beyond the 1:1 and the development plan, the head funds three
specific development rotations that produce the level-transition
capacity chapter [[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]]
maps.

### Architect mentorship

For level-35 evaluators and high-potential level-25 engineers on
a trajectory toward level-50 architecture, the head arranges
formal mentorship with the level-50 architect (or with an
external architect at the head's peer-set, where the enterprise
has only one internal architect and the mentorship would be
uncomfortable to run in-house).

The mentorship is scheduled — an hour every two-to-four weeks —
with a specific development focus: architectural range across
domains, design-authorship craft, the discipline of authoring
design that composes with the enterprise's ISMS, MRM, and
privacy frameworks. The mentor sets exercises (the mentee
takes an assigned design fragment and drafts a proposed
extension; the mentor critiques against the criteria the
architect uses in their own work); the mentee produces work
between sessions; the sessions are conversations about the
work rather than lectures.

The head is not the mentor for the architect track. The head
owns the level-60 program-leadership craft; the level-50
architecture craft is what the level-50 architect owns and
develops. The head funds the mentorship, respects the mentor-
mentee relationship's confidentiality, and reads the mentor's
signal on the mentee's readiness.

### Evaluation-engineer shadowing

For level-15 analysts and level-25 engineers on a trajectory
toward level-35 evaluation, the head funds shadowing rotations
with the evaluation engineer(s). The rotation is not open-ended
observation; it is structured — the shadower participates in a
specific evaluation cycle (a release-assurance sign-off, an
evaluation-harness authoring session, a regulator-facing
evidence-package assembly), does defined work under the
evaluator's supervision, and debriefs on what they learned.

Rotations run for a defined period (typically four to eight
weeks part-time alongside the shadower's standing role, or one
to two weeks full-time), with a debrief at the end that goes
into the shadower's development plan and into the head's
signal on the evaluator's coaching craft.

### Product-team embed rotation

The most delicate of the three rotations. For level-15 analysts
and level-25 engineers whose growth benefits from time inside
the first-line product context, the head arranges embed
rotations with named product teams. The embed is *not* the
Shape B embed from chapter
[[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]] — that is a
standing role. This is a *time-boxed* rotation for a level-15
or level-25 team member whose standing role is centralised.

The embed rotation has specific rules the head enforces:

- **Time-boxed.** Six to twelve weeks. Not open-ended. The
  rotation ends by design so the individual returns to the
  centralised function with fresh product-context insight
  rather than gradually becoming part of the product team.
- **Not solid-line reporting during the rotation.** The
  individual remains solid-line to the head throughout the
  rotation; the product team is the host, not the manager.
- **Specific development objective per rotation.** What the
  individual is going to learn, what they will produce during
  the rotation, what they will bring back to the function.
- **Debrief on return.** The rotation ends with a debrief with
  the head and the level-50 architect — what the individual saw
  in the product context that the function should know about,
  what discipline gaps or strengths the function has when read
  from the product perspective, what the individual now
  understands about first-line pressures that will shape their
  second-line craft.
- **Bounded independence exposure.** The individual is briefed
  before the rotation on the independence discipline in chapter
  [[04-second-line-independence-balance]]. The rotation is a
  development opportunity, not an assimilation risk.

Embed rotations are the strongest single development investment
the head can make in second-line hires who need first-line
context. They are also the highest-risk if unsupervised — an
individual who spends six months embedded in product without
scheduled debrief-and-return can drift into first-line
sympathies and lose the second-line-independence disposition
the function needs. The rules above are what keep the risk
managed.

## The onboarding rotation — the first 90 days

The onboarding rotation is a specific case of the coaching
cadence, run for every new hire, and authored *together* with
the interview loop for the role (chapter
[[02-multi-year-hiring-plan]]). A hire who lands into
"figure out what to do next" is a hire whose first-year
productivity will lag; a hire who lands into a documented
90-day rotation with named hosts, defined outputs, and a first
debrief with the head at day 30 is a hire who begins
contributing meaningfully by month three.

The 90-day rotation, adapted per role, typically looks like:

- **Days 1–7: onboarding week.** Enterprise-standard onboarding
  plus function-specific onboarding: introduction to the
  function's org chart and shape (chapter
  [[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]]); introduction
  to the AIMS scope and operating calendar
  ([[mod-102-running-the-program]] chapters 02, 04); read-in on
  the function's operating charter, the KRI portfolio
  ([[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 01),
  and the current major workstreams; first 1:1 with the head.
- **Days 8–30: shadowing and low-stakes contribution.** The
  hire shadows their level-appropriate peers on the function's
  standing rituals (intake queue, control-testing, evidence-
  collection, risk-register review). Contributes on defined
  low-stakes items — draft an impact-assessment on a low-risk
  system, produce a first-cut evidence review, participate in
  a governance council session as observer.
- **Days 31–60: named first-project ownership.** The hire takes
  ownership of a specific, defined piece of work — an intake
  cluster for a specific product line, a control-testing pass
  on a specific control cluster, an evidence-refresh cycle for
  a specific vendor tier. The work is small enough to complete
  well and important enough to matter. The head or the direct
  manager checks in weekly.
- **Days 61–90: broader integration.** The hire begins the
  standing rotations — the architect mentorship if their
  trajectory calls for it, the evaluation-engineer shadowing
  if theirs does. Attends cross-function partner meetings the
  role sits in on. Contributes to the function's ongoing
  cadence at the full participation level.
- **Day 90: 90-day check-in with the head.** Structured
  conversation: how the onboarding rotation landed for the
  hire, what worked and what did not, what the hire is finding
  hard, what the hire needs. The check-in is the head's
  signal on whether the hire is on trajectory. Serious concerns
  surfaced at day 90 that were not surfaced during the 30- and
  60-day check-ins are a signal the intervening 1:1 discipline
  broke down and the head fixes it.

The rotation is documented per role. When the hire is a level-15
analyst, the rotation names which analyst on the team is the
host for the shadowing week, which architect or evaluator is
the host for the low-stakes contribution phase, and which
partner meetings the hire attends. The rotation is not the head
inventing the plan on day 1 of the hire's start date; the
rotation is a template the function refreshes annually.

## Career-transition patterns the head coaches through

Beyond the level-to-level progression the ladder maps, three
specific career-transition patterns come up in the function that
the head coaches through explicitly.

### The individual-contributor to architect transition

A level-25 engineer or level-35 evaluator whose trajectory is
toward level-50 architecture is moving from doing craft
personally to authoring designs that others execute. The
transition is difficult; individual contributors who love the
craft can feel the transition as a loss even as it is a
promotion. The head's coaching:

- **Name the transition explicitly.** The individual is not
  becoming "a better engineer"; the individual is becoming an
  architect, which is a different discipline that composes with
  engineering craft but is not the same. Naming the transition
  helps the individual grieve the craft they are leaving and
  embrace the discipline they are entering.
- **Give architectural authorship exposure early.** Before the
  formal promotion, invite the individual to co-author small
  design fragments with the level-50 architect. The individual
  tests whether they enjoy the authorship craft; the head tests
  whether the individual has architectural range.
- **Be honest about time-to-readiness.** Architecture craft
  develops over years, not months. Signal readiness when it
  arrives; do not signal readiness prematurely to reward
  effort, and do not withhold signal from someone genuinely
  ready because the requisition is not yet open.

### The technical to management transition

A level-25 or level-35 individual contributor moving into
management responsibility (leading a small team, running a
work-stream with direct reports). Management is a different
craft again, and the technical strength that produced the
promotion opportunity does not guarantee management strength.
The head's coaching:

- **Explicit management coaching investment.** Enrol the
  individual in a management-specific training program (the
  enterprise's leadership-development curriculum, an external
  management coach, or peer coaching with other new managers
  in the function). The investment is a signal that the head
  takes management craft seriously as a distinct discipline.
- **Weekly 1:1s specifically on the management transition.** For
  the first six months of the transition, the head runs 1:1s
  that focus on the specific management challenges the
  individual is encountering — the difficult direct-report
  conversation, the delegation calibration, the boundary
  between coach and manager, the switch from being one of the
  team to being the team's manager. See chapter
  [[07-difficult-people-leadership-conversations]] for the
  difficult-conversation shape the individual is learning.
- **Do not double-book.** The individual is either an
  individual contributor or a manager; the head does not ask
  the individual to run their prior craft workload while also
  running a team. That "player-coach" configuration burns out
  the individual and produces a team that is under-managed by
  a manager who does not have time for management.

### The out-of-function move

Sometimes the head's best signal is that a team member should
move out of the AI-governance function — to internal audit, to
privacy, to product, to legal, to somewhere else in the
enterprise that is a better long-term fit for their trajectory.
The head coaches the move explicitly:

- **Name the move honestly.** The head does not hint at the
  move or manoeuvre the individual toward it; the head has the
  conversation directly.
- **Support the move actively.** The head talks to the
  receiving function's leader, endorses the individual, and
  ensures the transition is a promotion or a lateral rather
  than a demotion.
- **Do not treat the move as a loss to the function.** An
  alumnus in a peer function is a partner in cross-enterprise
  work (chapter [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]); the
  head's willingness to support out-of-function moves builds
  the function's reputation as a place people join for a
  chapter of their career rather than for permanent capture.

## The team-level ritual set

Beyond the individual coaching cadence, the head runs a set of
team-level rituals that build the function's culture and
coordination.

- **Weekly team standing meeting.** Thirty to sixty minutes,
  standing agenda: quick roundtable on what each member is
  working on, standing agenda items (upcoming governance
  council meetings, upcoming board or committee reports,
  major regulator engagements in flight), and one deep-dive
  topic (rotated across the team) on a specific technical or
  regulatory topic. The deep-dive is often led by a team
  member other than the head, on a topic the team member is
  developing depth on — the ritual doubles as speaker practice
  and as knowledge diffusion.
- **Monthly extended-function all-hands.** Where the function
  is larger than can fit in the weekly ritual (embedded
  analysts, matrixed contributors, cross-function partners),
  a monthly all-hands widens the audience for the standing
  agenda and includes an update from the head on
  cross-enterprise adjacencies (the CRO's read, the CAE's
  audit-plan progress, the CAIO's strategy update as it
  intersects governance).
- **Quarterly team retrospective.** Ninety minutes, structured
  retrospective on the prior quarter: what worked, what did
  not, what the team wants to change. The head models the
  retrospective by naming their own things that did not work.
  Actions are documented and reviewed at the next retrospective.
- **Annual team off-site.** Where the enterprise's shape
  supports it (budget, geography, team distribution), an
  annual off-site of one or two days for the function to plan
  the coming year together. The off-site is not a strategy
  offering the head hands down; it is a working session where
  the team's own view of the coming year shapes the head's
  plan.

The rituals are not decorative. They are what makes the
function a *team* rather than a set of individuals reporting to
the same head. A function without team rituals is a function
where coordination happens through the head's own working
memory, and the head becomes the single-point-of-failure
chapter [[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]] warned
against.

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**The rescheduled 1:1 pattern.** The head reschedules 1:1s
routinely because "something more important came up". The team
reads this as the head not valuing them. The remedy is the
protected-standing-hold discipline; the head reschedules
rarely, apologises when they do, and reschedules within the
week.

**The status-meeting 1:1.** The head uses the 1:1 to get status
updates from the direct report rather than to develop the
direct report. The direct report begins dreading the 1:1 and
brings only the safe items. The remedy is the direct-report-
owned agenda and the standing development-conversation cadence.

**The stale development plan.** The plan was authored at hire,
signed by both parties, filed, and never refreshed. Six months
in, the plan bears no relation to what the individual is
actually developing on. The remedy is the quarterly review
cadence and the rule that the plan updates on completion of
material development actions.

**The unhosted rotation.** The head schedules a shadowing or
embed rotation without a named host, a defined objective, or a
debrief plan. The individual spends six weeks unclear what
they are supposed to be learning; the host is unclear what they
are supposed to be teaching. The remedy is the rotation-shape
discipline above — named host, named objective, named debrief.

**The 90-day cliff.** The onboarding rotation ends at day 90
and the hire is dropped into full ambient workload without a
transition. The hire's first-year productivity plateaus and
they begin doubting the function invested in them. The remedy
is the continued coaching cadence beyond day 90 — the
development plan takes over from the onboarding rotation as
the running document.

**The head who does not have their own coach.** The head runs
1:1s with the team but has no analogous relationship for
themselves — no coach, no mentor, no peer group. The team
notices; the head runs out of feedback on their own craft; the
head's craft plateaus while they demand development from
others. The remedy is the head investing in their own coaching
relationship (an external executive coach, a peer group in the
[[mod-112-certifications-career-path-community-leadership]]
community, or a mentor from the head's own network).

**The under-coached manager transition.** A new manager in the
function is promoted without the specific management-coaching
investment above. The new manager runs their team on default
instincts, some of which are wrong. The team suffers; the head
loses the trust of the team and eventually of the new manager.
The remedy is the explicit management-coaching investment as
part of the promotion.

## Summary

- The head runs a *development portfolio* — 1:1 cadences,
  written career-development plans, skip-level cadences, a
  rotation program (architect mentorship, evaluator
  shadowing, product-team embeds), and a team-level ritual
  set (weekly meeting, monthly all-hands, quarterly
  retrospective, annual off-site).
- The 1:1 is the head's central management ritual — recurring,
  protected, direct-report-owned, note-anchored, and
  commitment-closing. Weekly for most reports; explicit
  cadence per report; not rescheduled routinely.
- The career-development plan per direct report is jointly
  authored, refreshed quarterly, honest on time-to-readiness,
  and shaped by the individual's own direction. It is not the
  same as the performance-review form.
- The rotation program funds three specific rotations —
  architect mentorship for the architecture track, evaluation-
  engineer shadowing for the evaluator track, product-team
  embeds for context — each with named hosts, defined
  objectives, and structured debriefs.
- The 90-day onboarding rotation is authored *together* with
  the interview loop and templated per role; the head runs
  the 30-, 60-, and 90-day check-ins personally.
- Three career-transition patterns the head coaches through
  explicitly: individual-contributor to architect, technical to
  management, and out-of-function moves that the head supports
  rather than resists.
- Team-level rituals build the function into a team rather
  than a set of individuals; the head does not skip them
  because "we're busy this quarter".
- Seven anti-patterns the head refuses: rescheduled 1:1s,
  status-meeting 1:1s, stale development plans, unhosted
  rotations, the 90-day cliff, the head-without-a-coach, and
  the under-coached manager transition.
- Chapter [[04-second-line-independence-balance]] runs the
  discipline that shapes what "productive" looks like for
  team members embedded near product; chapter
  [[05-cae-and-third-line-coordination]] is where the head's
  own coaching relationship with the CAE lives at cadence;
  chapter [[07-difficult-people-leadership-conversations]]
  runs the difficult-conversation craft the head coaches new
  managers through.
