# The Difficult People-Leadership Conversation

## Motivation

The head can run a well-shaped function
([[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]]), fill it with a
disciplined hiring plan ([[02-multi-year-hiring-plan]]), coach the
team with the cadence in [[03-coaching-and-career-pathing]], hold
the second-line balance in [[04-second-line-independence-balance]],
coordinate with the CAE in [[05-cae-and-third-line-coordination]],
and steward the CxO-scope vendor relationships in
[[06-cxo-vendor-relationships]] — and still, over any real tenure,
land in a small number of conversations that no operating cadence
resolves. A reorganisation the team will experience as personal.
A matrixed peer whose non-cooperation has become material and
whose behaviour has to be escalated. A senior team member whose
performance or conduct has crossed the line where termination is
the right call. A hire whose challenge to product was substantively
correct and organisationally costly, and whose position the head
now has to defend to a CxO peer who is asking the head to move the
hire off the account.

These are the difficult conversations. They are not the ordinary
1:1s in [[03-coaching-and-career-pathing]] and they are not the
stop-ship discipline in [[04-second-line-independence-balance]].
They arrive rarely, they arrive with adversarial context, they
are irreversible in effect, and they are the moments in which the
head's leadership is most visible to the team, the CxO peer layer,
and (in retrospect, in incident reviews and audit-committee
retrospectives) to the audit committee.

The failure mode this chapter opens with is not the difficult
conversation the head has poorly. It is the difficult conversation
the head *does not have* — the reorganisation deferred a quarter
too long, the non-cooperating peer never escalated, the senior team
member on a slow-burn performance failure whose termination the
head postpones through two cycles of "one more chance", the hire
under CxO pressure whose defence the head phones in rather than
carries. The unhad difficult conversation is the failure the audit
committee eventually names and the team eventually leaves over.

The four conversations this chapter runs are:

1. **Reorganising the second line.** The head has decided the
   function's shape has to change — a Shape B moving to Shape C, a
   consolidation of federated domains, a splitting of a level-50
   architect's remit, or the elimination of an embedded analyst
   role that is no longer warranted. The conversation with the
   team, individual by individual, is what the head runs before
   the new chart is published.
2. **Escalating a matrixed peer for governance non-cooperation.**
   A product VP, a CTO, or an engineering director has repeatedly
   declined to cooperate with the second line's discipline —
   intake responses late, evidence production incomplete, stop-
   ship calls contested outside the appeal path, embed access
   revoked. The head has exhausted the working-level channels and
   the escalation to the CxO peer layer or the CRO is now the
   next move.
3. **Terminating a senior team member.** A level-35 evaluator, a
   level-50 architect, or a similarly-senior team member has
   crossed the line where continued employment is no longer
   defensible — sustained performance failure through a
   documented improvement plan, a conduct issue that reaches HR,
   or a values or independence failure that cannot be walked
   back. The termination conversation, the transition, and the
   team-facing message are what the head runs.
4. **Defending a hire who challenged product too aggressively.**
   A risk engineer or evaluator has raised a substantive concern
   about a product release, has pressed the concern hard, and the
   product VP is now asking the head to move the hire off the
   account or off the function. The concern was substantively
   correct. The head has to defend the hire without conceding
   the discipline and without burning the CxO relationship.

Each conversation has its own discipline. What they share is that
the head prepares, holds the conversation directly, minutes what
was said and agreed, and follows up to close the loop. Nobody
else runs these conversations for the head. HR advises; the CRO
underwrites; the GC counsels on legal exposure. The head speaks.

## Core concept — the difficult-conversation discipline

A difficult conversation the head is about to hold has five
common elements the head runs before, during, and after the room.

**Preparation.** The head writes down what the conversation is
about, what the desired outcome is, what the fallback is if the
desired outcome is not reachable, what the other party's likely
response is, and what the head will and will not do in reply.
The preparation is on paper — not in the head's memory — because
adversarial conversations degrade recall and because the paper is
the record the head can hand to HR, the CRO, or (if it ever comes
to that) the GC. The preparation is not a script; it is a map of
the terrain.

**HR, CRO, and GC pre-alignment.** The head does not surprise the
enabling adjacencies. HR is briefed before a termination, a
reorganisation, or a matrix escalation involving a peer's direct
report. The CRO is briefed before any escalation to the CxO peer
layer that touches the function's operating authority. The GC is
briefed before any conversation where legal exposure is credible
— termination for cause, defence of a hire against a formal
complaint from a peer, or reorganisation touching protected
categories. The pre-alignment does not neutralise the head's
authority; it ensures that the head's authority is not undermined
by an adjacency the head did not consult.

**The conversation itself.** In person where feasible (video where
distance forces it, telephone rarely and only for time-critical
matters, never in writing for the initial delivery). The head
speaks directly and substantively. The head does not open with
small talk that primes the other party for good news. The head
does not soften the substance to the point of ambiguity — a
termination misheard as a performance improvement plan is a
termination the head has to repeat and that everyone experiences
worse the second time. The head listens; the head does not
interrupt; the head answers questions honestly; the head does not
volunteer information beyond what the conversation requires.

**Minuting.** Immediately after the conversation, the head writes
what was said and agreed. Two versions: the head's own working
minute (for the head's records), and the summary the other party
receives (for their records). The minute is factual and short. It
is not the head's interpretation of the other party's reaction; it
is what the head said, what was asked, and what was agreed.

**Follow-through.** The head does what the conversation committed
to. If the head committed to a follow-up conversation in a week,
it happens in a week. If the head committed to speaking to a
particular peer or advocating for a particular outcome, it
happens on the timeline the head committed to. Follow-through is
what makes the next difficult conversation possible; unclosed
loops accumulate and eventually the team, the peer, or the
successor reads the head as unreliable in the difficult moment.

The five elements are the same regardless of which of the four
conversations the head is running. What varies is the specific
substance and the specific enabling adjacencies. The rest of the
chapter is discipline per conversation.

## Reorganising the second line

The function reorganises when the shape decision from chapter
[[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]] needs to change.
Common triggers: an AI-portfolio shift makes Shape B's embeds
misaligned with where the risk now sits; a federated Shape C has
produced coordination costs that outweigh the domain-depth
benefit; a level-50 architect's remit has grown beyond one
person's capacity and needs to be split; the introduction of a
new sector overlay warrants a dedicated lead; a persistent
succession gap has forced the head to consolidate roles to make
the bench viable.

The head does not reorganise reactively (once a quarter because
the intake queue is stressed) and does not reorganise cosmetically
(because a new organisational fashion has arrived). The head
reorganises when the current shape is materially costing the
function's operating capacity and the new shape is materially
better. The reorganisation goes through the same charter
ratification as any material second-line design change — the CRO
ratifies, the CAE is copied for third-line-audit continuity, the
audit committee is notified for material re-shapings.

The people-leadership conversation is what the head runs before
the new chart is published — with each affected individual,
individually, in a specific order.

**The order matters.** The head speaks to the individuals whose
role is materially changing (a scope expansion, a scope
contraction, a reporting-line move, a level change) before the
individuals whose role is unchanged. Within materially-changing
individuals, the head speaks to those experiencing a *loss* (a
narrower remit, a lower authority, a departure from the team)
before those experiencing a *gain* (a broader remit, a promotion,
an expanded authority). The head speaks to those in a matrix
relationship with a first-line peer *before* the matrix partner
so the affected individual is not blindsided by their partner.
The order compresses into a few days — a reorganisation that
takes three weeks to communicate is a reorganisation the team is
already discussing in corridors before the head has spoken.

**The individual conversation.** Substantively: the head opens
with the change to this individual's role, states the reason for
the change at the shape-level (not "the CRO asked for it" and not
"we needed to shake things up"; "the AI portfolio has moved and
the function has to move with it, and this specific change is
what the shape now requires"), acknowledges the impact honestly
(a scope loss is a loss and the head names it), states what the
head is committing to on transition (specific timeline, specific
handoff plan, specific development support if the individual is
moving to a new remit that requires new craft), and invites the
individual to bring back questions or concerns within a specific
window.

**The team-wide moment.** After the individual conversations, the
head runs the team-wide moment — usually a special all-hands or
a team meeting called for the purpose — where the new chart is
walked through, the shape rationale is presented, and the head
takes questions. The head does not surface individual role
changes as news at this moment; every individual affected has
already been told. The team-wide moment is about the shape
rationale, not about the individual moves.

**The follow-up cadence.** For the first quarter after the
reorganisation, the head runs additional 1:1 depth with anyone
whose role materially changed. Individuals absorbing a scope
loss need explicit reassurance about their standing in the
function; individuals absorbing a scope gain need coaching depth
they may not previously have had. The 1:1 cadence in chapter
[[03-coaching-and-career-pathing]] is the substrate; the
reorganisation adds a layer of specific check-ins on the
individual's landing in the new shape.

**Failure modes.** The reorganisation announced first at the
team-wide meeting is the reorganisation individuals learn about
alongside their colleagues, which is a leadership failure the
team remembers for years. The reorganisation whose rationale is
"the CRO wants it" is the reorganisation the head has abdicated
authorship of. The reorganisation without a specific transition
plan per affected individual is the reorganisation that produces
uncoached individuals in new roles and predictable underperformance.

## Escalating a matrixed peer for governance non-cooperation

The matrixed relationship the head has with a product VP, a
CTO's direct report, an engineering director, a CIO's function
lead, or a CDO's team lead runs on the matrix-charter discipline
from chapter [[01-function-org-shape-and-reporting-lines]]. Most
of the time the charter and the working-level relationship
resolve issues without escalation. Sometimes they do not, and
the head has to escalate.

The escalation trigger is not one difficult exchange. It is a
pattern. Concretely:

- Intake responses from a specific product line are chronically
  late by more than a specified margin (typically two weeks past
  the standard turnaround) across three or more consecutive
  systems.
- Evidence production for the second line's control-testing is
  incomplete or unresponsive for three or more consecutive test
  cycles.
- Stop-ship calls are contested outside the appeal path — the
  first-line VP takes the disagreement directly to the CAIO or
  the CEO rather than through the appeal process the governance
  council operates.
- Embed access for the level-15 analyst or level-25 engineer has
  been revoked or degraded without CRO-agreed rationale.
- The specific first-line partner has adopted a public posture
  against the function — in engineering all-hands, in written
  communications, in cross-functional forums — that goes beyond
  substantive challenge into personal or positional attack on
  the head or the team.

The head assembles the pattern before escalating. The KRI/KPI
portfolio in [[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter
01 surfaces some of it (intake latency by product line, evidence-
completeness by product line, appeal-rate by first-line partner);
the head augments with specific instance evidence (dated emails,
minuted meetings, incident-response records). The head does not
escalate on impression; the head escalates on documented pattern.

**The pre-escalation conversation.** Before the head takes the
matter to the CRO or the peer's CxO manager, the head has one
final direct conversation with the peer. The head is explicit
about the pattern, the specific evidence, the specific remedies
sought, the timeline for those remedies, and the fact that
absent movement, the head will escalate. This conversation is
not a threat; it is a discipline. The peer who has not been told
the head is at the escalation threshold has not been given the
opportunity to change course. The peer who has been told and
does not change course has authored their own escalation.

**The escalation itself.** The head takes the matter to the CRO
first — the CRO is the head's own manager and the enabling
adjacency for the escalation. The CRO's counterpart CxO peer
(the CAIO for a product-source issue, the CTO for an engineering-
source issue, the CIO for a systems-access issue) is where the
escalation lands on the other side. The head is present in the
CRO-to-CxO conversation, either directly or by proxy through
the CRO carrying the material. The head's material at the
escalation is factual: the pattern, the evidence, the pre-
escalation conversation, the remedies sought.

**What the head does not do.** The head does not run the
escalation through the CAIO or the CTO if the CRO has not been
briefed first. Bypassing the CRO signals the head does not trust
their own reporting adjacency, which is a governance failure
apparent to the CxO peer layer. The head does not attack the
peer personally; the escalation is about behaviour and pattern,
not character. The head does not seek a specific personnel
outcome (the peer's removal, reassignment, or discipline); the
escalation asks for cooperation and offers the remedies the head
would accept, and leaves personnel decisions to the peer's own
management.

**The failure mode.** The most common failure is the escalation
that never happens. The head hears about the pattern from the
team, has repeated frustrated conversations with the peer at
working level, and never crosses the escalation threshold
because the escalation feels like an aggressive move and the
head values collegiality. Meanwhile the second line's ability to
run its discipline on that product line degrades. The team
notices; the CAE notices in the third-line audit; the audit
committee eventually asks the head why the function has not been
running its authority. The remedy is the escalation-threshold
discipline — the head writes down what the pattern-trigger looks
like and commits to escalating when the pattern crosses the
threshold. Escalation-as-discipline is not aggression; it is the
mechanism by which the second line's authority becomes real.

## Terminating a senior team member

Termination is the head's own conversation. HR is in the room
often — the standard practice in most enterprises is that HR is
present at any termination — but the head speaks. The head does
not delegate the delivery to HR because HR is the one whose
process runs the paperwork; the head delegates only the
paperwork, not the message.

Termination happens in three broad categories.

**Sustained performance failure.** The individual is not meeting
the expectations of the role, has been through a documented
performance improvement plan (PIP) or its enterprise equivalent
with clear success criteria, and has not met the criteria. The
head has been in the difficult conversation for months — the
initial "here is what I need to see from you" conversation, the
mid-PIP check-in, the final "the criteria have not been met and
here is what happens next" conversation. The termination is the
end of that arc, not the beginning of a surprise.

**Conduct issue.** The individual has done something that
enterprise conduct standards do not permit — a harassment
complaint substantiated by HR investigation, a security-
protocol violation of enterprise material, a values-based
failure (dishonesty on evidence submissions to the CAE, a
conflict-of-interest failure with a vendor). The head is the
accountable executive for the function and speaks the
termination even where HR has driven the investigation, because
the message the team receives has to be the head's own.

**Independence failure.** Rarer but the most difficult of the
three: a senior team member — often a level-35 evaluator or
level-50 architect — has repeatedly failed to hold the second-
line's independence position, has consistently softened findings
under first-line pressure, or has been complicit in a working-
level accommodation that materially compromised the function's
authority. Where PIP-and-develop does not work (usually because
the pattern is a values fit issue rather than a skills issue),
termination is the right call. This is the most difficult
category because the individual is often technically strong and
personally well-liked, and the failure is a professional
judgment issue that the individual disputes. The head speaks
the termination and defends the reasoning to the CRO and
(rarely, in genuinely material cases) to the audit committee.

**The conversation.** Preparation is the discipline. HR is
briefed and typically in the room; the GC is briefed if legal
exposure is credible; the CRO is notified before the
conversation happens (the CRO is the head's manager and does not
learn about a senior-role termination from the team). The head
opens with the substance: "I have made the decision to end your
employment with the enterprise, effective [date]. I will walk
you through the reason and the transition, and then I will
answer any questions you have." The head does not soften into
"we need to have a difficult conversation" (which invites
speculation and delays the substance). The head does not lead
with the transition logistics; the substance comes first, then
the logistics.

The head states the reason honestly and briefly. In a
performance case: "The performance improvement plan we agreed on
in March had specific criteria; the mid-plan check-in was in
June; the criteria were not met at the July close. That is the
reason." In a conduct case: "The investigation HR completed on
[specifics as they are permitted to state] concluded [as HR has
determined], and continued employment is not tenable." In an
independence case: "In the three specific incidents I have
minuted with you over the last six months, the pattern of
findings-softening under product pressure is what the function
cannot carry. I have concluded that the fit is not repairable."

The head then walks the transition logistics — the last date,
the specific handoffs the individual is being asked to run
(often none, or bounded), the specific separation terms HR has
approved, and the specific reference and re-employment posture
the enterprise will hold. The head answers the individual's
questions. The head does not negotiate the decision (the
decision is made and the conversation is not the venue to
reopen it); the head does negotiate the transition logistics
within HR-approved bounds where the individual raises specific
concerns.

**The team-facing message.** The head runs the team-facing
message the same day, in most cases. The message is short and
substantive: the individual is no longer with the enterprise as
of [date], the head is grateful for their contributions (where
this is true; where a conduct issue makes this awkward, the
head is factual rather than warm), the transition plan for the
individual's work is [specifics], and the head will take
questions in the next team meeting or in 1:1s. The head does
not disclose the reason for the departure; the individual is
entitled to their privacy and the team does not need the reason
to work through the transition.

**The follow-up.** In the following weeks, the head expects
team members to ask indirect questions — about their own
standing, about whether other departures are coming, about
whether the head is running the function in a way that puts
them at risk. The head runs those conversations in 1:1s and
answers substantively without disclosing information that
belongs to the departed individual. The follow-up is the arc
that closes the departure without leaving the team in prolonged
uncertainty.

**Failure modes.** The termination held off for one more cycle
because the head "wanted to give them a chance" — where the
chance was already given in the PIP — is a termination the
team has been waiting for and the head's delay costs the team's
trust in the head's judgment. The termination whose reason the
head is not confident in is a termination that will come back
in an employment claim; the head does not terminate on a
reason the head cannot defend to the GC. The termination
delivered in writing (an email, a Slack message, a letter left
on a desk) is a termination the individual will never forgive
and the team will notice; the head speaks it in person.

## Defending a hire who challenged product too aggressively

The scenario: a risk engineer or evaluator raised a substantive
concern about a product release. The concern was correct — the
release had a genuine risk exposure the second line was right
to surface. The way the concern was raised was, arguably,
aggressive — the engineer pressed hard in the working
sessions, wrote a sharp memo, took the issue up the chain
faster than the ordinary appeal path would have. The product
VP is now in the head's office asking the head to move the
engineer off the account or off the function. The product VP
has framed the request as "the engineer is a difficult
personality who is not effective in cross-functional work" and
has attached a specific incident where the engineer's tone
"crossed a line".

The head has to hold two positions simultaneously. First: the
substantive concern was correct. Backing off from the concern
would be a capitulation the second-line function does not
recover from — the message the team receives is that hard
challenge on a substantively-correct concern gets you moved off
the account. Second: the delivery may or may not warrant
coaching. Where the delivery genuinely was outside professional
norms (personal attacks on the product team, disparagement in
public forums, communications materially outside enterprise
communication standards), the coaching is honest; where the
delivery was hard-but-professional and the product VP is
mislabelling substantive challenge as "difficult personality",
the coaching is not warranted and the head does not accept
the framing.

The discipline separates the two.

**The head's own investigation.** Before responding to the
product VP, the head investigates. What was the substantive
concern; is the second-line's read on its correctness robust
(the level-50 architect and the head walk the technical
substance); what specifically did the engineer say and write;
where did the delivery cross professional norms and where did
it not; is there a pattern with this engineer or is this a
one-off; what is the head's independent read on the delivery
against enterprise communication standards. The head does not
respond in the conversation with the product VP; the head
takes the request seriously, commits to investigating, and
schedules a follow-up.

**The conversation with the engineer.** The head runs the
conversation with the engineer directly, before the head
responds to the product VP. The head opens with the
substantive concern — "the technical substance of the concern
you raised on [system] was correct, and the function stands
behind the substance". The head then discusses the delivery
honestly — where it was professionally within norms (the head
names those specifics) and where it may have crossed lines
(the head names those specifics too). The head asks the
engineer for their own read on the delivery. The head does not
concede the substance and does not pre-commit to any coaching
outcome; the head does gather the engineer's account, which
the head will need before responding to the product VP.

**The response to the product VP.** The head returns to the
product VP with a specific response. The substance of the
concern was correct, and the function is not moving the
engineer off the account for having raised a correct concern
— that is not a defensible personnel action and the head will
not take it. On the delivery, the head has coached the
engineer where coaching was warranted (specific instances the
head names) and where the delivery was hard-but-professional,
the function's position is that hard-but-professional
challenge is what the second line is here to provide. The head
does not agree to move the engineer; the head does agree to
coach specifically on the delivery instances where coaching is
warranted; the head invites the product VP to a joint follow-
up in the next quarter to review whether the working
relationship has recovered.

**The CRO and CAIO conversation.** Where the product VP
escalates further — to the CAIO, to the CRO, to the CEO — the
head is prepared. The head has minuted the substantive concern,
the investigation, the conversation with the engineer, and the
response to the product VP. The head takes the material to the
CRO first (as with any escalation involving the CxO peer layer);
the CRO carries the head's position into the CxO layer as
warranted. The head is not defensive; the head is factual.

**Coaching the engineer forward.** The engineer receives the
head's protection publicly and honest coaching privately. The
public message — the fact that the engineer stays on the
account, that the concern was substantively vindicated — is
what the rest of the team reads. The private coaching — where
the delivery could have landed better, what the head would
have done differently, how the engineer's professional
development includes the softer skills of cross-functional
communication — is what develops the engineer. The two are
separate and both are needed.

**Failure modes.** The head who moves the engineer off the
account because "it was easier" is the head who has taught the
team that hard challenge is career-limiting. Every subsequent
substantive challenge from the second line is now softer, and
the function's authority degrades quietly. The head who
defends the engineer's delivery even where the delivery was
genuinely outside professional norms is the head who confuses
protecting the function with protecting an individual whose
craft on delivery needs coaching. The head has to separate
those two dimensions and hold both accurately.

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**The unhad conversation.** The head sees the pattern that
warrants the difficult conversation — the pattern that warrants
reorganisation, escalation, termination, or defence — and
defers. The reorganisation is deferred a quarter. The escalation
is deferred another cycle of working-level engagement. The
termination is deferred through one more PIP. The defence is
deferred until the engineer has already been moved. The unhad
conversation is the failure the audit committee eventually
notices in the retrospective, the team notices in the
regretted-loss numbers, and the successor notices when they
inherit the accumulated debt. The remedy is the specific
threshold discipline per conversation — the head writes down
what the pattern that triggers each conversation looks like,
and commits to acting when the pattern crosses the threshold.

**The delegated delivery.** The head allows HR, the CRO, or a
level-50 architect to deliver a message that is the head's own —
the reorganisation message, the termination, the escalation of
a peer, the defence of a hire. The person delegated to does
their best but the message lands as second-hand; the receiving
individual reads the delegation as the head unwilling to be
present for the difficult moment. The team notices. The remedy
is the head personally in the room for each of the four
conversations — HR advises, HR is present at terminations, the
CRO enables escalations, the GC counsels — but the head speaks.

**The written substitute.** The head sends the difficult
message in email, in a written memo, or in a formal document
before speaking to the individual. The individual receives the
message from the head as text, not from the head as a person,
and the interaction quality is lost. The remedy is the
in-person (or video, where distance demands it) delivery as
the default; written material follows the conversation as the
minute or the formal record, not as the delivery vehicle.

**The reason the head cannot defend.** The termination is
delivered with a reason the head does not fully believe, the
escalation is opened on a pattern the head has not fully
documented, the defence is offered on a substantive concern
the head has not fully validated. The other party challenges,
and the head cannot hold the position under challenge; the
head backs off or fudges. The remedy is the preparation
discipline — the head does not enter a difficult conversation
until the head is confident in the substance and can defend it
to HR, the CRO, the GC, or the CxO peer.

**The team-facing silence.** The head runs a difficult
conversation — a termination, a reorganisation, an escalation
— and does not run the team-facing follow-up. The team is left
to piece together what happened from corridors, from the
departing individual's own account (if any), from the product-
line VP's account (if the peer is talking). The narrative the
team constructs is worse than the narrative the head could
have shaped by speaking. The remedy is the team-facing
message discipline — short, substantive, respectful of the
individual's privacy, honest about what has changed, running
on the same day or the next.

**The isolated head.** The head runs the difficult
conversations without any peer support — no coach, no mentor,
no peer-heads-of-role network to debrief with, no CRO
relationship deep enough to be honest in. Difficult
conversations are lonelier than the head expected; without
support the head begins to defer them (see the unhad
conversation above), or holds them badly (see the reason the
head cannot defend above). The remedy is the head's own
coaching investment ([[03-coaching-and-career-pathing]]),
including specific peer-relationship investments the head
makes at community scope
([[mod-112-certifications-career-path-community-leadership]]).

## Summary

- The head runs four archetypal difficult people-leadership
  conversations: reorganising the second line, escalating a
  matrixed peer for governance non-cooperation, terminating a
  senior team member, and defending a hire who challenged
  product too aggressively.
- Every difficult conversation runs the same five elements:
  written preparation, HR / CRO / GC pre-alignment as
  applicable, in-person delivery, immediate minuting, and
  disciplined follow-through.
- Reorganising the second line: individual conversations
  before the team-wide announcement, materially-changing
  individuals first, losses before gains, matrix-partner
  affected individuals before their matrix partners, tight
  compression, follow-up coaching depth in the first quarter.
- Escalating a matrixed peer: escalate on documented pattern
  not impression, hold the pre-escalation direct conversation
  first, route through the CRO, ask for cooperation and
  offer specific remedies, do not seek personnel outcomes.
- Terminating a senior team member: three categories
  (performance, conduct, independence), preparation with HR /
  CRO / GC as applicable, substance first in the room,
  transition logistics second, team-facing message the same
  day, respect the departed individual's privacy in team
  follow-up.
- Defending a hire against a CxO ask to move them: separate
  the substance of the concern from the delivery of it,
  validate the substance with the level-50 architect, coach
  the engineer where delivery warranted it, respond to the
  CxO peer that the substance is vindicated and the engineer
  stays on the account, do not confuse defending the function
  with defending an individual's delivery beyond what the
  delivery warrants.
- Six anti-patterns the head refuses: the unhad conversation,
  the delegated delivery, the written substitute, the reason
  the head cannot defend, the team-facing silence, and the
  isolated head.
- The four conversations are rare, adversarial, irreversible,
  and the moments in which the head's leadership is most
  visible. The discipline is what allows the head to hold
  them well when they arrive, and the follow-through is what
  makes the next one possible.
