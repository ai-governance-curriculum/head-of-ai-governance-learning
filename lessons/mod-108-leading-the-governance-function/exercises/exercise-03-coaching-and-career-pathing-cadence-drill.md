# exercise-03: Coaching and Career-Pathing Cadence Drill

**Estimated effort:** 3 hours

## Objective

Author the enterprise's **AI-governance function development
portfolio** — the 1:1 cadence per direct report, the career-
development plan per direct report, the skip-level cadence, the
three-rotation program (architect mentorship, evaluation-
engineer shadowing, product-team embed), the per-role 90-day
onboarding rotation, and the team-level ritual set. The
deliverable is the running discipline the head operates against
week-to-week, the operating substrate under the succession
picture in exercise 01, and the follow-through on the hiring
plan in exercise 02.

The portfolio has to survive three stress tests. First, a new
level-15 analyst joins in month one and immediately asks "what
is my path from here" — the onboarding rotation and the
career-development plan template have to answer without the
head inventing the answer in the moment. Second, a level-35
evaluator is entering the eighteen-month window where they
should either be progressing toward level-50 architecture or
committing to individual-contributor evaluation-engineer depth
— the rotation program and the development plan structure have
to make the progression conversation legible. Third, the head
takes a two-week absence and the team-level rituals continue
without collapse — the ritual set has to be genuinely owned by
the team, not by the head's presence.

The portfolio is not the head's private discipline. It is
published to the team, refreshed on cadence, and the head is
on the hook for delivering it.

## Steps

1. **Inherit the enterprise context.** Confirm the exercise-01
   context one-pager is current and reference it.

2. **The 1:1 cadence template.** Author the standing 1:1 shape
   the head runs with each direct report. Include:
   - The default cadence per level (weekly for level-15 through
     level-35; fortnightly for level-50 where weekly is over-
     touch; explicit per report, not a default).
   - The standing-hold discipline (same day, same time, in
     the head's calendar, rarely rescheduled).
   - The direct-report-owned agenda structure.
   - The head's standing items (development conversation,
     escalations from the past week, coordination the head
     owes).
   - The development-conversation rhythm (every fourth 1:1 or
     equivalent — a specific, non-ambient cadence).
   - The note-taking discipline the head runs.
   - The end-with-commitments protocol.
   The template is what the head uses to onboard new team
   members to the 1:1 shape and what the head reviews at the
   annual retrospective.

3. **The career-development plan template.** Author the
   template every career-development plan uses. Required
   fields, from chapter 03:
   - Current level and role.
   - Next-level competence gate (referenced against the level-
     ladder from exercise 01).
   - The individual's own career direction (as stated by the
     individual).
   - Named development actions in flight with target dates and
     expected outcomes.
   - Named coaching relationships (mentors, shadowing hosts,
     embed hosts).
   - Time-to-readiness estimate (honest read, in months or
     "unclear given current trajectory" — do not paper).
   - Refresh cadence and last-refresh date.
   - Joint authorship signature (head and direct report).
   The template is the shared contract; every plan on the
   function uses the same fields so the head's review across
   the team is consistent.

4. **Three example completed plans.** Author three example
   career-development plans, one per level, using the template:
   - A level-15 analyst on a trajectory toward level-25 risk
     engineer with a specific craft (fairness or red-team).
   - A level-25 engineer on a trajectory toward level-35
     evaluation engineer with a specific methodology focus.
   - A level-35 evaluator whose stated direction is
     individual-contributor depth rather than level-50
     architecture (the honest-read-not-a-progression case;
     the head respects the direction and shapes the plan
     against it).
   The examples make the template concrete and expose
   template gaps.

5. **The skip-level cadence.** Author the skip-level protocol.
   Include:
   - The head's skip-level cadence per indirect report level
     (typically quarterly with each level-15 analyst; monthly
     with each level-25 engineer if the direct manager is a
     level-35 evaluator; occasional with level-25 engineers
     managed by the head directly under a small function).
   - The framing the head uses so the skip-level is not read
     as surveillance (a signal on team health, not a check on
     the direct manager).
   - The specific things the head asks about (blockers the
     indirect report may be facing, development interests the
     direct manager may not have surfaced, culture signals
     from the team-level rituals).
   - How the head handles what surfaces (bringing it back to
     the direct manager in the manager's own 1:1, without
     turning the skip-level into a triangulation).

6. **The rotation program.** For each of the three rotations,
   author the specific programme structure:
   - **Architect mentorship.** The mentor(s), the cadence
     (one hour every two-to-four weeks), the specific
     development focus (architectural range across domains,
     design-authorship craft, cross-framework composition),
     the exercise pattern (mentee produces work between
     sessions; mentor critiques against specific criteria),
     the mentor confidentiality protocol.
   - **Evaluation-engineer shadowing.** The rotation length
     (four to eight weeks part-time or one to two weeks full-
     time), the specific evaluation cycle the shadower
     participates in (release-assurance sign-off, evaluation-
     harness authoring, regulator-facing evidence assembly),
     the debrief cadence, the plan integration.
   - **Product-team embed rotation.** The distinction from
     Shape B embeds (this is time-boxed, six to twelve
     weeks); the reporting-line discipline during the
     rotation (solid-line stays with the head; product is
     host, not manager); the pre-rotation independence
     briefing (referenced to chapter 04); the debrief-on-
     return with the head and the level-50 architect.
   Include for each rotation: the named-host discipline, the
   defined-objective discipline, and the debrief pattern.
   Unhosted rotations are chapter 03's anti-pattern; the
   exercise deliberately forces the structure.

7. **The 90-day onboarding rotation per role.** Author the
   90-day onboarding rotation for each of the role archetypes:
   level-15 analyst, level-25 risk engineer, level-35
   evaluation engineer, level-50 senior architect. For each:
   - Days 1–7: onboarding-week specifics (enterprise-standard
     onboarding, function-specific onboarding, first 1:1 with
     the head, read-in on the AIMS scope, KRI portfolio, and
     current major workstreams).
   - Days 8–30: shadowing and low-stakes contribution — the
     specific peers the hire shadows and the specific low-
     stakes items they contribute.
   - Days 31–60: named first-project ownership — the specific
     piece of work the hire owns (right-sized to complete
     well and important enough to matter).
   - Days 61–90: broader integration — the standing rotations
     they enter, the cross-function partner meetings they
     attend, the ongoing cadence at full participation.
   - Day 30, 60, and 90 check-ins with the head — structured
     conversation shape and the day-90 signal on trajectory.
   The rotation is authored per role; the level-15 analyst
   rotation is not the same as the level-50 architect
   rotation.

8. **The three career-transition coaching notes.** Author a
   short one-page note per transition pattern the head coaches
   through:
   - Individual-contributor to architect: the specific coaching
     the head runs (extending the design surface, cross-domain
     assignments, the enterprise-scope authoring exercises).
   - Technical to management: the specific management-coaching
     investment (enrolment, weekly 1:1s on the transition, the
     do-not-double-book discipline).
   - Out-of-function move: the honest-naming discipline, the
     active support pattern, the alumnus-as-partner framing.

9. **The team-level ritual set.** Author the specific team-
   level rituals the head runs:
   - Weekly team standing meeting with the standing agenda
     and deep-dive rotation.
   - Monthly extended-function all-hands with the head's
     cross-enterprise adjacency update.
   - Quarterly team retrospective (ninety minutes, structured,
     head models by naming their own things that did not
     work).
   - Annual team off-site (where the enterprise's shape
     supports it).
   For each ritual, name the owner (the head, a delegate, or
   a rotating team member), the participants, the standing
   agenda, and the note-and-follow-through discipline.

10. **The head's own coaching investment.** Author the head's
    own coaching relationship (external executive coach, peer
    group at community scope, mentor from the head's own
    network) with the cadence and the specific development
    focus for the coming year. The head-without-a-coach is
    chapter 03's anti-pattern; the exercise makes the head's
    own investment explicit.

11. **The failure-mode audit.** Author a short section — one
    paragraph per failure mode — naming how the discipline
    detects and recovers from each of chapter 03's anti-
    patterns:
    - The rescheduled 1:1 pattern.
    - The status-meeting 1:1.
    - The stale development plan.
    - The unhosted rotation.
    - The 90-day cliff.
    - The head who does not have their own coach.
    - The under-coached manager transition.

## Acceptance criteria

- The 1:1 template names all five discipline properties
  (recurring / protected, direct-report-owned, development-
  conversation rhythm, notes, ends-with-commitments). A
  template missing any property fails the criterion.
- Every career-development plan (template and the three
  examples) has all six required fields populated. A plan
  with a "TBD" on time-to-readiness fails the criterion —
  chapter 03 is explicit that the honest read is what
  matters, and "TBD" is not honest, it is deferral.
- Each rotation programme names the host, the objective, and
  the debrief pattern. A rotation without any of the three
  is chapter 03's unhosted-rotation anti-pattern.
- Each 90-day onboarding rotation has the four windows
  (days 1–7, 8–30, 31–60, 61–90) and the three check-ins
  (day 30, day 60, day 90). A rotation missing a check-in
  is the 90-day-cliff anti-pattern.
- The team-level ritual set names all four rituals (weekly,
  monthly, quarterly, annual) and any exception (an annual
  off-site skipped because the enterprise's shape does not
  support it is a defensible exception; skipping the
  quarterly retrospective is not).
- The head's own coaching investment is named specifically.
  An empty section on the head's own investment fails the
  criterion.
- The failure-mode audit names, for each of the seven failure
  modes, at least one specific detection signal and one
  specific recovery action.
- Any factual claim about a specific external coach network,
  a specific enterprise leadership-development curriculum,
  or a specific peer-group community carries a
  `<!-- needs-research: verify [specific fact] against
  [official source] -->` marker where the fact is not
  independently verifiable. Peer-group naming is often
  informal; the exercise does not require the head to name
  a peer group that does not exist.

  one-page document the head shares with the team on their
  first day and refreshes annually. The one-pager describes
  the 1:1 cadence, the development-plan cadence, the skip-
  level cadence, and the team-ritual cadence in terms the
  team can hold the head accountable to. Publishing the
  cadence is the discipline that makes the head accountable
  to their own portfolio.
- Draft the **coaching-and-development budget line** — the
  specific budget the head takes into the annual budget
  cycle for coaching investments (external coach for the
  head; management-coaching investment for new managers;
  rotation program hosting costs; conference-and-community
  investment for team members). The budget is coordinated
  with the finance business partner
  ([[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 05)
  and the vendor spend
  ([[mod-113-budget-vendor-p-and-l-and-investment-case]]).
- Author the **regretted-loss retrospective template** — the
  short review the head runs after a team member departs
  regrettably (a departure the head would have preferred to
  prevent). The review captures what the individual's plan
  looked like, what the last three 1:1s surfaced, what the
  competing offer looked like (where the individual is
  willing to share), and what the head would do differently
  in a similar case. Retrospectives feed the annual
  portfolio review.
- Draft the **manager-in-transition six-month curriculum**
  — the specific six-month management-coaching plan for a
  team member promoted into a management role. Includes
  the specific readings, the specific external programme
  enrolment, the weekly transition-focused 1:1 agenda, and
  the mid-transition check-in with the head.
- Author the **cross-function-partner briefing** — the
  specific note the head hands to a cross-function partner
  (a product VP, a CTO's direct report, a General Counsel
  practice lead) when a team member begins an embed rotation
  or a joint project. The briefing frames the second-line-
  independence discipline (chapter 04) and sets the
  expectations for the partner's role during the rotation.

## Notes and starter guidance

The most common first-cut failure of the portfolio is a plan
that reads as aspirational — "the head will run weekly 1:1s
with development conversations every fourth session, quarterly
skip-levels, and monthly all-hands" — without the head
actually holding to it. Force the honesty: the head who cannot
hold weekly 1:1s runs fortnightly and admits it; the head who
cannot run monthly all-hands runs quarterly and admits it.
Aspirational cadences that the head does not actually run are
worse than smaller cadences that the head does — the team
notices the gap and reads the head as unreliable on the
portfolio the head published.

The second common failure is the development plan that reads
as a competency-inventory checklist. A plan that names ten
competencies with a red-amber-green rating is a plan the
individual will not engage with. Force the plan into the
individual's own direction — what the individual wants to
develop, what the head sees them ready for, what the
enterprise's need is. The plan is a conversation captured on
paper, not a form-filling exercise.

The third common failure is the rotation program authored
without hosts. "The individual will shadow the evaluation
engineer for four weeks" is not a rotation; it is a hope. A
rotation without a named host, a defined objective, and a
debrief plan is chapter 03's unhosted-rotation anti-pattern,
and the rotation produces no development because no one is
responsible for producing it.

On the 90-day onboarding rotation — the temptation is to
author one rotation that "adapts" to each hire. A generic
rotation adapts to nothing. Force per-role rotations. The
level-15 analyst rotation runs against the intake queue; the
level-50 architect rotation runs against the control library
and the CxO adjacencies. The two are not the same document
with the level changed.

On the head's own coaching investment — the temptation is to
skip this or to gesture at it ("I have a peer group I check in
with occasionally"). A gesture is not an investment. Force
the specificity: who the head's coach is, what the cadence
is, what the head is working on this year. The head who takes
coaching seriously in the artefact is the head who takes it
seriously in practice; the head who cannot name a specific
coach is the head who does not have one.

The portfolio is a living discipline. Version the artefacts,
date them, refresh at the annual portfolio-review cycle, and
update whenever a material development action lands or a
rotation completes. A portfolio that has not been touched in
a year is a portfolio the team has stopped believing in.
