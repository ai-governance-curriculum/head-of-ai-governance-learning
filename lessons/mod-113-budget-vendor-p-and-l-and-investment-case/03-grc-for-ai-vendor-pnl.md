# The GRC-for-AI Vendor P&L at CxO Scope — Consolidation, Renewal, and Escalation

## Motivation

The GRC-for-AI category is emerging, fragmenting, and
consolidating at the same time. New AI-native governance
platforms are being founded and funded on a rolling basis;
enterprise GRC incumbents are extending their platforms with
AI-specific overlays; adjacent categories (model-risk
management, IT-service management, security posture management)
are pushing into the AI-governance category from three different
directions. The head who signs a five-year enterprise contract
with a single vendor without carrying a portfolio view is a head
who has bet the function's operating capability on that one
vendor's roadmap for the majority of a certification cycle. The
head who runs three overlapping tools because each was procured
in response to a separate point need is a head who is bleeding
budget on category duplication that nobody has ever taken the
consolidation decision against.

This chapter carries the *P&L* discipline underneath the
CxO-scope vendor *relationship* discipline authored in
[[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 06. The
relationship chapter covers the account cadence, the
satisfaction-pulse discipline, the continuity discipline. This
chapter covers what those disciplines produce on the CFO's side
of the ledger — the per-vendor P&L file the head maintains, the
consolidation-or-expansion decision the head takes on a three-
year cycle, the renewal negotiation the head runs at business-
owner scope alongside procurement and finance, and the SLA-slip
escalation ladder recast in commercial rather than relational
framing.

The failure mode the chapter opens with is not a head without a
GRC-for-AI vendor. The category is mature enough that most
enterprises carry at least one incumbent; a small subset carry
none and run on spreadsheets, which is its own transformation
case authored in [chapter 02](02-multi-year-investment-case.md).
The failure mode is a head who carries the vendor but does not
run the P&L underneath it — who does not know the true total
cost of ownership, who does not know the SLA attainment position
against contract, who does not know the renewal disposition
eighteen months ahead of renewal, who does not know whether the
incumbent is the right long-run bet against the category's
current shape. That head's CFO will compress the vendor line the
first time a cost cycle needs a number, and that head's audit
committee will ask why the platform is not producing the
assurance signal the platform's contracted scope implied.

The head who runs the vendor as a P&L runs it as a portfolio
position. Renewal is a commercial move. Consolidation is a
commercial move. Escalation is a commercial move. Each is taken
with the category shape, the contract shape, the SLA attainment
position, and the enterprise's operating trajectory all read
together.

## Core concept — the category map, refreshed annually

The head cannot run a portfolio position on a category the head
has not mapped. The first standing discipline of the chapter is
the category map — a one-page (or few-page) view of the
GRC-for-AI vendor landscape as the head reads it, refreshed
annually.

Vendor positioning shifts on a quarterly-to-annual basis; the "as-of" date on the map matters. -->

The head does not treat the map as static. Vendors reposition,
acquisitions happen, new entrants launch, incumbents deprecate
lines, analyst-house category definitions shift. A map that is
eighteen months old is a map the head is quietly relying on
without knowing whether it is still current. The head refreshes
the map annually as a standing calendar item, and updates it
inside a cycle when a material category event occurs (a large
acquisition, a category consolidation, a material vendor
struggle).

### Suggested groupings — starting positions, not fixed categories

The head starts with two coarse groupings and refines from
there. The groupings below are starting positions; a genuine
category map has to be verified against current public material
before citation to the CFO or the audit committee.

**Enterprise GRC platforms with AI overlays.** Vendors whose
core platform serves broader enterprise GRC, IT-service
management, or risk-management purposes, and who have extended
the platform with AI-specific modules or overlays. Examples in
public material at time of writing include ServiceNow AI Control
Tower and IBM watsonx.governance.

These fit the P&L of an enterprise that already runs the
underlying platform for wider GRC, IT-service management, or
model risk purposes; the marginal cost of adding the AI-specific
overlay against a platform whose economics are already sunk is

**AI-native governance-of-record platforms.** Vendors whose
core product is AI governance as system-of-record — the AIMS
substrate, the AI system inventory, the control library, the

These fit the P&L of an enterprise whose AI-governance function
is standing up as a distinct programme, or whose existing
enterprise GRC platform does not carry the AI-specific

### The map is honest about its limits

The head is honest about what the map is and what it is not.
The map is the head's own current best read on the category, as
of a specific date, informed by public material, peer
conversations, analyst-house reads with critical distance, and
the head's own vendor-briefing engagement. The map is not a
Gartner Magic Quadrant. It is not a definitive category
statement. It is not something the head cites without the
"as of" date and the caveats attached. The head who publishes
the map inside the enterprise labels it accordingly.

## The consolidation-or-expansion decision — the load-bearing commercial move

The load-bearing commercial decision the head takes on the
category is the consolidation-or-expansion decision. The
decision runs on roughly a three-year cycle — often coincident
with the incumbent contract's major-renewal window — and it is
the decision that shapes the vendor P&L for the next contract
term.

The decision has three coarse shapes.

### Single-vendor consolidation

The head consolidates on a single vendor as system-of-record for
the AIMS end-to-end — AI-system inventory, control library,
evidence store, workflow, and metric-production pipeline all on
one platform, with integration to adjacent systems (ISMS,
model-registry, evaluation harness, incident-management) running
through that platform's integration surface.

Trade-offs:

- **Single throat to choke.** One vendor account, one
  escalation path, one roadmap conversation. The account
  discipline in [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
  chapter 06 runs against a single relationship.
- **Deep integration.** One platform to integrate; workflow
  ergonomics can be tuned because there is one workflow layer.
- **Less category coverage.** A single vendor's depth in every
  sub-category (AIMS documentation, model-risk overlay,
  assurance workflow, regulatory-change tracking) is variable;
  the enterprise inherits the vendor's strengths and weaknesses.
- **Higher lock-in risk.** Single-vendor concentration is
  single-vendor renewal-price exposure and single-vendor
  migration cost if replacement is contemplated.

### Best-of-breed expansion

The head runs 2–3 vendors covering distinct scopes — one for
AIMS documentation and evidence store, another for model-risk
or assurance overlay, a third (where justified) for a specific
sub-category the core platforms do not cover well.
<!-- needs-research: verify whether current AI-native platforms genuinely have differentiated sub-category depth that supports best-of-breed splits, or whether the split is more legacy than substantive. Category is consolidating. -->

Trade-offs:

- **Better fit per scope.** Each vendor selected for its
  strength in the scope it covers.
- **Higher integration cost.** Two or three platforms have to
  integrate with each other and with adjacent systems; data
  flows between them are a standing engineering cost.
- **More vendor relationships to run.** Each vendor is a CxO-
  scope account; the aggregate cadence load is material.
- **Category-consolidation risk.** If the category consolidates
  mid-term — one vendor acquires another, one exits, one is
  acquired by an adjacent-category incumbent — the multi-vendor
  position may be forced into a mid-term reshape.

### Hybrid — core platform plus narrow-scope specialty

A common shape in enterprises with an existing IT-GRC incumbent.
<!-- needs-research: verify that "existing IT-GRC incumbent plus AI-native overlay" is genuinely the modal enterprise shape at the point the chapter is cited; the pattern is shifting as enterprise incumbents build out native AI modules. -->
The core platform runs the IT-GRC substrate and carries the AI
overlay for as much of the AIMS workflow as the overlay's depth
supports; a narrow-scope AI-native platform handles the AIMS
workflow depth the overlay does not carry.

Trade-offs:

- **Marginal-cost efficiency on the core.** The core platform's
  cost is amortised across broader GRC and IT-service uses; the
  AI-overlay marginal cost is relatively low.
- **Depth gap on the AI-native scope.** The specialty tool
  covers the AIMS depth the core does not.
- **Two vendor accounts to run.** Not as heavy as full
  best-of-breed; not as light as single-vendor.
- **Roadmap coordination risk.** The head aligns the two
  vendors' roadmaps at the joints — where the core's AI overlay
  is deepening, the specialty tool's scope may compress; where
  the specialty tool is expanding, the overlay may catch up.
  The head reads both roadmaps annually.

### The decision framework

The head does not take the consolidation-or-expansion decision
on preference or on the current vendor's charm. The head takes
it against a framework:

- **Current-state overlap map.** Which of the current vendors
  overlap in scope, and how much of each vendor's spend is
  covering scope already covered elsewhere? Overlap without a
  documented reason is category duplication.
- **Cost-of-consolidation model.** What does it cost to
  consolidate on a smaller vendor set — migration engineering,
  data-portability work, workflow retraining, dual-run period,
  integration rework, contractual termination costs on the
  vendors being retired?
- **Cost-of-run-parallel model.** What does it cost to keep
  running the current shape — contracted spend, integration
  cost, workflow-workaround cost (see [chapter 01](01-governance-function-budget-line.md)
  discussion of workaround labour as a P&L signal), roadmap-
  coordination cost across vendors?
- **Migration-risk model.** What is the operational risk of a
  migration executed inside the AIMS operating calendar —
  during a certification cycle, during a regulator-response
  window, during an audit-committee cycle? A migration
  scheduled against an in-flight certification is a migration
  that endangers the certification.
- **Roadmap-alignment view over the 3-year horizon.** What is
  each candidate vendor's public and briefing-material roadmap
  over the next three years, and how does it align to the
  enterprise's own AIMS trajectory (regulatory-change coverage,
  sector-specific overlays, portability and export commitments,
  integration surface)?

The framework produces a decision, not a preference. The head
brings the decision to the CFO, the CIO, and (where the
enterprise has one) the CAIO — see [chapter 08](08-caio-and-cxo-budget-envelope-coordination.md)
for the CAIO-and-CxO coordination discipline — with the
trade-offs on the table honestly.

## The per-vendor P&L file

The head maintains a per-vendor P&L file for each GRC-for-AI
vendor the function carries. The file is a working document,
refreshed quarterly. Its structure is stable across vendors so
the head can compare vendors on the same axes.

### Contracted spend

The contract's own financial shape — annual licence position
and trajectory over the term (fixed, indexed, tiered); tier
position and what would move the enterprise up or down;
growth escalators over the term (annual uplift, usage-based
expansion, seat-count growth clauses);
<!-- needs-research: verify current escalator ranges in publicly-disclosed AI-governance vendor contracts before citing an escalator norm; do not fabricate percentage figures. -->
and the professional-services envelope (hours, rate, cap or
open, and what triggers professional-services drawdown versus
support-covered work).

The head is precise about what "contracted spend" means — the
number the vendor invoices at end of period, not the
enterprise's internal budget line (which may carry allocations
from adjacent business units or centralised IT).

### Actual spend

The invoice-level view, refreshed quarterly — year-to-date
invoicing by invoice number, scope, and amount; over/under
against the annual contracted envelope (unbudgeted professional
services, seat-count overage, module additions, or contracted
commitments not drawn against); and reconciliation to the
enterprise's own general-ledger per-vendor spend line. Where
the two do not match, the head knows why, and does not sign the
file off until they do.

### Total cost of ownership

The number the CFO reads as the real cost of the vendor,
distinct from the contracted spend. Total cost of ownership
sums:

- **Contracted spend.** As above.
- **Implementation labour.** Enterprise-side FTE cost across
  the implementation year and the ongoing operating year.
  Level-50 architect, level-35 evaluator, level-15 analyst
  labour that the platform's operation consumes.
- **Integration cost.** The engineering cost of integrating the
  platform with adjacent systems (ISMS, model-registry,
  evaluation harness, ticketing, identity, data warehouse).
  This is often a materially larger number than the licence
  itself in the first two years of the contract.
- **Internal-admin cost.** The standing administrative cost of
  running the platform — user administration, integration
  maintenance, evidence-model curation, control-library
  curation.
- **Workaround labour.** The labour cost of workarounds the
  team is running because the platform does not carry the
  workflow depth the workflow needs — evidence collected
  outside the platform and imported, reports authored in a
  spreadsheet because the platform's reporting layer does not
  produce the shape the audit committee reads, integration
  work done manually because the platform's integration
  surface does not carry the specific adjacent system. See
  [chapter 01](01-governance-function-budget-line.md) — the
  workaround-labour signal is a P&L signal, not a minor
  operational annoyance.

The head's total-cost-of-ownership number is often materially
higher than the vendor's own account team's understanding of
what the account costs. The head's number is the number that
matters when the head takes the consolidation-or-expansion
decision.

### Value delivered

The per-vendor value delivered, mapped to the KRI/KPI portfolio
authored in [[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter
01 — evidence-completeness (proportion of inventory whose
evidence set is complete against the control library);
AIA / DPIA / conformity-assessment turnaround time; MTTR on
control-failure closure; control-coverage across the AI system
inventory; and audit-cycle support (evidence-production cycle
time and completeness during an ISO/IEC 42001 surveillance
audit, an internal-audit engagement, or a regulator-response
window).

The value column is where the vendor's contribution to the
function's operating capability lives. A vendor whose contracted
spend is material and whose value-column contribution is thin is
a vendor whose renewal disposition should be scrutinised. A
vendor whose value-column contribution is deep is a vendor whose
renewal disposition should reflect that.

### SLA position

The contracted service-level shape and the actual attainment —
contracted SLAs (uptime, support-response by severity,
resolution-time by severity, roadmap-delivery commitments where
the contract carries any, data-residency, security-and-privacy);
measured quarterly attainment against each, sourced from the
vendor's own SLA reporting and cross-checked against the
enterprise's own monitoring where independent monitoring exists;
and the credit position (credits the contract entitles the
enterprise to where SLAs have slipped, and whether each has been
invoked, applied, or is outstanding). A slipped SLA with an
uninvoked credit is money the head has left on the table.

### Renewal disposition

The head's advance disposition on the renewal, authored 12–18
months ahead of the renewal date (see the next section for the
timing discipline). The disposition is one of:

- **Renew as-is.** The vendor is performing to expectation, the
  category position is stable, the head is not opening the
  contract shape at the renewal. Renewal is a price
  negotiation and a term extension.
- **Renew with re-negotiation.** The vendor is broadly
  performing, but specific contract terms are opening for
  renegotiation — SLA definitions, portability commitments,
  roadmap commitments, price shape, tier position.
- **Consolidate away.** The vendor is being retired at renewal,
  its scope migrated to another vendor's platform. The
  disposition triggers the migration plan (chapter 02's
  transformation case shape) and the wind-down conversation
  with the vendor.
- **Expand to adjacent scope.** The vendor is performing well
  enough that the head is expanding scope at renewal — moving
  additional workflow onto the platform, adding modules,
  moving from a best-of-breed shape toward a consolidation
  shape with this vendor as the survivor.

The disposition is not the final decision. It is the head's
working disposition, revisited quarterly as the renewal window
approaches, and finalised only at the negotiation stage.

## Contract renewal negotiation

The head runs the renewal at CxO scope. Procurement and finance
lead the transactional negotiation — pricing, terms-and-
conditions, the specific commercial paper. The head is the
business-owner voice on the enterprise side, engaging on the
substantive shape of the deal beyond price.

### Timing — 12–18 months out, not 90 days out

The head opens the renewal file 12–18 months ahead of the
renewal expiry. Not 90 days out.

A 90-day-out renewal is a head who has forfeited negotiating
leverage. The vendor knows the enterprise is inside the
switching-cost trap — a switch cannot be executed in 90 days,
so any threat of a switch is not credible; a price uplift the
vendor asks for is a price uplift the enterprise pays. The
head enters the negotiation without options and negotiates on
price alone.

A 12–18-month-out renewal is a head who has the time to run a
credible alternative-vendor evaluation (see below), to author a
walkaway position, to align the CIO and CFO to execute the
walkaway if the vendor does not move, and to give the vendor's
own account team the runway to escalate internally for account
protection.

### Leverage — where it comes from

The head's leverage in the renewal comes from four sources.

- **Credible alternative-vendor evaluation.** The head has
  evaluated 2–3 alternative vendors at head-to-head scope in
  the 12 months leading to renewal — not a paper RFP but a
  substantive briefing, a competitive-demo cycle, a peer-
  reference check, a total-cost-of-ownership model. The
  vendor's account team knows the evaluation has happened; the
  head does not run the evaluation as a secret. See the
  continuity discipline in [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
  chapter 06 for the alternate-vendor awareness posture the
  evaluation extends.
- **Documented SLA underperformance.** The SLA position tracked
  in the per-vendor P&L file, cited with specifics — dates,
  incidents, credit positions, remediation-commitment slippage.
  The head does not negotiate on generic "we've been unhappy";
  the head negotiates on documented pattern.
- **Defensible growth trajectory.** The enterprise's own AI-
  system inventory growth, workflow-expansion trajectory, and
  scope-expansion plan over the next contract term. The
  vendor wants share of that growth; the head's growth story is
  leverage where the vendor sees it as expansion revenue.
- **Reference-account value.** Where the enterprise's own name
  and use-case is valuable to the vendor's own market
  positioning — a public reference, a case-study contribution,
  a speaking slot at the vendor's user conference — the head
  can trade reference-account participation for contract
  terms. The head takes the trade only where the reference is
  substantively earned; a paid-for reference the market
  discounts.

### Terms the head engages on beyond price

Procurement runs the price line. The head engages on the
substantive shape of the deal:

- **SLA definitions.** Are the SLAs measured, credited, and
  reported in ways that reflect the enterprise's actual
  operating need? An "availability" SLA that excludes
  scheduled maintenance overlapping with the enterprise's
  month-end reporting window is an SLA that does not protect
  the enterprise's operation.
- **Uptime commitments.** The specific uptime number, the
  measurement method, the exclusions, the credit schedule for
  slippage.
  <!-- needs-research: do not cite a specific uptime commitment norm across AI-governance vendor contracts without verifying against current contract templates; the range is wide. -->
- **Roadmap commitments.** Where a roadmap item is material to
  the enterprise's operating shape over the term, whether the
  contract carries a commitment against the item and what the
  remedy is if the item slips.
- **Portability commitments.** The specific export format
  commitments — OSCAL, CycloneDX ML-BOM, SPDX AI profile, or
  vendor-native but well-documented. See the continuity
  discipline in [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
  chapter 06 — a vendor whose contract does not carry
  portability is a vendor whose lock-in the head is
  underwriting.
- **Data-residency commitments.** Where the enterprise's data
  is hosted, where it is processed, where it is backed up,
  what sovereign-jurisdiction constraints apply.
- **Security-and-privacy commitments.** Independent-security-
  attestation commitments (SOC 2 Type II, ISO/IEC 27001,
  ISO/IEC 27701), breach-notification commitments,
  data-processing-agreement scope.
- **Escape-clause definitions.** The specific circumstances
  under which the enterprise can terminate the contract for
  cause, the notice period, the transition-services
  obligation the vendor carries on exit, the data-return
  discipline.

### "Renewal is not negotiation until execution" — the walkaway discipline

The head enters the renewal with a documented walkaway
position — the specific terms and price the head is not willing
to renew below — and the CIO and CFO alignment to execute the
walkaway if the vendor does not move.

The failure mode is the head who uses the renewal window to
signal displeasure to the vendor, does not have a walkaway
position, does not have CIO/CFO alignment, and eventually
renews on the vendor's terms. The vendor's account team learns
to price against that pattern; the enterprise's contract
worsens over successive renewals.

The head's discipline is the reverse. The walkaway is authored
before the negotiation opens. The CIO and CFO have seen it and
signed off. The head enters the negotiation with the alternative
plan in hand — the migration path to an alternative vendor, the
cost model of the migration, the timeline — and is prepared to
execute it. The vendor's account team reads the difference
between real walkaway posture and theatre immediately; the
negotiation shifts accordingly.

## SLA-slip escalation — the four-stage ladder in P&L framing

When a vendor is materially underperforming against contracted
SLAs, the head runs a four-stage escalation ladder. The ladder
echoes the relationship-scope escalation ladder in
[[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 06 but is
recast in P&L rather than relationship framing — each level
carries a P&L consequence the head models before triggering.

### Level 1 — account-executive engagement

The head engages the vendor's account executive with specific
evidence, a specific request, and a specific remediation
window. The engagement is documented in the vendor account file
and referenced at the next quarterly account review.

- **Evidence specificity.** Dates, incidents, SLA-attainment
  measurements, impact on the enterprise's operating shape —
  a documented pattern, not "we've been unhappy."
- **Request specificity.** Fix the pattern by a named date,
  deliver the slipping roadmap item, assign a dedicated
  technical resource, invoke SLA credits.
- **Remediation window.** The specific date the response has to
  land by. Not open-ended.
- **P&L consequence.** SLA credit invoked against the current
  period's invoice where the contract carries a credit
  schedule. The head models the credit position ahead of the
  engagement so the ask is anchored to a contractual number.

### Level 2 — vendor CxO sponsor engagement

If level 1 does not produce substantive remediation inside the
window, the head escalates to the vendor's CxO sponsor for the
account with the pattern (not a one-off incident) and a
CxO-scope commitment ask.

- **Pattern framing.** Multiple incidents, multiple missed SLA
  attainments, multiple missed remediation windows — the
  pattern level-1 engagement has failed to move.
- **CxO-scope commitment ask.** Roadmap re-commitment with
  named executive accountability, dedicated account-team
  reassignment, SLA-credit acceleration or restructuring, an
  executive-visibility remediation plan with named milestones.
- **P&L consequence.** Larger SLA credits, contract-restructure
  discussion opened mid-term, professional-services engagement
  provided at reduced or zero cost. The head models the P&L
  delta of each possible outcome before the conversation opens.

### Level 3 — internal CFO / CIO engagement

If level 2 does not produce substantive change inside the
window, the head escalates internally — to the CFO and CIO,
with the material-underperformance case, to invoke contractual
remedies and begin replacement-vendor evaluation.

- **The case.** The documented pattern, the level-1 and
  level-2 engagement records, the current cost of continued
  underperformance, and the modelled cost of the replacement
  pathway.
- **The ask.** Authority to invoke contractual remedies —
  material breach notice, SLA-credit acceleration at full
  contractual maximum, contract-restructure demand — and
  authority to begin replacement-vendor evaluation with budget
  for the migration-planning work.
- **P&L consequence.** Contract restructuring (potentially
  favourable if the enterprise extracts material concessions,
  potentially adverse if the vendor uses the moment to push
  mid-term concessions the other way), migration-planning cost,
  and replacement-vendor evaluation cost. The head brings the
  P&L model to the CFO with the case.

### Level 4 — contract termination and replacement

If the vendor still does not move, the head recommends
contract termination for cause and the CFO / CIO execute.

- **Termination-for-cause invocation.** The contract's
  material-breach clause invoked against the documented
  pattern and the failed remediation windows, with the
  contract's notice period.
- **Transition-services execution.** The exit-services
  obligation the head negotiated into the contract is invoked
  — data return in agreed-portable format, transition support
  through cutover, wind-down of vendor access.
- **Replacement-vendor onboarding.** The alternative vendor
  identified through the continuity discipline stands up on an
  accelerated timeline; the migration executes.
- **P&L consequence.** Full migration cost (implementation,
  integration, data-migration, workflow-retraining, dual-run
  period), plus replacement-vendor onboarding cost, minus the
  terminated vendor's future contracted spend, plus any
  recovered credits or termination-remedy payments the contract
  entitles the enterprise to. The head models the full P&L
  before triggering level 4; termination is not a step the head
  takes without knowing the number.

The four-stage ladder is not a threat display. Each level is
a substantive move the head is prepared to execute; each level's
P&L consequence is modelled before the head triggers it. The
vendor's account team reads the difference between posture and
real escalation posture immediately.

## Multi-year commercial disciplines

Beyond the year-over-year P&L and the renewal cycle, the head
runs several standing disciplines that keep the category
position sharp over time.

### Annual category refresh

The head runs a standing annual category refresh —
competitive-briefing sessions with 2–3 alternative vendors per
year. Not an RFP; not the head signalling a switch; an
intelligence-gathering discipline that keeps the category map
current and the alternative-vendor awareness credible. The
briefings are substantive — executive-sponsor engagement,
capability demonstration, roadmap-and-positioning questions.
The vendors know the briefing is intelligence-gathering rather
than pre-RFP; some engage enthusiastically for the account-
relationship value, some engage sceptically. Both are useful.

### Peer-institution reference network

The head talks to 2–3 peer heads-of-role annually about their
vendor experience. Not the vendor's reference list — the head's
own peer network. The conversations are candid and reciprocal.
The peer network is the source of the reference-check leverage
the head brings into renewals and into the consolidation-or-
expansion decision, and the source of the earliest signal on
category shifts — a peer whose vendor has begun to slip, a
peer whose vendor has been acquired, a peer whose vendor has
changed executive team.

### Analyst-house reads

The analyst position is one input, not the deciding one. The

### Contract-generation discipline

Every renewal is an opportunity to upgrade the contract shape,
not just to renew the price line. The head enters each renewal
with a list of specific contract-shape improvements —
portability language, escape-clause sharpening, SLA-definition
tightening, roadmap-commitment specificity — and does not
accept the vendor's paper unchanged. Successive renewals produce
successive contract generations; a contract renewed three times
without shape improvement is an account the enterprise has
under-run.

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**The RFP-and-forget pattern.** The head runs a RFP at year
zero, signs a five-year contract, and does not open the P&L
again until renewal. The vendor's account team loses focus on
the account because there is no engagement to feed. The
enterprise's implementation drifts because there is no
substantive account discipline. Renewal arrives with no
leverage — no documented pattern to negotiate against, no
alternative-vendor awareness, no walkaway position. The
remedy is the per-vendor P&L file refreshed quarterly, the
quarterly account review discipline from [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
chapter 06, and the renewal file opened 12–18 months ahead of
expiry.

**The three-tool-drift pattern.** The head has three
overlapping platforms because each was procured in response to
a different point need over time — one for a specific
sector-regulator response, one for a specific model-risk
overlay a peer institution recommended, one that came in as
part of a wider ServiceNow deployment. Nobody has ever
taken the consolidation decision. Each tool costs, each tool
requires integration and admin overhead, none of them is being
run at the depth its licence implies. The remedy is the
consolidation-or-expansion decision framework above — run the
current-state overlap map, model the cost-of-consolidation and
cost-of-run-parallel, take the decision explicitly.

**The single-vendor-lock-in pattern.** The head has consolidated
on a single vendor without portability discipline. The vendor
knows the switching cost is prohibitive and prices to the
lock-in over successive renewals. The contract's SLA and
portability terms drift because the vendor knows the head
cannot walk. The remedy is the portability discipline in the
contract-renewal section — OSCAL / CycloneDX ML-BOM / SPDX AI
profile export commitments, escape-clause specificity, and
the continuity discipline in [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
chapter 06.

**The analyst-house-driven selection.** The head has selected
based on the current Gartner Magic Quadrant / Forrester Wave
position without substantive first-hand evaluation.
<!-- needs-research: verify current analyst-house AI-governance category structures and their current leader/challenger/visionary/niche-player placements before citing specific quadrant positions; the placements shift materially between publications. -->
The enterprise inherits the analyst's shape — the criteria the
analyst weighted, the vendors the analyst covered, the biases
the analyst's methodology carries — rather than the enterprise's
own shape. When the platform underperforms against the
enterprise's actual AIMS operating need, the head has no
substantive answer beyond "the analyst rated it a leader." The
remedy is the category-map discipline above — the analyst read
is one input, not the deciding one; the head's own peer network,
briefings, and evaluation are the substantive inputs.

**The "renewal is procurement's job" abdication.** The head has
never engaged the renewal at business-owner scope. Procurement
runs the negotiation on price alone. The SLA definitions, the
portability commitments, the escape-clause specificity, the
roadmap commitments — the substantive shape of the contract —
drift because nobody with substantive scope authority engages
them. The remedy is the head's business-owner engagement on the
substantive terms alongside procurement's transactional
engagement on price. Procurement is not the enemy; procurement
is the transactional partner. The head is the business owner.

**The escalation-avoidance pattern.** The head sees the SLA-
slip pattern but does not open the escalation because the head
does not want the vendor relationship damaged. The pattern
persists. The vendor's account team learns that the pattern is
tolerated. Successive quarters compound. The audit committee
eventually asks — at the annual maturity assessment, in the
audit-committee reporting window, in the assurance-body's
findings — why the platform is not delivering against its
contracted scope. The head is unprepared to answer because the
head has never documented the pattern or opened the escalation.
The remedy is the four-stage escalation ladder above, run with
discipline. Escalation is a commercial move, not a personal
one; the vendor's account team expects the escalation when the
pattern warrants it and does not read the escalation as a
relationship rupture.

## Summary

- The GRC-for-AI category is emerging, fragmenting, and
  consolidating at the same time; the head runs the vendor
  portfolio as a commercial position rather than as a set of
  point procurement events.
- The category map is a one-page (or few-page) view of the
  GRC-for-AI landscape, refreshed annually, grouped coarsely
  into enterprise-GRC-plus-AI-overlay vendors, AI-native
  governance-of-record platforms, and adjacent-category
  vendors pushing into GRC-for-AI from model-risk,
  observability, and runtime-security directions. The map is
  labelled with its "as of" date and its caveats.
- The consolidation-or-expansion decision is the load-bearing
  commercial move, taken on a ~3-year cycle: single-vendor
  consolidation, best-of-breed expansion, or hybrid (core
  platform plus narrow-scope specialty). The head takes it
  against a documented framework — current-state overlap map,
  cost-of-consolidation, cost-of-run-parallel, migration-risk,
  roadmap-alignment over the 3-year horizon — and brings the
  decision to the CFO, the CIO, and the CAIO with the
  trade-offs on the table honestly.
- The per-vendor P&L file carries contracted spend, actual
  spend reconciled to the general ledger, total cost of
  ownership (including implementation, integration, admin, and
  workaround labour), value delivered mapped to the KRI / KPI
  portfolio, SLA position and credit position, and renewal
  disposition authored 12–18 months ahead of expiry.
- The head runs the renewal at CxO scope alongside procurement
  and finance — 12–18 months out, not 90 days out; leverage
  from credible alternative-vendor evaluation, documented SLA
  underperformance, defensible growth trajectory, and
  reference-account value; engagement on SLA definitions,
  uptime, roadmap commitments, portability, data-residency,
  security-and-privacy, and escape-clause definitions beyond
  price; and a documented walkaway position with CIO and CFO
  alignment to execute.
- The SLA-slip escalation ladder runs four stages — account
  executive, vendor CxO sponsor, internal CFO / CIO, contract
  termination and replacement — each with a modelled P&L
  consequence the head runs before triggering.
- Multi-year commercial disciplines — annual category refresh,
  peer-institution reference network, analyst-house reads with
  critical distance, and contract-generation discipline — keep
  the category position sharp over time.
- Six anti-patterns the head refuses: RFP-and-forget, three-
  tool-drift, single-vendor-lock-in, analyst-house-driven
  selection, "renewal is procurement's job" abdication, and
  escalation-avoidance.
- Chapter [04](04-ai-observability-vendor-pnl.md) carries the
  same P&L discipline against the AI-observability vendor
  portfolio; chapter [05](05-ai-runtime-security-vendor-pnl.md)
  against the AI-runtime-security vendor portfolio;
  chapter [06](06-assurance-body-relationship-pnl.md) against
  the assurance-body relationship. The disciplines compose

  governance function's spend envelope.
