# The Multi-Year Investment Case — Programme, Transformation, Incident Preparedness

## Motivation

The head of AI governance who has done the work of chapter
[01 — Owning the AI-Governance Function Budget Line](01-governance-function-budget-line.md)
carries a multi-year budget model into the annual cycle. The
model has line items. The line items have trajectories. The
trajectories have assumptions. What the model does not yet have
is a narrative — the strategic story the CFO, the CEO, and the
audit committee read the budget against.

A budget without an investment case is a spreadsheet. The CFO's
finance team can carve pieces off it in isolation — a headcount
line here, a tooling line there, a training-and-certification
line the finance team judges "nice to have" — and each carve
looks locally defensible because the case underneath is not
authored. A budget with an investment case is a strategic
narrative the CFO cannot compress without breaking the narrative
itself; the finance team can still push back, the CFO can still
challenge, but the compression conversation happens at the
level of the case rather than at the level of the line item.

The multi-year investment case is what turns the budget model
into a *decision-quality pack* the CFO, the CEO, and the audit
committee can approve. It is authored once conceptually and
delivered three-audience-differentiated. It carries three cases
in parallel — the programme case (steady-state), the
transformation case (change-of-state), and the incident-
preparedness case (contingency). It passes stress tests before
it leaves the head's desk. And it is sequenced deliberately
through the CFO one-on-one, the joint CFO/CEO conversation, and
the audit-committee pre-read before it lands in front of a
decision body.

This chapter is how the head authors that pack. It is not the
budget model itself — chapter 01 covers that — and it is not the
ROI modelling underneath the case — chapter
[07 — The ROI Case with Defensible Modelling](07-roi-case-modelling.md)
covers that. It is the case authoring discipline that sits
between the budget model and the ROI model and turns both into a
decision the enterprise's principal financial and assurance
decision-makers can materially approve.

The failure mode this chapter opens against is not the head
without a case. It is the head who has a case but has authored
it once, in a single voice, for a single audience — usually the
CFO — and who then wonders why the audit committee reads the
same pack and discounts the substance, or why the CEO reads the
same pack and cannot find the two sentences that name the
strategic imperative. The single-audience investment case is
the case that dies in three-audience delivery.

## Core concept — one case, three audiences, three case types

The investment case is authored once conceptually — the head
does not run three separate investment cases with three
different sets of assumptions — but delivered three-audience-
differentiated. The head keeps a single spine (assumptions,
line items, trajectory, ROI hooks) and produces three
audience-shaped renderings from that spine.

The three audiences read for different things.

**The CFO** reads for value-for-cost. The CFO's finance team
will re-run the numbers, challenge the assumptions, compare to
peer envelope where they have access, and probe the capex/opex
mix. The CFO's own risk register interfaces with the head's
case — an operational-risk register line for governance-function
under-investment, an enterprise-risk register line for AI-
incident exposure — and the CFO reads the case against those
register lines. The head's case for the CFO carries the three-
year cost envelope, the capex/opex breakdown, the ROI hooks
(cross-reference chapter
[07 — The ROI Case with Defensible Modelling](07-roi-case-modelling.md)),
the peer-benchmark comparators where the head has credible
peer data, and the specific finance-team-facing detail the CFO's
staff will re-run against.

**The CEO** reads for strategic imperative. The CEO does not
have the finance team's time to re-run the numbers; the CEO
has two slides and about ten minutes. The head's case for the
CEO is two slides — the strategic imperative and the specific
ask. The strategic imperative is the answer to "why is this
worth the CEO's attention and endorsement now"; the ask is
what the head needs the CEO to do. The strategic imperative
typically composes three threads: regulatory-licence-to-operate
(the enterprise cannot operate in its material markets without
this investment), competitive-differentiator (the enterprise's
customers and partners increasingly require this assurance
posture, see
[[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]]), and
incident-non-repeatability (the enterprise has committed, in the
wake of a prior incident or in anticipation of the sector's
incident pattern, to a specific incident-preparedness posture).
The ask is one of three: endorsement in the next board meeting,
veto over the CFO's compression proposal, or a specific CxO-
peer instruction (e.g., the CEO instructing the CIO to allocate
platform-integration engineering time to the transformation
case).

**The audit committee** reads for assurance adequacy. The audit
committee's question is not "is this investment worth the
money" — that is the CFO's question — but "is this investment
adequate to the assurance obligation the committee has ratified"
(see [[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]]). The
head's case for the audit committee carries the assurance-
adequacy narrative, the independence-and-rigour signal, the
mapping to the ratified AI risk appetite, and the mapping to the
audit-plan disclosure the CAE has authored (see
[[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 05). The
committee expects to see the case *before* the CFO's compression
has been applied, so the committee understands what the head is
asking for; if the CFO compresses, the committee expects to see
the compression and its assurance-adequacy implications
explicitly.

The three-audience shape is not the same document with three
covers. It is three genuine renderings of a single spine, each
shaped for the audience's decision. A CEO two-pager that opens
with a capex/opex breakdown is not a CEO document. A CFO pack
that opens with the audit-committee's risk-appetite line is not
a CFO document. An audit-committee pack that opens with ROI is
not an audit-committee document. The head authors each rendering
deliberately.

## The three case types

Underneath the three-audience delivery, the head authors three
case types in parallel. Each has its own commercial shape, its
own risk shape, and its own defence discipline. Collapsing them
into a single ask is one of the anti-patterns this chapter
names; keeping them distinct is the discipline that lets the
CFO carve compression cleanly if compression is warranted, and
that lets the head defend each case on its own merits.

### The programme case — steady-state investment

The programme case is the "running the function" case. It is
what the enterprise has to spend, each year, to run the AI-
governance function at its committed level of assurance
capability. It is the case the head expects to re-author every
year, with year-on-year trajectory changes but with a stable
structural shape.

The programme case composes from the artefacts of other
modules:

- **Headcount trajectory.** The multi-year hiring plan from
  [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 02 is
  the primary input. The programme case does not re-author the
  hiring plan; it summarises the plan's cost implication for
  each year and defends the headcount envelope against the
  function's operating workload (from
  [[mod-102-running-the-program]]) and its measurement backbone
  (from [[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]]).
- **Tooling cost.** The GRC-for-AI vendor P&L (chapter
  [03 — The GRC-for-AI Vendor P&L at CxO Scope](03-grc-for-ai-vendor-pnl.md)),
  the AI-observability vendor P&L (chapter
  [04 — The AI-Observability Vendor P&L](04-ai-observability-vendor-pnl.md)),
  and the AI-runtime security vendor P&L (chapter
  [05 — The AI-Runtime Security Vendor P&L](05-ai-runtime-security-vendor-pnl.md))
  each contribute a multi-year cost line to the programme case.
  The programme case carries the totals and points at the
  vendor P&L files for the substantive detail; a CFO who wants
  vendor-by-vendor depth reads the P&L files.
- **External-audit cost.** The assurance-body relationship P&L
  (chapter
  [06 — The Assurance-Body Relationship P&L](06-assurance-body-relationship-pnl.md))
  contributes the ISO/IEC 42001 CB engagement, the
  independent-assurance-body engagement (ForHumanity, BABL AI,
  or peer), the Big Four engagement where the enterprise runs
  one, and any specialist audit engagement the enterprise's
  risk profile warrants. The programme case treats these as
  a category, with the assurance-body P&L file as the source.
- **Maturity progression cost.** The annual maturity assessment
  cost from [[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]]
  chapter 03, plus whatever specific capability investments the
  maturity trajectory calls for that are not already captured
  in tooling or headcount. If the maturity assessment has
  named a specific gap the function has committed to closing,
  the programme case names the cost of closing it.
- **Regulatory-change-readiness cost.** The regulatory-change
  management discipline in [[mod-105-regulatory-change-
  management]] carries a standing cost the programme case
  reflects — the horizon-scanning subscription, the counsel-
  retainer for regulatory-change interpretation, the internal
  cost of running the change-management operating cadence. The
  programme case does not budget for specific regulatory
  changes at the line level (specific changes typically arrive
  faster than the annual cycle); it budgets for the *capability*
  to absorb regulatory change on the schedule the enterprise's
  markets demand.

The programme case's commercial shape is stable. Year-on-year
change is typically incremental — headcount growth in line with
the hiring plan, tooling inflation, audit-scope expansion as
the AIMS scope grows, marginal maturity-progression investment.
The programme case does not carry front-loaded capex spikes and
does not carry dual-run costs. It is the boring, essential,
running-the-function case that funds the function's baseline
capability.

The programme case's risk shape is under-investment risk. If
the CFO compresses the programme case, the function loses
capacity — evidence production slows, evaluations back up,
regulatory-change lands unabsorbed, maturity progression
stalls. The consequence is gradual rather than acute; the head
who cannot defend the programme case watches the function
degrade over eighteen to twenty-four months rather than fail
sharply in a quarter.

### The transformation case — change-of-state investment

The transformation case is the "change-of-state" case. It is
what the enterprise has to spend, over a defined multi-year
programme window, to move the function from one operating
capability level to another. Transformation cases are not
annual; they are programme-level cases that persist across two
or three annual budget cycles, with distinct commercial and
risk shapes at each stage.

The transformation cases the head is likely to carry, at
different points in the function's evolution:

- **Off spreadsheets onto a certifiable AIMS runtime.** The
  early-stage transformation. The enterprise has run AI
  governance out of spreadsheets, shared drives, and ad-hoc
  documents. The function cannot support ISO/IEC 42001
  certification against a spreadsheet backbone (see
  [[mod-102-running-the-program]]); the transformation is the
  migration onto the certifiable runtime — the GRC-for-AI
  platform, the evidence store, the control library, the
  metric-production pipeline. This transformation typically
  runs across two to three years, with front-loaded
  implementation labour in year one, dual-run cost in year one
  and part of year two (the spreadsheet substrate cannot be
  retired until the new runtime is proven), and the retirement
  savings on the far side (the spreadsheet substrate's hidden
  labour cost — the level-15 analysts' spreadsheet maintenance
  time, the level-35 evaluators' evidence-collation time, the
  level-50 architect's document-integrity supervision — is
  recovered).
- **Point tools onto a governance-of-record platform.** The
  middle-stage transformation. The enterprise has run AI
  governance on a stack of three or four point tools — a
  policy-management tool, a risk-register tool, a model-
  inventory tool, an evidence-attachment tool — each
  substantive in its own scope but none of them the system of
  record. The transformation is the consolidation onto a
  governance-of-record platform (see chapter
  [03 — The GRC-for-AI Vendor P&L at CxO Scope](03-grc-for-ai-vendor-pnl.md)),
  with the point tools either retired or repositioned as
  feeder systems. Commercial shape: implementation labour in
  year one, migration cost across year one and year two,
  parallel-run in year one, retirement savings in year two and
  onwards.
- **Legacy privacy-tooling retirement.** The late-stage
  transformation. The enterprise has been running a privacy-
  focused tool (OneTrust, TrustArc, or peer) that has been
  repurposed for AI-governance beyond its native capability.
  The transformation is the retirement of the AI-governance
  workload from the privacy tool and its migration to the
  certifiable AIMS runtime; the privacy tool continues to serve
  its native privacy scope. Commercial shape: modest
  implementation cost, migration cost across a single year,
  a marginal recurring saving as the AI-governance module of
  the privacy platform is downgraded or discontinued. Risk
  shape: the transformation has to be sequenced so the AI-
  governance scope is genuinely covered by the new runtime
  before the privacy tool's AI module is retired.

Every transformation case has a distinct commercial shape from
the programme case:

- **Front-loaded capex.** Implementation labour, license
  purchase or annual commitment, integration engineering. The
  capex weight sits in year one; year two and year three are
  primarily opex.
- **Dual-run cost during migration.** The legacy substrate has
  to continue running until the new substrate is proven; the
  enterprise pays for both for some period.
- **Retirement savings on the far side.** Post-migration, the
  legacy substrate is retired, the hidden labour it consumed is
  recovered, and the recurring cost drops materially. The
  transformation case models the recovery honestly (see
  chapter [07 — The ROI Case with Defensible Modelling](07-roi-case-modelling.md)
  for the transformation-ROI axis).

Every transformation case has a distinct risk shape from the
programme case:

- **Transformation failure is not the same as steady-state
  under-run.** A programme case under-run compresses the
  function's capacity; a transformation failure leaves the
  function stranded between two substrates, running dual-run
  cost indefinitely, and with no clear escape either forward
  or back. The head who runs a transformation case runs it as
  a commit-or-abandon decision, with the CFO's explicit
  understanding that partial funding produces the worst
  outcome (stranded transformation).
- **Sequencing risk.** The transformation depends on the AIMS
  scope being ready to move (see [[mod-102-running-the-
  program]] chapter 02), the hiring plan carrying the
  implementation capacity (see [[mod-108-leading-the-
  governance-function]] chapter 02), and the CxO peers being
  aligned on the platform-integration work (see chapter
  [08 — Coordinating with the Chief AI Officer and the Rest of the CxO Layer on the AI Budget Envelope](08-caio-and-cxo-budget-envelope-coordination.md)).
  Sequencing risk is real; the transformation case names it and
  the mitigations.

The transformation case is authored explicitly as a
transformation case — the CFO reads the front-loaded shape, the
dual-run cost, and the retirement-savings tail, and understands
that the case is not the programme case with a spike in it. The
head who blurs the transformation case into the programme case
invites the CFO to treat the front-loaded capex as programme-
case padding and to compress it accordingly.

### The incident-preparedness case — contingency investment

The incident-preparedness case is the "when it happens" case.
It is what the enterprise spends to be *ready to spend* when an
incident arrives, without necessarily spending it in the absence
of an incident. The case has a distinctive shape — some of the
spend is standing (retainers, exercises, subscriptions), some
of it is reserved (contingency), and some of it is unlocked on
event (surge capacity, external-communications engagement,
regulator-response counsel).

The incident-preparedness case composes:

- **Contingency reserve.** A named financial reserve, sized
  against the enterprise's plausible incident scenarios (see
  [[mod-110-incident-leadership]]), held by the CFO and
  releasable on the head's incident-response authority within
  a defined governance path. The reserve is not spent in the
  absence of an incident; it is *committed* in the budget so
  it is available on hours-scale rather than weeks-scale when
  an incident lands. Size the reserve honestly against
  plausible incident scope — a small incident-scope reserve
  that would exhaust in the first day of a material incident
  is worse than no reserve, because it invites the enterprise
  to treat the reserve as sufficient and delay the CFO
  conversation until it is materially too late.
  <!-- needs-research: verify typical contingency-reserve sizing bands at peer institutions and by sector, e.g. via ISF, EY / Deloitte AI-risk survey publications, or the ISACA / IIA AI-governance publications -->
- **Incident-response retainer.** A standing engagement with an
  external digital-forensics-and-incident-response (DFIR) firm
  with AI-incident specialism, or with a specialist AI-
  incident-response firm directly. The retainer is a modest
  standing cost that guarantees hours-scale response when an
  incident lands. Without the retainer, the enterprise's
  first-incident response is delayed by procurement — the head
  cannot procure a specialist DFIR firm in the hours the
  incident calendar allows.
- **Tabletop exercise budget.** A recurring annual budget for
  cross-functional tabletop exercises — the head, the CISO,
  the CPO, the DPO, the CRO, the GC, and the CAE running
  scenario-based exercises against plausible AI-incident
  patterns (see [[mod-110-incident-leadership]] for the
  scenario library and rehearsal cadence). Tabletop exercises
  cost real money — external facilitation, executive time, the
  post-exercise remediation programme — and they are what
  turns a plan on paper into a rehearsed capability.
- **Cross-functional rehearsal cadence cost.** Beyond the
  standing tabletop exercises, the head budgets for
  rehearsals that involve the operational teams — evaluation
  rehearsals, containment rehearsals, external-communications
  rehearsals. The rehearsal cadence is the difference between
  a plan and a capability.
- **External-communications retainer.** A standing engagement
  with an external communications firm with sector and
  incident-communications specialism, unlockable on incident
  event. The head is not the crisis-communications leader —
  that is typically the Chief Communications Officer or the
  General Counsel — but the head is often the substantive
  content lead on the AI-specific communications posture, and
  the head's own function needs the retainer to run the
  substantive drafting when an incident lands.

The incident-preparedness case's commercial shape is
distinctive. Most of the cost is standing — retainers,
subscriptions, exercise budgets — at a modest and predictable
level. Some of it is reserve — held but not spent in the
absence of an incident. And some of it is on-event unlock —
budgeted authority to spend on hours-scale when an incident
arrives.

The incident-preparedness case's risk shape is asymmetric. The
case defends what the enterprise *does not spend* absent an
incident but *is enabled to spend* within hours when one
arrives. The CFO's temptation is to compress the standing costs
(retainers, subscriptions, exercises) because they look like
insurance premiums with no visible payout in the absence of an
incident. The head's discipline is to make the asymmetry
visible: the standing cost is small, the on-event authority is
what the case actually buys, and the on-event authority is
worthless if the standing infrastructure is not in place when
the incident lands.

The case cites public incident precedent where the sector has
one — a widely covered prompt-injection incident, a widely
covered training-data-leakage incident, a widely covered model-
integrity incident. The precedent is not a case-study of a
specific enterprise (see the strict rule against naming
enterprises); the precedent is the *pattern* the sector has
seen, and the case names the pattern rather than the party.

## The case-document structure

The head authors the investment case to a stable template. The
template is not decorative — it is what allows the CFO's team,
the CEO's chief of staff, and the audit committee's secretary
to find the same information in the same place across annual
cycles, across the three audiences, and across the three case
types.

**Title.** The case-type and the year. "AI Governance Programme
Case — FY[X] — FY[X+2]" or "AI-Governance-of-Record Platform
Transformation — Programme Case Year [1|2|3]" or "AI Incident
Preparedness Case — FY[X]." The title is not decorative; it is
how the case is filed and referenced.

**Executive summary.** Two pages. The strategic imperative in
one paragraph. The ask (headline number, headline outcome, the
specific CFO / CEO / audit committee decision the head needs)
in a second paragraph. The risks-and-mitigations in a
compressed table. The two-pager is what the CFO and audit
committee members actually read; the deeper detail is for
their staff.

**Strategic context.** One to two pages. Where the function is
today, where the case takes it, why the case is now rather
than next year. Cross-references to the artefacts underneath —
the maturity assessment (see [[mod-107-program-measurement-
and-maturity]] chapter 03), the regulatory landscape (see
[[mod-105-regulatory-change-management]]), the sector position
(see [[mod-111-sector-program-leadership]]).

**The ask.** One page. The specific investment the head is
asking the enterprise to make. Numbers, years, categories,
CFO / CEO / audit-committee-specific decisions required.

**The case.** The substantive detail — three to five pages for
the programme case, five to seven pages for the transformation
case (transformation cases carry more phase-and-sequencing
detail), three to four pages for the incident-preparedness case.
Line-by-line defence of the ask, cross-referenced to the vendor
P&L files (chapters 03 through 06) and the ROI model (chapter
07) for the underlying detail.

**Risks and mitigations.** One page. What can go wrong with
the case as proposed. What the head has done or will do to
reduce the risks. This is not "risk theatre" — the head
identifies the genuine risks (transformation failure,
sequencing risk, key-person risk on the implementation team,
regulatory-timing risk) and the specific mitigations.

**ROI hooks.** Half a page, pointing at chapter 07. The
programme case has modest ROI hooks (the function is a cost
centre; its ROI story is primarily avoided-loss). The
transformation case has substantive ROI hooks (spreadsheet-
elimination FTE recovery, tool consolidation savings, dual-
run retirement savings). The incident-preparedness case has
distinctive ROI hooks (incident-cost avoidance, regulator-
enforcement avoidance) that the head is especially careful not
to overclaim on (see anti-patterns below).

**Appendix.** Unlimited. Line-item detail, vendor quotes,
peer-benchmark data, regulatory-source references, maturity
assessment excerpts, KRI/KPI trajectory data (from
[[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 01). The
appendix is not for the CFO; it is for the finance team, the
CAE's internal-audit staff, and the head's own defence under
challenge. The appendix carries the substance the two-pager
compresses; when a challenge lands on a specific assumption,
the head opens the appendix rather than the two-pager.

## Framing disciplines

The head applies specific framing disciplines to strengthen the
case against challenge. Each discipline is a choice about how a
line item is defended rather than about what the line item is.

**Regulatory-obligation framing.** Where a line item is required
by regulation — EU AI Act Article 9 risk-management-system
obligations for high-risk AI systems, Article 55 GPAI Code of
Practice obligations for general-purpose AI models where the
enterprise is a provider or downstream deployer at the relevant
threshold, sector-specific obligations (see
[[mod-111-sector-program-leadership]]) such as FDA SaMD
requirements or financial-services model-risk-management
requirements or medical-device-directive obligations — the head
does not defend the line as discretionary. The head describes
the obligation, cites the source, and names the enforcement
risk of non-compliance. The CFO does not compress a regulatory-
obligation line; the CFO compresses discretionary lines. The
head who fails to frame regulatory-obligation lines as
regulatory obligations invites compression that lands the
enterprise in non-compliance.

**Board-adopted risk-appetite framing.** Where the board or
audit committee has ratified an AI risk appetite (see
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]]), the head
maps the ask directly to the appetite line. "This investment
is what closes the gap between the enterprise's current AI
residual-risk position and the ratified appetite" is a defence
the CFO cannot compress without asking the audit committee to
revise its appetite — and the audit committee is unlikely to
revise the appetite it just ratified. The head does not fabricate
appetite mapping; where the appetite is genuinely silent on a
line, the head is honest that the line is not appetite-anchored
and defends it on other framing.

**Peer-benchmarking framing.** Where the head has credible peer
data — through the maturity assessment (see
[[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 03),
through community-leadership network conversations (see
[[mod-112-certifications-career-path-community-leadership]]),
or through a formal peer-benchmarking engagement — the head
positions the ask against the peer envelope. Peer-benchmark
framing is the CFO-favoured framing (finance teams work in
comparators), and the head uses it where it is genuinely
available. Two disciplines apply: (a) do not over-anchor on
peer data at the expense of enterprise-specific factors —
sector, scale, risk profile, and current maturity level all
mean the peer envelope is a range rather than a benchmark; (b)
do not cite peer data the head cannot substantiate — if the
CFO's team asks for the source and the head cannot produce
it, the case suffers materially.
<!-- needs-research: verify typical peer-benchmark envelope sources for AI-governance budget across ISF, IAPP AI Governance Center, Gartner AI TRiSM MQ, and sector regulators' published surveys -->

**Incident-precedent framing.** Where a public incident in the
sector maps to a specific investment — the case for red-team-
as-a-service after a widely covered prompt-injection incident
in the sector, the case for training-data-provenance tooling
after a widely covered data-provenance incident, the case for
model-integrity monitoring after a widely covered model-
integrity incident — the head cites the public precedent rather
than inventing one. Incident-precedent framing is particularly
powerful for the incident-preparedness case (obviously) but
also for tooling lines in the programme case that a public
incident has made materially prudent. The head cites the
pattern rather than naming a specific enterprise; the
sector's practitioner audience recognises the pattern, and the
CFO does too.

## Stress tests the case must pass

Before the head takes the case to the CFO, the head runs the
case through a stress-test discipline. The stress tests are
what the finance team will run when the case lands with them;
the head who has already run them is the head who does not lose
credibility in the finance-team review.

**The "why now, not next year" test.** The case has to name the
specific reason a year of delay is not free. Regulatory-obligation
lines pass this test easily — the obligation has a compliance
date, and delay past the compliance date is enforcement risk.
Transformation-case lines pass this test on sequencing —
delaying the transformation past its window means dual-run cost
accumulates, or the enabling capacity ages out, or the
regulatory horizon overtakes the transformation and forces a
re-scope. Incident-preparedness lines pass this test on
readiness — the incident does not schedule around the enterprise's
budget cycle, and a year of delay is a year in which the
enterprise is not ready. The head who cannot answer "why not
next year" for a line item has authored a discretionary line
that will be compressed.

**The "what breaks if compressed" test.** The case has to be
honest about what specific capability is lost at each level of
compression. "The line cannot be compressed at all" is rarely
credible; the CFO reads that framing as posture. The credible
framing is "at [X] percent compression, the following specific
capability is lost, with the following specific consequence."
The head who has authored the compression graph is the head who
negotiates from information rather than from posture. Different
line items have different compression graphs — some are
linearly compressible (each dollar of compression removes a
dollar of capability), some are step-function compressible
(compression up to a threshold has modest impact, compression
past the threshold breaks a capability), some are commit-or-
abandon (partial funding produces the worst outcome). The head
names the shape for each line.

**The "how do we know it worked" test.** The case has to name
the specific measurement — the KRI or KPI from
[[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 01, the
specific maturity-progression indicator, the specific audit-
finding trajectory — by which success is judged. "This
investment will improve governance capability" is not a
measurement; "this investment will move the intake-queue depth
KRI from [current level] to [target level] within [window]" is
a measurement. The head does not commit to measurements the
head does not believe the function can deliver; the head does
commit to measurements the head is prepared to be held to. The
audit committee particularly reads for this test — a case
without measurement is a case the committee cannot assure has
worked.

**The "who else pays" test.** The case has to be honest about
whether the cost sits with the governance function or is a
shared cost with product, engineering, security, privacy, or
legal (cross-reference [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]).
Some line items are governance-function-owned entirely (the
GRC-for-AI platform, the ISO/IEC 42001 CB engagement); some
are shared (the AI-runtime security tooling that the security
function co-owns, the AI-observability tooling that the
evaluation team co-owns); some are owned elsewhere but co-
governed (product-team model cards, engineering-team model
inventory contributions). The case that claims sole ownership
of a shared cost invites the CFO to ask why the shared partner
is not contributing; the case that hides a shared-cost
dependency invites the shared partner to disclaim in the CFO's
presence. The head is explicit about ownership and about the
cross-CxO co-funding conversations the head has had (see
chapter [08 — Coordinating with the Chief AI Officer and the Rest of the CxO Layer on the AI Budget Envelope](08-caio-and-cxo-budget-envelope-coordination.md)).

**The "what if we did nothing" counter-factual.** The case has
to describe the no-investment scenario honestly. What happens
if the case is denied entirely? The programme case's counter-
factual is capacity degradation over eighteen to twenty-four
months. The transformation case's counter-factual is stranded
substrate — spreadsheets or point-tool sprawl continue, and the
enterprise's certification and assurance posture is capped at
the substrate's ceiling. The incident-preparedness case's
counter-factual is unreadiness — the incident arrives and the
enterprise responds on days-to-weeks scale rather than hours-
to-days scale. If the case cannot survive the counter-factual —
if the honest description of "nothing happens" is "not much" —
the case is padded, and the head should compress it before the
CFO does.

## Delivery discipline

The case is authored once and delivered in a deliberate
sequence. The sequence matters — the case that lands in front
of the audit committee before the CFO has been aligned is a
case that produces a difficult audit-committee conversation
with no CFO backing; the case that lands with the CEO before
the CFO has run the finance-team analysis is a case the CEO
cannot substantively endorse.

**Step one: the CFO one-on-one.** Before the case lands with
the finance team, the head sits with the CFO alone. The
purpose is not to negotiate the number; the purpose is to walk
the CFO through the strategic context, the three case types,
the ask, and the risks — and to hear the CFO's substantive
reaction. The CFO's reaction typically identifies two or three
lines the CFO will challenge, one or two lines the CFO will
support, and the CFO's own political constraints (the board's
cost-compression signal, the CFO's own year's financial
context, the CFO's read on peer envelope). The head listens
substantively and adjusts the delivery — not the substance —
of the case for the joint conversation.

**Step two: the finance-team analysis window.** The finance
team receives the full pack, including the appendix, and has a
defined window (typically two to three weeks) to re-run the
numbers, challenge the assumptions, and produce a finance-team
read. The head is available for questions but does not push;
the finance team's read is more credible to the CFO if the
head has not lobbied for a specific outcome. Where the finance
team lands on a materially different number, the head engages
substantively — not defensively — on the specific difference.

**Step three: the joint CFO / CEO meeting.** The joint
conversation is where the CEO's endorsement is sought. The
head brings the two-slide CEO rendering and the substantive
pack; the CFO brings the finance-team analysis and the CFO's
own recommendation. The head does not surface CFO-alignment
gaps to the CEO for the first time in the joint meeting — that
is a CFO relationship failure — and the head does not go
around the CFO to the CEO. The joint conversation resolves
whatever is resolvable at CxO scope; anything unresolved goes
to the board or the audit committee.

**Step four: the audit-committee pre-read.** Before the audit
committee meets, the audit-committee-specific rendering of the
pack goes to the committee chair (and to the committee
secretary for distribution) with an offer of a pre-read
conversation. The committee chair typically takes the pre-read
offer for material asks and declines for routine renewal cases.
The head does not deliver the audit-committee rendering with
CFO-alignment gaps unresolved; the committee reads unresolved
CxO gaps as governance-function immaturity and discounts the
substance accordingly.

**Step five: the audit-committee meeting.** The head delivers
the case, hears the committee's questions, responds
substantively, and takes any committee-side commitments back
into the plan. The head does not treat the committee meeting
as ratification theatre; the committee's questions are the
substantive test of the case, and the head who cannot answer
them credibly is the head whose case will be re-worked.

**The response-to-challenge log.** Across the delivery sequence,
the head keeps a running log of the challenges raised — by the
CFO, by the finance team, by the CEO, by the audit committee —
and the response the head produced. The log has three purposes:
(a) the head does not have to re-author the same defence twice
in a single cycle; (b) the head has the material for next
year's re-authoring; (c) the head has a written trace of the
case's evolution under scrutiny, which is a governance artefact
in itself. The log is not confidential from the case audiences;
the head shares it on request.

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**The everything-in-one-case pattern.** The head collapses the
programme case, the transformation case, and the incident-
preparedness case into a single ask. The CFO cannot carve
compression cleanly — because the three case types have
different commercial and risk shapes — so the CFO carves
everything proportionately. The programme case survives at a
modest compression; the transformation case (which has commit-
or-abandon structure) is damaged worst, because a proportional
compression on a commit-or-abandon case produces the stranded
outcome. The remedy is the three-case-type discipline in this
chapter — the head keeps the cases distinct and lets the CFO
carve, or not carve, each one on its own merits.

**The narrative-only case.** The case has strategic language,
regulatory-obligation framing, and audit-committee-adjacent
prose, but no line items the finance team can model. The
finance team receives the pack, cannot re-run the numbers, and
produces a "the head has not shown the work" read. The CFO's
confidence in the substance collapses. The remedy is the
appendix discipline — the two-pager and the strategic-context
prose are for the CFO and audit committee members personally;
the appendix carries the line-item detail the finance team
needs to re-run the numbers.

**The overclaim-on-ROI case.** The case claims specific
avoided-fine amounts, specific deal-enablement upside, specific
retention deltas that the ROI model in chapter
[07 — The ROI Case with Defensible Modelling](07-roi-case-modelling.md)
will not survive when internal audit or the finance team
challenges it. The head has authored a case with a headline
ROI number the model cannot defend; when the challenge lands,
the case's credibility collapses across every line, not just
the ROI line. The remedy is the discipline in chapter 07 — ROI
is modelled with defensible inputs, sensitivity bands, and cited
assumptions, and the investment case's ROI hooks are the
modelled numbers rather than aspirational ones.

**The pass-through-of-vendor-quotes case.** The case is a stack
of vendor quotes with a covering page, and the head is a
vendor-management pass-through rather than a business owner.
The CFO reads the pack, sees no business-ownership discipline
underneath the vendor lines, and treats the case as procurement-
level rather than executive-level. The remedy is the
substantive business-owner authoring discipline in this
chapter — the vendor P&Ls (chapters 03 through 06) are inputs to
the case, but the case is the head's business-ownership
position on those P&Ls, not a re-package of them.

**The audit-committee-lite pattern.** The case for the audit
committee is a light re-brand of the CFO case — the cover
page and the executive summary are re-titled, but the substance
underneath is CFO-shaped. The audit committee reads the wrong-
audience framing (ROI opening, capex/opex detail early,
assurance-adequacy framing muted) and discounts the substance.
The head is judged as one who does not understand the audit
committee's mandate. The remedy is the three-audience-
differentiated authoring in this chapter — the audit committee
rendering opens with assurance adequacy, maps to the ratified
risk appetite, and treats ROI as a secondary concern.

**The single-year investment case.** The case has no year-two
or year-three trajectory; the CFO ratifies year one, and year
two's re-authoring lands the head in the same position twelve
months later — re-defending line items the head expected to
carry across the multi-year window, negotiating each year from
scratch, and inviting the CFO to compress each year in isolation.
The multi-year case is what turns the annual negotiation into a
programme conversation. The remedy is the multi-year discipline
in this chapter and in chapter
[01 — Owning the AI-Governance Function Budget Line](01-governance-function-budget-line.md) —
the case carries three-year trajectory even if only year one
is being ratified.

**The undiscussed-with-CxO-peers case.** The case has cross-
functional cost dependencies (shared tooling costs with
security, incident-preparedness spend that the CISO and DPO
co-govern, tooling integration cost the CIO's team carries)
that the head has not aligned with the CxO peers before the
CFO conversation. The CFO asks whether the security head has
agreed to the shared line; the answer is no; the case fails
in the CFO's presence. The remedy is the CxO-peer discipline
in [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] and in chapter
[08 — Coordinating with the Chief AI Officer and the Rest of the CxO Layer on the AI Budget Envelope](08-caio-and-cxo-budget-envelope-coordination.md) —
the head does the CxO-peer alignment before the CFO
conversation, not during it.

## Summary

- The multi-year investment case is what turns the budget model
  from chapter 01 into a decision-quality pack the CFO, the
  CEO, and the audit committee can approve. A budget without a
  case is a spreadsheet the CFO's team can carve piecewise; a
  budget with a case is a strategic narrative the CFO cannot
  compress without breaking the narrative.
- The case is authored once conceptually and delivered three-
  audience-differentiated: to the CFO on value-for-cost, to
  the CEO on strategic imperative in a two-slide summary with
  a specific ask, and to the audit committee on assurance
  adequacy mapped to the ratified risk appetite and the CAE's
  audit plan.
- The case carries three case types in parallel: the programme
  case (steady-state — headcount, tooling, external audit,
  maturity progression, regulatory-change readiness), the
  transformation case (change-of-state — off spreadsheets onto
  a certifiable AIMS runtime, off point tools onto a
  governance-of-record platform, off repurposed legacy privacy
  tooling), and the incident-preparedness case (contingency
  reserve, incident-response retainer, tabletop and rehearsal
  cadence, external-communications retainer).
- Each case type has a distinct commercial shape and a distinct
  risk shape. The programme case is stable and year-on-year
  incremental. The transformation case is front-loaded capex,
  dual-run cost during migration, and retirement savings on the
  far side, with commit-or-abandon risk shape. The incident-
  preparedness case is standing cost plus reserve plus on-event
  authority, with asymmetric risk shape.
- The case-document structure is title / executive summary
  (two pages) / strategic context / the ask / the case /
  risks and mitigations / ROI hooks / unlimited appendix. The
  two-pager is for the CFO and audit-committee members
  personally; the detail is for their staff.
- Framing disciplines the head applies: regulatory-obligation
  framing (where a line is regulator-required, defend on the
  obligation rather than as discretionary), board-adopted-
  risk-appetite framing (map the ask to the ratified appetite),
  peer-benchmarking framing (position against peer envelope
  where credible peer data exists, without over-anchoring),
  and incident-precedent framing (cite public incident patterns
  where they map to specific lines, without naming enterprises).
- Stress tests the case passes before it leaves the head's
  desk: why now not next year, what breaks if compressed, how
  do we know it worked (KRI / KPI-anchored), who else pays
  (shared-cost honesty), and the "what if we did nothing"
  counter-factual.
- Delivery sequence: CFO one-on-one, finance-team analysis
  window, joint CFO/CEO meeting, audit-committee pre-read,
  audit-committee meeting, response-to-challenge log
  maintained throughout.
- Six anti-patterns the head refuses: the everything-in-one-
  case pattern, the narrative-only case, the overclaim-on-ROI
  case, the pass-through-of-vendor-quotes case, the audit-
  committee-lite pattern, and the single-year investment case
  — plus the undiscussed-with-CxO-peers case as a seventh
  common failure the head avoids by running the CxO-peer
  alignment ahead of the CFO conversation.
- The investment case is the pack the head carries into the
  CAIO / CxO budget-envelope negotiation in chapter
  [08 — Coordinating with the Chief AI Officer and the Rest of the CxO Layer on the AI Budget Envelope](08-caio-and-cxo-budget-envelope-coordination.md);
  the case's substantive underpinning is the multi-year budget
  model in chapter
  [01 — Owning the AI-Governance Function Budget Line](01-governance-function-budget-line.md),
  the vendor P&L files in chapters 03 through 06, and the ROI
  model in chapter
  [07 — The ROI Case with Defensible Modelling](07-roi-case-modelling.md).
