# The ROI Case with Defensible Modelling — Avoided-Loss, Deal-Enablement, Trust-Margin, Transformation

## Motivation

The head of AI governance who cannot answer "what is the ROI on
your function this year" with a modelled number is a head who
negotiates the function's budget on narrative alone. The
narrative may be a good one — the regulator posture is real, the
audit-committee tone is favourable, the deal team has stopped
routing around the function on enterprise deals — and the CFO
may fund the function anyway because the narrative reads well.
But narrative funding is unstable funding. The next CFO reads
the same narrative and asks the question the current CFO has not
asked. The next audit-committee chair reads the same case pack
and finds the missing modelled backbone. The next cost cycle
compresses the function because narrative is what compresses
first when the enterprise's own margin comes under pressure.

The opposite failure is louder and destroys the head faster.
The head who answers "what is the ROI on your function" with an
over-claimed number — an eight-figure regulator-fine-avoidance
figure anchored at the EU AI Act ceiling, a deal-enablement
figure sourced from consultant benchmarks the sales team never
ratified, a transformation FTE-recovery figure that assumes
every reclaimed hour redirects into higher-value work — is a
head whose CFO finance team dismantles the model in a working
session and whose credibility does not survive the second
budget cycle. The dismantled ROI model is worse than no ROI
model at all. A head without a model can author one; a head
whose model has been dismantled by finance has to rebuild trust
before the model itself is heard.

This chapter is about the middle path. Not the aspirational
number that reads well in the case pack. Not the humble refusal
to model at all. The *defensible* ROI model — the number the
CFO's finance team can re-run against the same inputs and reach
substantively the same conclusion, and that the audit committee
can read for the discipline underneath the conclusion. The head
authors the model in four axes — avoided-loss, deal-enablement,
trust-margin, transformation — each with its own inputs, its
own probability discipline, its own sensitivity band, and its
own counter-factual baseline. The four axes compose into a
single reported ROI with per-axis breakdown, per-axis payback
period, and a sensitivity envelope the finance team can stress
without producing surprises.

The failure mode this chapter opens against is not a head
without a modelled ROI. It is a head who has a modelled ROI
that reads well and does not survive scrutiny. The chapter is
the discipline that keeps the ROI model surviving scrutiny
across the audit-committee cycle, the CFO working session, the
peer-benchmark conversation, and the multi-year budget cycle
the model has to defend.

## Core concept — the four-axis ROI model

The head authors the ROI model in four axes because a single-
axis ROI model does not survive the audit committee, and a
five-or-more-axis model overlaps enough that the finance team
double-counts and discounts. Four is the number that carries the
substantive commercial logic of the function without collapsing
into one figure or fragmenting into many.

**Avoided-loss.** The function reduces the enterprise's expected
loss across three specific categories: regulator-fine avoidance
(fines the enterprise would have incurred absent the function's
control surface), incident-cost avoidance (direct and indirect
cost of AI incidents the function's evidence and control surface
would have reduced the frequency or severity of), and third-
party-defence cost avoidance (the reduced cost of defending the
enterprise against third-party claims — customer, regulator,
class-action, partner — when the head can produce the AIMS audit
trail on demand rather than reconstruct it under litigation
pressure). Avoided-loss is modelled as expected-loss reductions
— probability times impact — rather than as deterministic
outcomes; the head does not claim avoided fines that were never
threatened.

**Deal-enablement.** The function enables commercial outcomes
the enterprise would otherwise have missed. Four specific
sub-lines: trust-margin at the deal level (revenue that would
not have closed absent the function's assurance surface),
regulator-approval-speed premium (revenue captured earlier in
the regulated-product cycle because regulatory-approval cycles
moved faster with the function's evidence pack in hand),
tender-eligibility uplift (public-sector and enterprise
tenders the enterprise is qualified for that competitors
without an AIMS are not), and enterprise-customer AI-trust
requirements coverage (enterprise customers whose AI-trust
requirements — the customer's own supplier-AI questionnaire,
the customer's own third-party AIMS expectations — the
enterprise satisfies where competitors do not). Deal-enablement
is sourced from the enterprise's own deal registry rather than
from external benchmarks the audit committee will discount.

**Trust-margin.** The function shifts the enterprise's trust
posture in two markets that materially move over time:
customer-retention delta (retention above the counter-factual
baseline the head defines explicitly) and employee-attraction
delta (recruitment win-rate and retention above the counter-
factual baseline). Trust-margin is not deal-enablement — deal-
enablement is what happens at contract signing, trust-margin
is what happens across the customer lifecycle and the employee
lifecycle — and the head models it separately so the audit
committee reads two distinct kinds of value.

**Transformation.** The function converts labour hours from
manual GRC work — spreadsheet maintenance, evidence-collection
by hand, control-mapping re-authoring per audit, ad-hoc report
production — into higher-value work when the governance-of-
record platform lands and the AIMS operating calendar is
running (see chapters
[03](03-grc-for-ai-vendor-pnl.md) and
[[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 01).
Transformation is the most tractable axis to model because it is
internal labour arithmetic — hours redirected times loaded rate
— and it is also the axis where the head is most tempted to
over-claim; the head models it carefully.

The four axes are chosen deliberately to be non-overlapping. A
deal where the enterprise-customer explicitly cited AI-governance
posture as a factor is deal-enablement, not also trust-margin;
the head does not double-count. A regulatory approval that
arrived faster because of the function's evidence pack is deal-
enablement (via the regulator-approval-speed premium), not
avoided-loss; the head does not double-count. Anti-double-count
discipline is what keeps the composed ROI number defensible.

## The modelling discipline — what makes an ROI number defensible

Every axis rests on the same modelling discipline. The discipline
is what the audit committee reads; the number is what the CFO
reads. A CFO satisfied on the number and an audit committee
unsatisfied on the discipline is the head's worst outcome — the
budget lands in year one and the audit committee's discipline
questions surface in year two and undo year one. The head
authors the discipline first.

### Inputs are cited to primary source

Every input in the model carries a citation. The head does not
cite the ROI model to itself and does not cite the ROI model to
consultant summaries that themselves cite unknown sources. The
head cites to primary source and holds the citations in the
model file so the finance team can trace each number back to the
source in one hop.

- **Regulator-fine averages** are sourced to the regulator's
  public enforcement records — the ICO's enforcement register,
  the CNIL's decisions register, the FTC's consent-decree
  archive, the AI-Act national-competent-authority enforcement
  publications as they come online. Each specific figure the head
  uses carries a `<!-- needs-research: verify current fine
  averages against source register -->` marker in the model so
  the head or a research assistant checks the source before the
  model is circulated.
- **Incident-cost benchmarks** are sourced to the industry-
  standard cost-of-a-breach reference (Ponemon / IBM Cost of a
  Data Breach report, Verizon DBIR, and adjacent sources) with
  the standing caveat that AI-incident cost data is scarce and
  the head is modelling against the closest available proxy.
  Every benchmark the head uses carries a `<!-- needs-research:
  verify current benchmark; note AI-specific proxy limitation
  -->` marker.
- **Deal-enablement inputs** are sourced to the enterprise's own
  deal registry — the CRM deal record, the win/loss debrief, the
  buyer-side procurement questionnaire — not to external
  benchmarks. The head cross-references with the enterprise's
  sales function so each deal-enablement claim carries a sales-
  side ratification.
- **Trust-margin inputs** are sourced to the enterprise's HR
  data (recruitment funnel, offer-accept rate, tenure
  distribution) and the enterprise's CRM data (customer-retention
  cohorts, customer-satisfaction longitudinal reads). External
  benchmarks are used as sanity checks, not as the primary
  citation; the audit committee reads external benchmarks with
  suspicion, and the head does not stake the model on them.
- **Transformation inputs** are sourced to the function's own
  operating data — hours-per-artefact from the platform, hours-
  per-audit-cycle from the level-15 analysts' time capture,
  loaded-rate figures from the enterprise's finance function.

A citation the head cannot trace to primary source is a citation
the head removes from the model.

### Probabilities are named ranges, not points

The avoided-loss axis rests on probability. The head does not
express the probability as a single number; single numbers read
as false precision, and the audit committee discounts them. The
head expresses the probability as a named range with the
reasoning behind the range.

The good expression: "regulator-fine avoidance in the 5–20%
probability range per year at the enterprise's current control
maturity, reasoning: (i) the sector's public enforcement history
across the last three years shows a base rate of
[base-rate-figure] per enterprise-year at comparable maturity;
(ii) the enterprise's specific exposure is elevated relative to
the base rate because of [named factors — high-impact use case
concentration, cross-border data-flow surface, sector-specific
regulator scrutiny]; (iii) the enterprise's specific control
maturity is above the base rate because of [named factors —
ratified AIMS scope, running control library, standing evidence
production, independent-assurance signal]." The range is 5–20%
because those specific factors compose to something inside that
band; the head does not know where inside the band, and the
head does not pretend to.

The bad expression: "regulator-fine avoidance at 12% probability
per year". The audit committee reads 12% as an unearned
precision and discounts the entire axis on that reading alone.

The same range discipline applies to incident-cost avoidance
(probability of an AI incident of specified severity per year,
in a range), to third-party-defence cost avoidance (probability
of a third-party claim reaching defence-cost threshold per year,
in a range), and to every probability input in the model. The
head lists the range, lists the reasoning, and lists the source
of the reasoning.

### Sensitivity analysis is built in

Every headline number the model reports is reported at three
sensitivities — low, central, and high — with the assumptions
behind each named. The finance team can re-run the model at
their own inputs; the CFO can read the central number and know
the low and high shape without asking; the audit committee can
read the sensitivity band as evidence that the model was
authored honestly rather than optimistically.

Sensitivity is not the same as probability range. Probability
range lives inside the avoided-loss axis (what is the
probability of the loss occurring). Sensitivity lives across
every axis (what is the reported ROI number under conservative,
central, and aggressive input assumptions). A model that reports
only the central number and hides the sensitivity band reads as
an advocacy pack; the CFO's finance team reads that reading
immediately.

The head reports the central number in headline position and
puts the low-central-high band directly under it. The head does
not report the high number as headline and the low number as
footnote; the audit committee reads the reordering as advocacy
and discounts the pack.

### Counter-factual baseline is explicit

Every ROI figure is versus a counter-factual baseline. The head
defines the counter-factual explicitly, and the counter-factual
is realistic rather than convenient. The most common failure is
to model against a "no-function" counter-factual that the
enterprise would never have chosen — a counter-factual with no
AI-governance function at all, no AIMS scope, no control
library, no evidence surface — which produces a very large
positive ROI that the audit committee reads as strawman
authoring.

The head's counter-factual is the *realistic alternative* the
enterprise would plausibly have run absent the function's
current shape. Usually that is one of:

- **The prior-state counter-factual** — what the enterprise's
  AI-governance posture was two or three years ago, with the
  spreadsheet-based control tracking, the ad-hoc evidence
  production, the reactive incident response, the peripheral
  audit-committee reporting posture. This is the counter-factual
  a head at the early-stage end of the maturity progression
  ([[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]] chapter 02) uses
  because it is the counter-factual the enterprise actually
  came from.
- **The peer-baseline counter-factual** — what an enterprise of
  the same size, sector, and regulatory exposure without the
  function's current investment would plausibly look like today.
  This is the counter-factual a head at a more mature end of the
  progression uses because the prior-state is now several years
  stale.
- **The regulated-minimum counter-factual** — what the enterprise
  would have to do at the bare regulatory-minimum level to
  remain in operation. This is the counter-factual the head
  uses when the sector has moved to a regulated-minimum floor
  ([[mod-111-sector-program-leadership]]) that the enterprise's
  investment must be measured against.

The head names which counter-factual the model uses, why, and
what changes about the ROI number under each of the plausible
counter-factuals. The audit committee reads the counter-factual
choice as one of the discipline signals.

### Attribution discipline

The head does not claim credit the function did not earn. A deal
that closed and where the buyer cited AI-governance posture as a
factor is one thing; a deal that closed while the enterprise had
an AI-governance posture is a different thing, and the head
does not conflate the two. The head models attribution honestly
in three tiers:

- **Sole cause** — the function is the reason the outcome
  occurred (rare; the head reserves this tier for outcomes where
  the function's absence would demonstrably have produced the
  opposite outcome, and where the sales function or the customer
  or the regulator has affirmatively named the function as sole
  cause).
- **Material contributor** — the function is one of several
  named factors and the outcome would plausibly have differed
  absent the function (most deal-enablement lines sit here;
  regulator-approval speed and enterprise-customer AI-trust
  coverage typically sit here).
- **Contributing signal** — the function is present in the
  picture and the outcome may have been influenced but the head
  cannot attribute directly (trust-margin lines often sit here;
  the head reports these at reduced weighting rather than
  full-weight ROI credit).

The tier is named in the model. A deal-enablement figure
reported at material-contributor weight is a different number
from the same figure reported at sole-cause weight; the head
publishes both, marks which tier each figure is at, and lets the
CFO and audit committee read the attribution discipline
directly.

## Avoided-loss modelling in detail

Avoided-loss is modelled in three sub-axes, each with its own
inputs, its own probability discipline, and its own composition
with the enterprise's own risk register.

### Regulator-fine avoidance

The head models per regulator relevant to the enterprise's
scope. For most enterprises that means at least:

- **The EU AI Act** — Article 99 fine ceilings apply to the
  provider or deployer of a high-risk or GPAI system operating
  in the EU market. The head models the enterprise's specific
  AI-system inventory against the Article 99 fine architecture
  (prohibited-practice ceilings, high-risk-obligation ceilings,
  information-obligation ceilings) with a `<!-- needs-research:
  verify current AI Act Article 99 fine ceilings and their
  application at provider vs deployer scope -->` marker. The
  model does *not* anchor at ceiling; the head models the
  expected loss (probability of triggering ceiling times
  ceiling, plus probability of triggering lesser tiers times
  those tiers) and reports the composed expected loss with the
  named probability range.
- **GDPR-adjacent regulators** where the AI system processes
  personal data — GDPR Article 83 fine architecture applies at
  the data-processing scope, and the head models the enterprise's
  AI-system inventory against the Article 83 fine architecture
  (higher tier — up to 4% of global annual turnover — for
  Article 83(5) infringements; lower tier for Article 83(4)
  infringements) with a `<!-- needs-research: verify current
  Article 83 tiering and its application to AI-processing
  scenarios -->` marker. Again the head models expected loss,
  not ceiling.
- **Sector-specific regulators** — FCA / PRA in financial
  services, FDA / MHRA / EMA in medical devices, OCR / HHS in
  health-adjacent, EEOC / OFCCP in hiring-decisioning, sector-
  specific consumer-protection regulators in credit-decisioning,
  and the sector-specific AI-adjacent guidance that regulators
  in each vertical are publishing on a rolling basis (see
  [[mod-111-sector-program-leadership]] for the sector-specific
  regulatory architecture the head models against).

For each regulator the head lists: the fine-architecture
citation with the needs-research marker, the enterprise's
specific exposure (which AI systems fall in scope), the base
rate of enforcement action against comparable enterprises, the
probability range given the enterprise's control maturity, and
the expected-loss figure at low-central-high sensitivity.

The head does *not* claim avoided-fine at ceiling. Ceiling
anchoring is the single fastest way to have the entire ROI model
dismissed; the audit committee reads a ceiling anchor as
advocacy authoring and discounts the model wholesale.

### Incident-cost avoidance

The head models per-incident-class rather than per-incident.
Three classes carry most of the modelled load:

- **Data-breach-adjacent incidents** — AI systems that leak
  training-data content or user-input content into inference
  output, model-inversion incidents that reconstruct training
  data, cross-tenant leakage in multi-tenant model deployments.
  Modelled against the industry-standard breach-cost benchmarks
  with the AI-proxy caveat named, and against the enterprise's
  own historical breach-cost data where applicable.
- **Discrimination-litigation-adjacent incidents** — AI systems
  producing outcomes that trigger class-action or regulator-
  driven discrimination claims (hiring-decisioning, credit-
  decisioning, insurance-underwriting, educational-decisioning).
  Modelled against the sector's public discrimination-litigation
  cost history and the enterprise's own exposure.
- **Mis-decisioning-adjacent incidents** — AI systems producing
  materially wrong outputs at scale (medical-decisioning error,
  financial-loss-producing recommendation, safety-adjacent
  mis-decisioning). Modelled against the sector's mis-
  decisioning cost history and the enterprise's own historical
  incident cost.

For each class the head lists: the benchmark citation with the
needs-research marker, the enterprise's specific exposure (which
AI systems and use cases fall in scope), the probability range
of an incident of that class at specified severity, and the
expected-loss figure. The incident-preparedness discipline in
[[mod-110-incident-leadership]] is what the frequency-and-
severity reduction rests on; the ROI credit the head takes for
incident-cost avoidance is bounded by the actual reduction the
incident-preparedness discipline is delivering.

### Third-party-defence cost avoidance

Third-party defence is the cost of defending the enterprise
against claims from customers, regulators, partners, class-
action plaintiffs, and other third parties. The AI-governance
function reduces this cost specifically because the AIMS audit
trail is producible on demand. Absent the AIMS audit trail, the
enterprise's third-party defence goes through the same
reconstruction-under-pressure exercise every claim triggers —
counsel hours re-authoring the control history from spreadsheets
and email, litigation-hold surface expanding across every system
that touched the AI system, expert-witness cost for the AIMS
posture the enterprise is now trying to reconstruct in
depositions.

The head models the avoided defence cost per plausible third-
party engagement type — regulator inquiry (short-window
information request), regulator investigation (multi-month deep
audit), customer contractual dispute, class-action discovery,
partner-driven third-party AIMS verification — with the
enterprise's own counsel-cost data or the sector's publicly
reported defence-cost data (with needs-research markers). The
head cross-references with the General Counsel (see
[[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]) to ensure the defence-
cost inputs match the GC's own view of what those defences would
cost.

## Deal-enablement modelling in detail

Deal-enablement is the axis where the head is most tightly
coupled to the enterprise's own sales and commercial functions.
The head does not source deal-enablement from external
benchmarks; the head sources from the enterprise's own deal
registry, cross-referenced with the sales function's own
attribution.

### The four deal-enablement sub-lines

- **Trust-margin at deal scope.** Deals in the enterprise's own
  registry where the buyer explicitly cited AI-governance posture
  as a factor — usually captured in the win/loss debrief field or
  the buyer's own procurement questionnaire response. The head
  runs the deal-registry query against a set of specific tags
  (buyer AIMS requirement satisfied, buyer AI-trust
  questionnaire satisfied, buyer named ISO 42001 certification
  or equivalent, buyer named the enterprise's AI-governance
  posture as a decision factor). The head reports the deal-
  registry-derived figure at the material-contributor tier
  unless the win/loss debrief specifically names the function
  as sole cause.
- **Regulator-approval-speed premium.** For regulated products
  where regulatory approval gates market entry (medical device,
  drug, financial product, high-risk AI system under the EU AI
  Act), faster regulatory approval means earlier revenue capture.
  The head models the speed premium as revenue-per-week
  advanced, cross-referenced with the regulatory-affairs
  function's own view of approval-cycle acceleration attributable
  to the function's evidence pack. The head does not claim the
  entire regulatory-approval-cycle acceleration; the head claims
  the portion attributable to the function's evidence
  contribution.
- **Tender-eligibility uplift.** Public-sector tenders, large-
  enterprise tenders, and regulated-sector tenders increasingly
  require an AIMS or equivalent AI-governance posture as an
  eligibility criterion. The head models the eligibility uplift
  as the value of tenders the enterprise participated in where
  eligibility hinged on the function's posture. The head cross-
  references with the tender-response function or the government-
  relations function to ratify the eligibility attribution.
- **Enterprise-customer AI-trust requirements coverage.**
  Enterprise customers — banks, insurers, healthcare providers,
  regulated-sector buyers — increasingly issue supplier-AI
  questionnaires with specific AI-trust requirements. The head
  models the value of the enterprise-customer revenue segment
  where these requirements are being met, cross-referenced with
  the customer-success function's data on questionnaire
  response outcomes.

### The sales-side ratification discipline

Every deal-enablement figure in the model carries a sales-side
ratification. The head runs the model past the Chief Revenue
Officer or Chief Commercial Officer, past the head of enterprise
sales, past the tender-response lead where relevant, and past
customer-success leadership where relevant. Each sub-line
carries a named ratifier and a ratification date. A figure the
sales function has not ratified is a figure the head reports at
significantly reduced weight or removes from the model
altogether.

The head is disciplined about *what* the sales-side ratification
means. Ratification is not sales-side endorsement of the ROI
figure as marketing material; ratification is sales-side
confirmation that the deal attribution is honest. A sales
leader who confirms "yes, the AIMS posture was a material
factor in the buyer's decision, and the win/loss debrief field
supports the attribution" has ratified. A sales leader who says
"sure, put it in the model" without looking at the underlying
deal record has not ratified; the head treats the unlooked-at
endorsement as un-ratified.

## Trust-margin modelling in detail

Trust-margin sits between deal-enablement and transformation.
It is longer-cycle than deal-enablement — retention plays out
across a customer lifecycle, employee attraction plays out
across recruiting funnels and tenure — and it is less tractable
than transformation, because retention and attraction outcomes
are influenced by many factors of which the function's
governance posture is only one.

The head models trust-margin cautiously. The temptation to
claim large retention deltas and large employee-attraction
deltas is real, and the audit committee reads unclaimed
attribution as unearned; the head under-claims deliberately.

### Customer-retention delta

The head models retention delta versus the counter-factual
baseline. Counter-factual is critical here: retention absent the
function's governance posture is not zero, and the head does
not claim the full retention figure as governance-driven. The
head models the *delta* — the portion of retention that is
plausibly attributable to the governance posture.

Inputs are the enterprise's own CRM and customer-success data:
retention rates by customer cohort, exit-survey data where
customers name governance-adjacent factors, satisfaction-
longitudinal reads that correlate with governance-posture
milestones (AIMS certification, independent-assurance publication,
material regulatory approval). External benchmarks — industry
retention rates for enterprises with and without AI-governance
postures — are used as sanity checks only.

The head reports the retention delta at contributing-signal
tier unless there is specific evidence — an exit survey where
governance-posture is the top-named factor, a customer-success
retention play that specifically leveraged the governance
posture — that supports a material-contributor tier.

### Employee-attraction delta

Employees — especially senior AI-adjacent talent, senior
governance-adjacent talent, and adjacent senior technical
talent — increasingly assess prospective employers on AI-
governance posture. The head models the employee-attraction
delta as the recruitment win-rate and tenure delta versus the
counter-factual baseline.

Inputs are the enterprise's own HR data: offer-accept rates
for target-role recruiting, tenure distribution by hire cohort,
exit-interview data where governance-adjacent factors are
named, recruiting-funnel data segmented by the enterprise's
governance-posture visibility (candidates who cited the
enterprise's public governance materials, candidates who
attended a community event the head participated in, and so on
— see
[[mod-112-certifications-career-path-community-leadership]] for
the community-visibility discipline).

The head cross-references with the CHRO
([[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]) so the employee-
attraction figure carries HR-side ratification the way the
deal-enablement figure carries sales-side ratification. The
figure the CHRO has not ratified is the figure the head
reports at reduced weight or removes.

## Transformation modelling in detail

Transformation is the axis most tractable to model because it
is internal labour arithmetic — hours redirected times loaded
rate — and it is also the axis where the head is most tempted
to over-claim. The head models it with the tightest discipline
of the four axes because every over-claim in transformation is
visible on the operating side of the function and the CFO's
finance team can validate the claim directly.

### Spreadsheet-elimination FTE-recovery

The mechanics are simple. Before the governance-of-record
platform lands (chapter [03](03-grc-for-ai-vendor-pnl.md)),
level-15 analysts and level-35 evaluators spend a measurable
number of hours per audit cycle on spreadsheet maintenance,
evidence-collection by hand, control-mapping re-authoring, and
ad-hoc report production. After the platform lands and the
AIMS operating calendar is running, those hours reduce
materially. The reduction times the loaded rate is the labour
cost recovered.

The head measures the before-and-after hours with the level-15
analysts' own time capture — not from memory, not from an
estimate the head produces without asking, but from a specific
time-capture exercise the analysts run before the platform
lands and again after the platform lands.

### The redirection discipline

The head is disciplined about one specific failure mode: the
recovered hours must actually redirect to higher-value work for
the ROI credit to hold. Hours that disappear into unstructured
overhead — general availability, meeting expansion, ambient
administrative slack — are not recovered hours in the ROI
sense; the CFO's finance team runs the "did those hours
redirect" check on transformation figures specifically because
this failure mode is common.

The head models redirection explicitly. For each recovered
FTE-hour category the head names where the hours redirected:
into higher-tier evaluation work, into deeper independent-
assurance participation, into the maturity progression
described in [[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]],
into the incident-preparedness deepening described in
[[mod-110-incident-leadership]], into sector-program
contribution described in [[mod-111-sector-program-
leadership]], or into direct expansion of the AIMS scope. Hours
without a named redirection destination are not counted in the
transformation ROI.

The head also models the redirection at the *individual level*
where possible — the specific analyst's specific reassignment
of hours from spreadsheet maintenance to a specifically-named
higher-value workstream — so the finance team's "did those
hours redirect" check succeeds when run against actual named
work.

### Composing with the headcount P&L

Transformation FTE-recovery composes carefully with the
headcount P&L authored in
[[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 02. The
head does not double-count. If the transformation recovers ten
analyst-FTE-hours per week and the headcount P&L reflects
that recovery as capacity absorbed into the plan's growth
trajectory, the head does not also claim the recovered hours
as ROI credit at loaded rate. The head claims either the
avoided-headcount-growth credit *or* the redirected-hours
credit, not both.

## Composing the four axes into a single ROI number

The four axes compose into a single reported ROI with the
composition discipline the finance team reads directly.

### Non-overlapping by construction

The axes were chosen to be non-overlapping. Deal-enablement is
distinct from trust-margin (deal-scope versus lifecycle-scope);
avoided-loss is distinct from deal-enablement (expected-loss
reduction versus revenue enablement); transformation is distinct
from headcount P&L (recovered labour versus authored labour).
Every apparent overlap the finance team may raise is one the
head has already thought through and named in the model. The
common candidates:

- **A deal where the buyer cited AI-governance posture and the
  customer subsequently renewed for reasons that include the
  posture.** The head counts the initial deal-close in deal-
  enablement and counts subsequent renewal delta in trust-
  margin only if the renewal exceeded the counter-factual
  renewal rate and the delta is attributable at contributing-
  signal or better tier. The head does not count the same
  revenue twice.
- **A regulator-approval-speed premium and an avoided-fine
  figure for the same regulator.** The head counts the speed
  premium in deal-enablement (revenue captured earlier) and
  counts the avoided-fine figure in avoided-loss (expected-
  loss reduction). These are distinct outcomes and the head
  names the distinction explicitly so the finance team does not
  read them as double-counting.
- **A transformation FTE-recovery figure and an avoided-headcount
  figure in the multi-year budget model.** The head counts one
  or the other, not both, as noted in the transformation
  section above.

### Modelled separately, reported together

The head reports the ROI as the composed number with the axis
breakdown directly under it. The CFO reads the composed number
first; the axis breakdown is what the CFO's finance team reads
to validate. The audit committee reads the axis breakdown as the
substantive content and treats the composed number as summary.

The model file itself is authored so each axis is a distinct
section — distinct inputs, distinct probability discipline,
distinct sensitivity band — and the composed number is a
computed cell rather than an authored one. A model where the
composed number is authored directly, and the axis breakdowns
are back-solved from it, is a model the finance team reads as
advocacy and dismantles.

### Payback-period discipline

Every ROI figure carries a payback period per axis and a
combined payback period, at the low-central-high sensitivity
band. Payback is what the CFO's finance team reads for capital-
allocation comparison against other enterprise investments; a
model that reports ROI without payback is a model the CFO reads
as incomplete.

Payback period per axis matters because the axes have different
time profiles. Transformation payback is fast (labour is
recovered in months); deal-enablement payback is medium (deal
cycles play out across quarters and years); trust-margin
payback is slow (retention and attraction play across
multi-year lifecycles); avoided-loss payback is discontinuous
(expected loss is realised in the year of the event, not
smoothly across time). The head reports the profile
transparently so the CFO reads the combined payback as
composed of distinct-shape sub-payback profiles, not as a
uniform payback that averages out the shape.

## The audit-committee reading — discipline before number

The audit committee reads the ROI model differently from the
CFO. The CFO's finance team reads the number and the workings
underneath. The audit committee reads the discipline the model
is authored with, and reads the discipline as one of the signals
that the head is running the function credibly (see
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]]).

A CFO satisfied on the number and an audit committee
unsatisfied on the discipline is the head's worst outcome. The
budget lands in year one and the audit committee's discipline
questions surface in year two — as pointed questions in the
audit-committee cycle, as material findings from the internal
audit function, as loss-of-confidence signals in the board's
private session — and undo year one. The head anticipates the
audit-committee questions before the pack is circulated.

The specific questions the head prepares for:

- **"How was this probability estimated?"** The head answers
  with the named range, the source of the range, the specific
  factors that place the enterprise inside the range, and the
  specific caveats about factors that could shift the range.
- **"What is the sensitivity band?"** The head answers with the
  low-central-high figures and the specific input assumptions
  that produce each band position. The audit committee does not
  want a range without the workings; the range without the
  workings reads as manufactured.
- **"How much of the deal-enablement claim did the sales
  function ratify?"** The head answers with the named ratifier
  for each sub-line, the ratification date, and the ratification
  scope. The head does not name a ratifier who did not review
  the underlying deal record; the audit committee reads that
  gap immediately.
- **"What is the counter-factual you are modelling against?"**
  The head answers with the specific counter-factual (prior-
  state, peer-baseline, or regulated-minimum), the reasoning
  for the choice, and the ROI figures under each of the
  plausible counter-factuals.
- **"How much of the transformation FTE-recovery has actually
  redirected to higher-value work?"** The head answers with the
  named redirection destinations, the individual-level
  reassignment evidence where available, and the specific
  workstreams that received the redirected capacity.

Every anticipated question has an answer prepared in the model
before the pack is circulated. The audit-committee cycle is not
the venue for the head to think through the answer for the
first time.

## Refresh cadence — the ROI model as a living artefact

The ROI model is not authored once and left in place. It is a
living artefact refreshed on a defined cadence.

- **Annual refresh at budget-cycle preparation.** The head
  refreshes the full model against the current year's inputs
  as part of the multi-year budget authoring cycle. The refresh
  updates the deal-registry-derived deal-enablement figures
  against the most recent close-year, updates the incident-cost
  benchmarks against the most recent industry-standard reports,
  updates the regulator-fine architecture against current
  enforcement records, updates the HR and CRM inputs to
  trust-margin against the current cohort, and updates the
  transformation figures against the current operating data.
- **Material refresh on a specific triggering event.** The head
  refreshes the affected axis of the model on:
  - Ratification of a new regulation that changes the fine
    architecture (a new AI Act national implementing statute,
    a new sector-specific AI regulation, a new GDPR-adjacent
    guidance update). The avoided-loss axis is refreshed.
  - A public incident in the sector that changes the incident-
    cost benchmarks or the plausible probability range
    (a competitor's material AI incident that shifts the
    sector's base rate, a public regulator enforcement action
    that changes the fine ceiling anchor). The avoided-loss and
    trust-margin axes are refreshed.
  - A substantive customer or investor conversation that shifts
    trust-margin inputs (a major customer's public statement on
    AI-governance requirements, a major investor's public
    stance on portfolio-company AI-governance expectations). The
    trust-margin axis is refreshed.
  - A completed transformation programme that shifts
    transformation inputs (the governance-of-record platform
    landing, the AIMS operating calendar becoming steady-state,
    the level-15 analysts' hours-per-audit-cycle stabilising at
    the post-transformation figure). The transformation axis is
    refreshed.
- **Not refreshed quarterly.** The head does not refresh the ROI
  model at the same quarterly cadence as the budget forecast.
  The number does not move at that cadence; quarterly refresh
  reads as churn to the CFO and audit committee and undermines
  the model's authority. The head refreshes on the cadences
  above and reports "no material change since last refresh"
  when quarterly discussion touches the ROI.

The refresh log lives in the model file itself. Every refresh
has a date, a scope (which axes were refreshed), a triggering
reason (annual budget cycle, specific event), and a summary of
what changed. The audit committee reads the refresh log as
evidence the model is maintained; a model with no refresh log
is a model that has been static since first authoring, and the
audit committee discounts it accordingly.

## Anti-patterns the head refuses

**The single-number ROI pattern.** The head reports "£X return
on £Y invested" as one composed number with no axis breakdown,
no sensitivity band, no counter-factual named, no attribution
tier discipline, no refresh log. The CFO's finance team dismantles
it in ten minutes — the composed number is not decomposable to
inputs, the inputs cannot be traced to primary source, the
probability ranges are absent, and the attribution is opaque.
The remedy is the axis-decomposed, sensitivity-banded, counter-
factual-named authoring discipline above.

**The over-claimed avoided-fine pattern.** The head cites
regulator-fine avoidance at ceiling levels — 4% of global
turnover, 7% of global turnover — as the anchor figure, without
the probability discipline that produces expected loss rather
than ceiling. The audit committee reads the ceiling anchor as
advocacy authoring and discounts the entire ROI model on that
reading alone. The remedy is expected-loss modelling — ceiling
times probability, in a named range, with the reasoning behind
the range — and the head never anchors the model on the
ceiling itself.

**The unattributed deal-enablement pattern.** The head claims a
deal-enablement figure that the sales function did not ratify.
The sales function pushes back in a joint conversation ("we
don't attribute that deal to your function"), the deal-
enablement axis collapses, and the composed ROI figure loses
credibility across all axes because the finance team now reads
the whole model as advocacy. The remedy is the sales-side
ratification discipline — every deal-enablement sub-line
carries a named ratifier and a ratification date — and the head
removes or reduces figures the sales function has not ratified.

**The all-transformation-FTE-recovery-is-credit pattern.** The
head claims full labour-hour recovery from spreadsheet
elimination without demonstrating redirection to higher-value
work. The CFO's finance team runs the redirection check —
"which specific analyst redirected which specific hours to which
specific higher-value workstream" — and the answer is
"unstructured availability" for a material portion of the
claimed recovery. The transformation axis is dismantled, and
the model's operational discipline is questioned wholesale.
The remedy is the redirection discipline — every recovered
FTE-hour category has a named redirection destination, and
hours without a named destination are not counted.

**The point-estimate no-sensitivity pattern.** The head reports
one number per axis without a sensitivity band. The audit
committee reads the single number as false precision and
discounts the pack; the CFO's finance team cannot re-run the
model at their own input assumptions because the model does not
expose the sensitivity structure. The remedy is the low-central-
high sensitivity discipline for every headline figure, with the
input assumptions behind each band position named.

**The refresh-neglect pattern.** The head authored the ROI model
in year one and has not refreshed it in three years. The model
reflects the year-one regulatory environment, the year-one deal
registry, the year-one HR data, and the year-one transformation
baseline. The audit committee reads the model against the
current environment and discounts every axis. The remedy is the
refresh cadence above — annual at budget-cycle preparation,
material on triggering events, with a refresh log in the model
file — and the head refuses to circulate a model whose refresh
log shows year-old figures against a materially-changed
environment.

**The strawman-counter-factual pattern.** The head models the
ROI figure against a no-function counter-factual the enterprise
would never plausibly have run — no AIMS scope, no control
library, no evidence surface, no incident response, no third-
party defence capability. The counter-factual is convenient
because it produces a very large positive ROI, but the audit
committee reads it as strawman authoring and discounts the
entire model. The remedy is a realistic counter-factual —
prior-state, peer-baseline, or regulated-minimum — named
explicitly with the reasoning and the alternative counter-
factuals' ROI figures reported alongside.

## Summary

- The head authors the ROI model on a middle path between
  narrative-only defence and over-claimed advocacy — the
  defensible number the CFO's finance team can re-run against
  the same inputs and reach substantively the same conclusion,
  and that the audit committee reads for discipline as much as
  for number.
- The model has four axes chosen to be non-overlapping:
  avoided-loss (regulator-fine avoidance, incident-cost
  avoidance, third-party-defence cost avoidance), deal-
  enablement (trust-margin at deal scope, regulator-approval-
  speed premium, tender-eligibility uplift, enterprise-
  customer AI-trust requirements coverage), trust-margin
  (customer-retention delta, employee-attraction delta), and
  transformation (spreadsheet-elimination FTE-recovery).
- Every axis rests on the same modelling discipline: inputs
  cited to primary source with needs-research markers where
  external benchmark verification is pending; probabilities
  expressed as named ranges with reasoning, not as points;
  sensitivity analysis built in at low-central-high; counter-
  factual baseline named explicitly with the alternatives'
  ROI figures reported alongside; attribution discipline in
  three tiers — sole cause, material contributor, contributing
  signal — with the tier named per figure.
- Avoided-loss is modelled per regulator (EU AI Act Article 99,
  GDPR Article 83, sector-specific), per incident class (data-
  breach-adjacent, discrimination-litigation-adjacent, mis-
  decisioning-adjacent), and per third-party engagement type
  (regulator inquiry, regulator investigation, customer
  contractual dispute, class-action discovery, partner-driven
  AIMS verification), with the fine ceilings and benchmarks
  carrying needs-research markers.
- Deal-enablement is sourced from the enterprise's own deal
  registry, cross-referenced with the sales function's own
  attribution, and every sub-line carries a sales-side
  ratifier with a ratification date; figures without
  ratification are removed or reduced.
- Trust-margin is modelled cautiously — retention delta versus
  counter-factual, employee-attraction delta versus counter-
  factual — sourced to the enterprise's own CRM and HR data
  with external benchmarks used only as sanity checks; the
  head reports trust-margin at contributing-signal tier unless
  specific evidence supports a higher tier.
- Transformation FTE-recovery is the most tractable axis to
  model and the one where over-claim is most tempting; the
  head models the recovered hours from the level-15 analysts'
  own time capture, names the redirection destination for
  every recovered hour category, and composes carefully with
  the headcount P&L so no double-counting occurs.
- The four axes compose into a single reported ROI with the
  axis breakdown directly under the composed number, payback
  period reported per axis and combined, and sensitivity band
  across the whole model.
- The audit committee reads the model for discipline —
  probability derivation, sensitivity workings, sales-side
  ratification, counter-factual choice, transformation
  redirection evidence — and the head prepares an answer for
  each anticipated audit-committee question before the pack is
  circulated.
- The ROI model is refreshed annually at budget-cycle
  preparation and materially on triggering events (new
  regulation, sector incident, customer or investor
  conversation, completed transformation programme); it is
  not refreshed quarterly, and the refresh log lives in the
  model file itself.
- Six anti-patterns the head refuses: the single-number ROI
  pattern, the over-claimed avoided-fine pattern, the
  unattributed deal-enablement pattern, the all-transformation-
  FTE-recovery-is-credit pattern, the point-estimate no-
  sensitivity pattern, and the refresh-neglect pattern — plus
  the strawman-counter-factual pattern the head is equally
  disciplined against.
- Chapter
  [08](08-caio-and-cxo-budget-envelope-coordination.md) closes
  the module with the coordination discipline that carries the
  ROI figure into the enterprise-wide AI budget envelope
  conversation with the CAIO where one exists and the CIO /
  CTO / CDO / CFO directly where one does not.
