# exercise-05: Difficult-Conversation Rehearsal

**Estimated effort:** 2 hours

## Objective

Rehearse the difficult-conversation pattern from chapter 06 for one
of the four scenario types — appetite breach, deteriorating
regulator engagement, Article 73 (or sector-equivalent) serious-
incident investigation, or stop-shipping decision — for the
enterprise you have been working against in exercises 01-04. The
deliverable is a rehearsed briefing that a peer or coach could
watch you deliver as if to the audit-committee chair, followed by
adversarial follow-up questions the peer runs against you.

This is not a written exercise dressed up as rehearsal. The pattern
in chapter 06 is muscle-memory work. The deliverable includes both
the drafted briefing artefacts *and* a debrief note capturing what
happened when you delivered them out loud and were challenged.

## Prerequisites

- Chapter [06-difficult-conversation-rehearsal](../06-difficult-conversation-rehearsal.md)
  read and internalised.
- Chapters [01](../01-board-report-shape.md),
  [02](../02-audit-committee-deep-dive-shape.md), and
  [03](../03-risk-committee-and-tech-committee-shape.md) —
  the pack shapes the conversation refers into.
- Exercises 01-04 in draft form. The rehearsal uses one of the
  packs and a plausible late-breaking event that would surface
  a difficult conversation.
- A peer, coach, or (ideally) an experienced committee member,
  CxO, or head-of-role willing to spend 45 minutes running the
  challenge portion. If no live peer is available, use a
  recorded self-run with a written follow-up-question list you
  do not read in advance.

## Steps

1. **Pick the scenario.** Choose one of the four scenario types
   from chapter 06 (appetite breach, deteriorating regulator
   engagement, Article 73 or sector-equivalent serious-incident
   investigation, stop-shipping decision). Compose the scenario
   in specific terms for the enterprise from exercises 01-04 —
   the specific system, the specific regulator or appetite
   statement, the specific event, the specific dates. The
   scenario is written down before the rehearsal so it does not
   drift under challenge.
2. **Draft the five-move opening.** Author the five moves from
   chapter 06 — name the fact in one sentence, locate
   ownership, state what has been and is being done, state the
   ask, hold-under-challenge preparation. The opening is at
   most one and a half pages typed. Every sentence in the first
   three moves is authored to survive being read back in a
   transcript.
3. **Author the follow-up-question bank.** Draft a list of at
   least twelve adversarial follow-up questions a committee
   member would plausibly ask against your five-move opening.
   Use the follow-up categories from chapter 06 for your chosen
   scenario as a seed; extend with questions specific to your
   enterprise. Give the list to the peer running the challenge
   *without* the head's prepared answers.
4. **Deliver the opening out loud.** Deliver the five-move
   opening to the peer or coach (or to a recording device) as
   if the audit-committee chair is the audience. Time it — the
   opening should land in under three minutes. If it runs
   longer, it is over-narrated.
5. **Run the challenge round.** The peer asks questions from
   the bank in random order for 20 to 30 minutes. The head
   answers under chapter 06 discipline: answer the specific
   question, use the questioner's language, admit
   don't-know where honest, cite evidence, honour escalation
   invitations. The session is recorded (with peer consent) so
   the head can review.
6. **Debrief note.** After the session, write a one-page
   debrief. What moves held under pressure; where the head
   fell into over-narration, under-ownership, or ambiguous-
   next-step; which follow-up questions surfaced material
   the head had not thought about; what will be different
   next rehearsal. The debrief is candid — the value of the
   exercise is what the head learns, and self-critique that
   pulls its punches is self-critique that does not improve
   the next delivery.
7. **After-meeting artefacts.** Draft (as if the meeting had
   actually happened) the minute item, the same-day follow-up
   commitments circulation, and the private debrief note the
   head would send the audit-committee chair. Chapter 06 names
   these as the after-meeting discipline; drafting them in the
   rehearsal builds the reflex.

## Deliverables

- `scenario.md` — the specific scenario written down before
  rehearsal, including system, regulator or appetite reference,
  event, and dates.
- `five-move-opening.md` — the authored opening at chapter 06
  depth.
- `follow-up-question-bank.md` — the list of adversarial
  follow-up questions.
- `challenge-round-recording` (audio or video, or a full
  transcript) — the recorded session, if consent allows. If
  not, a detailed session-log.
- `debrief-note.md` — the one-page candid debrief.
- `after-meeting-artefacts.md` — the minute item, follow-up
  commitments circulation, and private-debrief-to-chair note.

## Acceptance criteria

- The scenario is specific — a specific system, a specific
  regulator or appetite statement, a specific event, specific
  dates. Generic scenarios ("an incident happened") do not
  test the pattern.
- The five-move opening runs in under three minutes when
  delivered out loud. Openings that run longer are over-
  narrated.
- The follow-up-question bank contains at least twelve
  questions. Fewer means the challenge round does not surface
  the head's rough edges.
- The challenge round runs 20 to 30 minutes and is recorded or
  transcribed in enough detail that the debrief is grounded in
  what happened, not what the head remembers happening.
- The debrief note names at least two specific
  moves-that-broke, with what will be different next
  rehearsal. Debriefs that report "everything went well" have
  under-critiqued.
- The after-meeting artefacts are drafted at the depth chapter
  06 requires — minute item captures fact, ownership, and ask
  cleanly; follow-ups are named same-day; the private note to
  the chair reflects any softening or hardening detected in
  the room.

## Stretch goals

- Rehearse a second scenario type. The four scenario types
  (appetite breach, deteriorating regulator engagement,
  serious-incident investigation, stop-shipping decision)
  exercise different follow-up categories; two rehearsals
  across two types exercises the pattern more broadly than
  one.
- Run the rehearsal with two peers — one asking the committee-
  member questions, one observing and taking notes on the
  head's language patterns (diminishers, aggrandisers,
  attribution slippage). Peer-observed language notes are one
  of the fastest ways to improve.
- Rehearse the same scenario twice, spaced by 48 hours, with
  the debrief from the first run incorporated. Notice what
  transferred and what did not.
- Draft the version of the five-move opening the head would
  deliver *if the fact had been known for a week* versus *if
  the fact broke this morning*. The pattern is the same; the
  language discipline flexes with the freshness. Rehearse
  both.

## Notes and starter guidance

- The single most common failure mode of a first rehearsal is
  the opening balloons to seven or eight minutes because the
  head cannot resist providing context first. Time the opening.
  Cut context out of moves 1-3; if context matters, it can
  come out under challenge in move 5.
- The second most common failure is under-ownership. The head
  says "we are looking into it" instead of naming who at the
  enterprise is accountable. If the peer running the challenge
  can walk out of the session and not know who owns what, the
  ownership move failed.
- The value of the rehearsal is not in reaching a perfect
  opening. The value is in surfacing the specific language
  reflexes the head has under stress (over-narration when
  uncomfortable, diminishing when defensive, hedging when the
  facts are unresolved). Naming those reflexes in the debrief
  is where the improvement compounds.
- Do not rehearse into a mirror. Rehearse to a human. The
  pattern is a *human interaction* discipline; a mirror does
  not surface the specific ways the head's language changes
  when someone is disagreeing with them in the moment.
