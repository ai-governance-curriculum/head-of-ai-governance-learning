# The Difficult-Conversation Pattern

## Motivation

Most board and audit-committee meetings are routine. The head walks
the committee through the pack, answers clarifying questions, notes
decisions, and leaves. The difficult conversations are the ones the
head is remembered for — telling the board that the appetite has
been breached, that a regulator engagement is deteriorating, that a
serious-incident investigation is under Article 73 of the EU AI Act,
that a stop-shipping decision has been taken and the revenue impact
is material.

Handling these badly ends careers. Handling them well *makes* the
head's credibility with the committee, and that credibility is what
the head spends over the following quarters when the merely-routine
questions get harder.

This chapter is the pattern for the difficult conversation — the
shape the head brings to the room, the discipline the head holds
under challenge, and the language the head does and does not use.
It is not a script. Scripts fail on contact with the committee.
The pattern is a shape.

## Core concept: the room wants facts, ownership, and next step — in that order

Under stress, committees do not want reassurance. They want to know:

- What happened, factually, in language they can restate to
  someone else.
- Who owns it — who at the enterprise is accountable for what
  has been done and what is being done.
- What the next step is — the specific action or decision the
  committee is being asked to take, or to note as taken.

The head's job in the difficult conversation is to deliver those
three, in that order, without ornamentation. The room will fill in
alarm, blame, and analysis on its own; the head does not need to
supply any of them.

The failure modes are three, and the head must know them:

- **Over-narration** — burying the fact in analysis, context,
  history, or explanation of what the program was trying to do.
  The committee cannot hear the fact through the narration.
- **Under-ownership** — describing what "the industry" or "the
  vendor" or "the regulator" did without saying what *this
  enterprise* did or will do. The committee experiences the head
  as passive.
- **Ambiguous next step** — leaving the committee unclear on what
  is being asked. Committees under stress will not chase
  ambiguity; they will act on the ambiguity's worst reading.

The pattern below is designed to fail-safe against all three.

## The five-move pattern

Every difficult conversation follows the same five moves. The exact
wording is the head's; the moves are non-negotiable.

### Move 1 — name the fact in one sentence

The head opens with a single declarative sentence stating what
happened. No preface, no context, no history.

Examples of the move done correctly:

- *"The Q3 portfolio exceeded the fairness-risk appetite ratified
  by this committee in February, by two of the five appetite
  bands, in the customer-underwriting cluster."*
- *"On September 14, an AI system deployed in our EU market
  produced an output that satisfies the serious-incident
  definition in Article 3(49) of the AI Act, and we have submitted
  the incident report to the relevant national authority within
  the required timeline."*
- *"The Financial Conduct Authority opened a supervisory review of
  our AI use in credit decisioning on October 3, requesting the
  documented response by November 3."*
- *"At 06:00 UTC yesterday, the release-assurance function halted
  new deployments of our customer-facing generative model pending
  investigation of a Q3 evaluation-suite regression."*

Notice what these do and do not do. They do not editorialise. They
do not sequence into context. They *state the fact* the way a
newspaper's first sentence states the fact — subject, verb, object,
timeframe.

### Move 2 — locate ownership

The second sentence names who at the enterprise is accountable and
what the accountability covers. Not "we are" — someone specific.

- *"Accountability for the appetite breach sits with me as the
  head of AI governance, jointly with the head of the customer-
  underwriting portfolio and with the CRO on the appetite
  framework itself."*
- *"The General Counsel is the enterprise's counterpart to the
  national authority; the CISO is running the technical
  investigation; I am running the AI Governance Council response."*
- *"The head of retail credit is the enterprise's counterpart to
  the FCA; I am the second-line lead on the response; the CRO is
  the CEO's delegate on the engagement."*
- *"The head of release assurance triggered the halt under a
  pre-committed stop-shipping trigger; the product owner and the
  head of the AI platform team are running the investigation; I
  am the second-line lead."*

Ownership language is *precise*. "The company is looking into it"
is not ownership. "The relevant function is on it" is not
ownership. Names of roles are ownership. Where a role does not
exist yet or is not filled, the head names the person acting into
the role and the plan to fill it.

### Move 3 — state what has been done and what is being done

Concise past-and-present. Not future speculation. Not
recommendations. What has actually happened and what is actually
in flight, in the tense that fits.

- *"Since detection on September 30, we have paused new
  applications in the affected cluster, notified the CRO and the
  audit-committee chair, launched a root-cause review, and started
  the appetite-recalibration conversation for the December risk
  committee."*
- *"We containment-triaged the incident within four hours of
  detection, notified the DPA within 24 hours as required, filed
  the AI Act serious-incident report on the specified template
  within the statutory window, and engaged external counsel on
  the enforcement posture."*
- *"We have acknowledged the FCA's letter within the required
  timeframe, appointed external counsel, initiated the document
  collection, and confirmed the response-team composition to the
  CEO."*
- *"We halted deployments, retained the last known good version
  in production, opened an incident channel, notified the
  affected business owners of the deployment pause, and initiated
  the evaluation-suite root cause."*

The committee is not asking whether the response is perfect. The
committee is asking whether *something is happening* and whether
that something is being run by someone accountable. The move
answers both.

### Move 4 — state the ask

The specific decision the committee is being asked to take, or the
information the committee is being asked to note as taken. Phrased
so the minute-writer can capture it verbatim.

- *"I am asking the committee to note the breach, to endorse the
  containment approach, and to ratify the recalibration of the
  fairness-risk appetite band that will be tabled at the December
  risk committee."*
- *"I am asking the committee to note the incident and the
  reporting posture, and to ratify the engagement of external
  counsel at the fee level in the pre-read for the enforcement
  response."*
- *"I am asking the committee to note the FCA engagement and to
  approve the delegation of ongoing correspondence to the head of
  retail credit and the General Counsel, with monthly update to
  this committee until closure."*
- *"I am asking the committee to note the deployment halt, to
  endorse the investigation timeline, and to approve the
  communication to the CEO and CFO on the expected revenue impact
  during the halt."*

If the head is asking for nothing — if the situation is under
control and the update is informational — the head says so:
*"I am asking the committee to note the position; no committee
decision is requested at this time."* Silence about the ask is
the ambiguity failure mode; explicit "no ask" is not.

### Move 5 — hold under challenge

The committee will ask. The pattern for holding under challenge is:

- **Answer the question that was asked**. Not the question the
  head wishes had been asked. Not a longer version of the question
  that lets the head restate ownership or context. The specific
  question.
- **Use the committee's language back to them**. If the committee
  member asks about "the breach," the head does not correct to
  "the deviation" or "the excursion." The head accepts the
  framing that is offered and answers within it.
- **If the head does not know, the head says so**. "I do not
  know" is a survivable answer. "I do not know, and I will have
  the answer to you within X" is a strong answer. Fabrication is
  career-ending.
- **If a fact is contested, the head cites the source**. Not "we
  believe" — the log entry, the finding number, the correspondence
  reference. Contest a fact by producing evidence.
- **Escalation invitations are honoured**. If a committee member
  asks whether the CEO knows, the head says whether the CEO
  knows. If a committee member asks whether external counsel has
  been engaged, the head answers directly. Deflection is read as
  concealment.

## Four rehearsed scenarios

The head rehearses (out loud, with a peer or a coach) four scenario
types so the pattern is muscle memory when the meeting comes:

### Scenario A — appetite breach

*"The Q# portfolio exceeded the [category] risk appetite that this
committee ratified in [meeting date]. The breach is in [scope] and
is quantified as [magnitude]."*

The follow-up the committee will ask: has this happened before, is
the appetite the wrong appetite or is the operation the wrong
operation, what does recalibration look like, what is the revenue
impact of containment. The head rehearses each answer.

### Scenario B — deteriorating regulator engagement

*"The [regulator]'s engagement with the enterprise on [scope] has
moved from [prior status] to [current status]. The trigger was
[event]."*

The follow-up the committee will ask: what is the likely
enforcement outcome, what is the disclosure implication for the
listing, who is running the response, has external counsel been
engaged, what is the CEO's involvement. The head rehearses each.

### Scenario C — Article 73 serious-incident investigation

*"An AI system in [scope] produced [factual outcome] on [date].
The event satisfies the serious-incident definition in
Article 3(49) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 [or the sector-specific
equivalent for the enterprise's regime]. The report was filed with
[authority] on [date] within the statutory window."*

The follow-up the committee will ask: was the system's evaluation
suite adequate, was the vendor at fault, what is the exposure to
downstream users, what is the exposure to the enterprise's other
AI Act obligations, what does remediation look like. The head
rehearses each.

### Scenario D — stop-shipping decision

*"On [date], the [release-assurance function / AI governance
council / head] exercised the pre-committed stop-shipping trigger
under [named policy / capability tier framework], halting
[deployment scope]. The trigger fired on [evaluation result /
incident / regulator action]."*

The follow-up the committee will ask: what is the revenue impact,
how long until reinstatement, are competitors affected the same
way, what does the disclosure look like, who is coordinating with
customers. The head rehearses each.

## Language discipline

Four specific language habits the head holds:

- **Present the fact in a way that survives quotation**. Every
  sentence the head speaks in the room could end up in a
  transcript, a minute, a leaked email, or a deposition. If the
  head would not stand by the sentence when read back three years
  later, the head does not say the sentence.
- **Do not diminish**. "Just a minor," "in reality it was fine,"
  "the coverage was overwrought" — every diminisher is read by the
  committee as defensiveness and by a plaintiff's counsel as
  admission-adjacent. State the fact and let it land.
- **Do not aggrandise**. Equally, "unprecedented," "existential,"
  "catastrophic" — the head does not paint in the room. The
  committee will do that if it is warranted.
- **Attribute where attribution is fair**. If a vendor's action
  caused an incident, the head says so and cites the evidence. If
  the enterprise's own control gap caused an incident, the head
  says so equally. Selective attribution is read as spin.

## After the meeting

The difficult conversation continues after the meeting closes:

- The head writes the minute for the item personally (or reviews
  the secretariat draft closely) to ensure the fact, the
  ownership, and the ask are captured cleanly.
- The head circulates the follow-up commitments the same day, so
  the committee sees the ownership carrying forward.
- The head files the pack section, the addendum, and the
  committee's decision under document control on the standing
  retention path.
- The head debriefs privately with the audit-committee chair (or
  the risk-committee chair, or the board chair — whichever is
  the natural adjacency) so any softening or hardening of
  position the head detected in the room can be understood
  before the next cycle.

## Summary

- The difficult conversation follows a five-move pattern: name the
  fact in one sentence, locate ownership, state what has been
  and is being done, state the ask, hold under challenge.
- Three failure modes to fail-safe against: over-narration,
  under-ownership, ambiguous next step. The pattern is designed
  around each.
- Four rehearsed scenarios — appetite breach, deteriorating
  regulator engagement, Article 73 (or equivalent) serious-
  incident investigation, stop-shipping decision. The head
  rehearses each out loud so the pattern is muscle memory.
- Language discipline: survive quotation, do not diminish, do
  not aggrandise, attribute fairly.
- After the meeting: minute captured cleanly, follow-ups
  circulated same day, private debrief with the natural committee
  chair.
