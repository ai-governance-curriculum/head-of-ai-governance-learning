# Career Paths *Out Of* the Head of AI Governance Role

## Motivation

The head-of-role tenure is not the terminus. A five-year head has
built a substrate — cross-CxO peer relationships, board-committee
standing, regulator counterparts by name, standards-body seats,
community-leadership commitments, a documented succession slate —
that opens onward moves the level-50 architect could not have
attempted. Chapter [[02-career-paths-into-role]] mapped the seven
routes *into* the seat. This chapter maps the routes *out*.

The chapter serves three audiences at once:

- **The sitting head who is planning their own next step.** The
  head who leaves without a plan is a head whose exit reads as
  reactive rather than authored. The routes out are heads' own
  strategic terrain, and knowing which routes their tenure has
  opened is how the head negotiates the exit at the point they
  choose rather than at the point pressure produces it.
- **The board or CEO who is planning for the head's departure.**
  Some routes out are elevations within the enterprise (Chief AI
  Officer, Chief Risk Officer, Chief Compliance Officer). Some are
  exits (industry-body chair, non-executive director seat, public-
  sector CAIO). The enterprise's succession preparedness depends on
  knowing which route the sitting head is on.
- **The successor slate.** Where the head's next role is a
  cross-functional promotion at the same enterprise, the successor
  inherits both the seat and the peer-CxO relationship the head is
  now on the other side of. Naming the pattern in advance turns a
  potentially disruptive transition into a rehearsed one.

The chapter is deliberately paired with [[02-career-paths-into-role]]
so that a head reading the pair reads their own career arc in both
directions: which of the seven routes brought them here, and which
of the five routes lies ahead. The credentials portfolio
([[01-certifications-portfolio]]) and community-leadership scope
([[04-community-leadership-scope]]) turn out to matter as much for
the routes *out* as for the seat itself — the CAIO promotion the
head is offered often turns on a community-leadership commitment the
head has held for years, and the non-executive-director seat the
head is invited into often turns on a specific credential and a
specific published body of work.

## The five paths — an overview

Five distinguishable routes out, in rough order of frequency across
the observed placements:

1. **Head of AI Governance → Chief AI Officer (level 70).** The
   scope-expanding elevation within the enterprise. The head takes
   ownership of the AI *strategy* and *value* mandate in addition
   to the *risk and governance* mandate, and reports directly to
   the CEO with a much broader remit.
2. **Head of AI Governance → Chief Risk Officer / Chief Compliance
   Officer / Chief Privacy Officer.** The cross-functional
   elevation. The head takes the broader CxO seat whose AI
   dimension the head already substantively owned, and inherits
   the non-AI portions of that CxO's remit as well.
3. **Head of AI Governance → Non-Executive Director / Board seat
   (AI-governance NED).** The board-portfolio route. The head
   accumulates non-executive-director seats at other enterprises
   whose boards are looking for AI-governance depth on their risk
   or audit committees, and often carries a portfolio of two-to-
   four seats as their principal professional occupation.
4. **Head of AI Governance → Public-sector CAIO under OMB M-25-21
   (or equivalent).** The public-service route. The head moves
   from an enterprise-side seat into a federal, state, or
   international-body CAIO designation, taking on public
   accountability for an agency's AI use.
5. **Head of AI Governance → Industry-body chair or academic-
   affiliation.** The institutional-leadership route. The head
   moves into a full-time or nearly-full-time role chairing an
   industry body (Frontier Model Forum, Partnership on AI, IAPP
   AIGP body-of-knowledge chair) or taking an academic affiliation
   (endowed chair, executive-in-residence, distinguished fellow at
   a policy institute).

The rest of the chapter unpacks each path — what the sitting head's
tenure has to have looked like to qualify, the specific move
mechanics and negotiation posture, the credential and community-
leadership prerequisites, and the anti-pattern the head avoids on
each path.

## Path 1 — Head of AI Governance → Chief AI Officer (level 70)

### When the path opens

The Chief AI Officer role is not universal. Some enterprises operate
without one, delegating the AI strategy and value mandate to the CIO,
the CTO, the CDO, or a Chief Digital Officer. Where the CAIO role
opens up, three conditions typically co-occur:

- **AI has become material to enterprise value creation.** The
  enterprise's AI portfolio has grown from a defensive risk-and-
  compliance surface into an offensive value surface — foundation-
  model-based products the enterprise sells, AI-enabled operational
  efficiencies at scale, AI-derived competitive positioning. When
  AI value creation is material, the CEO wants a single accountable
  executive.
- **The AI risk surface has stabilised.** The AIMS runs at cadence,
  the audit committee's questions are steady-state, the regulator
  engagements are known and worked. The risk-and-governance
  discipline has been operationalised to a degree that the head-of-
  AI-governance can plausibly delegate portions of it and expand
  scope.
- **The enterprise's CxO composition has room.** The CEO reads the
  existing CTO / CIO / CDO / CDO composition and identifies a
  discrete CAIO seat that does not duplicate an existing peer. In
  many enterprises the CAIO seat consolidates AI-adjacent portions
  of the CTO, CIO, and CDO remits.

The sitting head who is well positioned for the CAIO elevation
holds:

- Standing peer relationships with the CTO, CIO, CDO, and CFO that
  are already substantive on AI value questions ([[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]
  chapters 01 and 02 established the technical-CxO relationships;
  chapter 03 established the CFO relationship). The head who has
  only substantive relationships with the CRO, CCO, GC, and CPO is
  well positioned for path 2, not path 1.
- Published external artefacts that the market reads as AI-strategy
  rather than only AI-governance. A head whose thought-leadership
  cadence ([[05-thought-leadership-cadence]]) has been ISO/IEC
  42001 and AI Act depth alone reads as a specialist; a head who
  has authored a mixed portfolio including public writing on
  foundation-model adoption strategy and AI product economics reads
  as a strategist.
- ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer, the AIGP baseline, and at least
  one governance-and-strategy credential (CGEIT) or an MBA-adjacent
  credential the CEO reads as strategic vocabulary. The pure
  compliance credential stack is a signal of specialism the CAIO
  posting sometimes wants to move past.

### The move mechanics

The CAIO elevation is typically an internal promotion negotiated
directly with the CEO. External-market CAIO postings do exist but
are still rare; the more common pattern is the sitting head being
elevated after a period during which they have taken on progressively
larger scope — the head who has been serving as *acting* head of AI
strategy, or the head who has been chairing an enterprise AI Council
that has effectively become the CAIO decision body.

The negotiation posture:

- **Scope specification.** The head negotiates the exact scope of
  the CAIO seat — which of the AI-strategy, AI-product, AI-
  engineering, and AI-risk mandates report in, and which stay with
  peer CxOs. Ambiguous scope in the CAIO seat is the single biggest
  first-year failure mode; the head who takes the promotion without
  a scope agreement takes ownership of the ambiguity.
- **Successor placement.** The head does not vacate the AI-
  governance seat while the CAIO transition is in progress. The
  successor slate ([[02-career-paths-into-role]] closing section)
  is now activated — the sitting head has thirty to ninety days to
  land the internal or external successor, and the CAIO promotion
  is not effective until the successor is confirmed.
- **Reporting-line documentation.** The CAIO reports to the CEO
  (not to the CIO or the COO). Where the CAIO does not report to
  the CEO, the seat is a scope-expanded head-of-role, not a Chief
  officer. The head who accepts a CAIO title without CEO-reporting
  has accepted something the market and the enterprise both read
  as less than the title suggests.

### The prerequisite: a stable AI-governance function

The head who has not built a function ([[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]])
that can outlast their departure is a head whose CAIO elevation
strands the enterprise's AI-governance discipline. The head who has
built the function is a head whose CAIO scope now includes strategy
and value alongside the risk-and-governance discipline they have
already stabilised. The successor's readiness is a prerequisite for
the head's own elevation; treat it that way.

### The anti-pattern

Accepting a CAIO title that carries the strategy mandate but strips
the risk mandate away. A CAIO seat that has been designed to
"unlock" the enterprise's AI product without carrying accountability
for the risk-and-compliance surface is a seat whose incentives are
one-sided. Twelve months later the CAIO is fronting a product
incident whose risk framing has been picked up by a CRO or CCO
peer who was not consulted at design time. The head who accepts
this seat has traded a functional accountability for a title.

The remedy: declining the CAIO title unless the risk-and-governance
mandate is preserved inside the seat, or unless the peer CRO / CCO
seat has been strengthened in a way the sitting head and the incoming
peer have both negotiated. The difficult-career-conversation chapter
([[06-difficult-career-conversation-rehearsal]]) rehearses this
specifically.

## Path 2 — Head of AI Governance → Chief Risk Officer / Chief Compliance Officer / Chief Privacy Officer

### When the path opens

The cross-functional elevation opens when the sitting head's
performance in the AI-adjacent portion of a peer CxO's seat has
convinced the CEO or the board that the head is the right candidate
to succeed that peer. Three common variants:

- **CRO elevation.** The head whose reporting line runs through the
  CRO, whose ISACA CRISC / CGEIT credentials sit alongside AIGP,
  and whose second-line integration with the enterprise risk
  framework has been consistently strong is a candidate to succeed
  the CRO on their retirement or elevation. The AI mandate remains
  in the head's portfolio in the CRO seat; the head's successor as
  head of AI governance now reports to them.
- **CCO elevation.** The head whose compliance-programme design
  ([[mod-102-running-the-program]]) has been read as a template by
  the enterprise's non-AI compliance functions, and whose regulator
  engagements have covered instruments beyond AI (privacy, cyber,
  sector-specific), is a candidate to succeed the CCO on their
  departure.
- **CPO elevation.** The head whose privacy adjacency
  ([[01-certifications-portfolio]] privacy trio) is complete and
  whose AI-governance work has substantially interfaced with the
  privacy programme is a candidate to succeed the CPO. Common where
  the enterprise's operating structure has AI-governance and
  privacy peered under the same reporting line and the CPO is
  retiring.

### The move mechanics

The cross-functional elevation is an internal promotion negotiated
with the CEO and the outgoing peer. The negotiation turns on three
questions:

- **Does the AI mandate come along?** The default is that it does —
  the incoming CRO / CCO / CPO now owns AI governance as one of
  the risk / compliance / privacy substreams. Where the enterprise
  wants a distinct head of AI governance to continue reporting to
  the elevated head, that arrangement is negotiated up front.
- **How is the outgoing peer's remaining team integrated?** The
  head who has been running a function of six-to-nine now inherits
  a function of thirty-to-sixty (in the CCO / CPO case) or three-
  hundred-plus (in the CRO case at a bank). The head's first-year
  people-leadership priority is the integration of the outgoing
  peer's leadership team, not the running of the AI-governance
  work they were doing before.
- **What is the accountable posture on the non-AI remit?** The
  CRO seat carries market risk, credit risk, operational risk,
  liquidity risk (at a bank); the CCO seat carries anti-money-
  laundering, sanctions, anti-corruption, sector-conduct-risk; the
  CPO seat carries privacy law across every enterprise data
  processing activity, not only AI-adjacent. The head who accepts
  the elevation without depth on the non-AI remit is a head who
  will be caught out on the non-AI portions of the audit-committee
  questions in the first six months.

### The prerequisite: non-AI-adjacent professional depth

The head who accepted only AI-specific credentials and did none of
the non-AI adjacencies is a head with a narrow substrate for the
cross-functional elevation. The head whose credential portfolio
([[01-certifications-portfolio]]) deliberately included the CRO-,
CCO-, or CPO-relevant adjacencies (CRISC / CGEIT / CISM for the CRO
seat; ISACA CGEIT + IIA CIA for the CCO seat; CIPP + CIPM + CIPT
for the CPO seat) has already prepared the substrate. The credential
sequencing decision in the first chapter was, among other things,
about this — which onward path the head is preserving as an option.

### The anti-pattern

Accepting the elevation and running the seat as though it were an
AI seat. The head who has just been elevated to CRO now runs the
market-risk and credit-risk portions of the CRO seat as a distant
adjacency and treats the AI portion as the substantive work.
Twelve months later the audit committee reads the CRO as a
specialist in the wrong specialism, and the head loses standing on
the seat they just accepted. The remedy: deliberate onboarding into
the non-AI portions of the new seat, at the depth [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]'s
first-year discipline enforced on the head's first AI-governance
tenure.

## Path 3 — Head of AI Governance → Non-Executive Director / Board Seat

### When the path opens

The non-executive-director seat (in the US, an outside director on
a board of directors; in the UK and EU, a non-executive director;
in Australia, a non-executive director; in Canada, an independent
director) has become a mainstream onward path for the sitting head
who has developed a market profile. The typical trigger points:

- **A public-company board of directors is refreshing its risk or
  audit committee** and has explicitly identified AI-governance
  expertise as a needed skill on its skills matrix. Where the
  sitting head has been publicly visible through
  [[05-thought-leadership-cadence]] and community-leadership
  commitments ([[04-community-leadership-scope]]), they are a
  named candidate.
- **A private-company board or a venture-backed board** wants AI-
  governance depth as it prepares for a funding round, a
  regulatory expansion, or an IPO. These seats are often
  compensated in cash or in equity and are less demanding of
  regulatory-jurisdiction domicile than public-company seats.
- **A regulated non-profit board** (a hospital-system board, a
  university board of trustees, a foundation board) wants AI-
  governance depth as their institution's AI use grows. These
  seats carry public-service compensation levels but confer
  substantial standing.

The path is often *concurrent* with a sitting head's employment —
the head takes one or two board seats while still serving as head
of role, subject to their employer's outside-board policy. Where
the head then transitions to a board-portfolio-only career, the
concurrent seats become the substrate the transition is built on.

### The move mechanics

The NED seat is offered through one of three channels:

- **A board-search firm** (Egon Zehnder, Spencer Stuart, Heidrick &
  Struggles, Russell Reynolds) reaches out because a client's board
  is refreshing. The search firm is asking for a candidate
  interview, not offering the seat directly.
- **A personal introduction** from a peer director, an incumbent
  audit-committee chair, or the CEO of the enterprise seeking the
  seat. This is the most common path for the sitting head with an
  established peer network.
- **A regulator-referred seat.** At regulated enterprises
  (financial-services, health-system, critical-infrastructure), the
  regulator's supervisory relationship with the board has produced
  a specific request that the board add AI-governance expertise;
  the regulator sometimes refers specific candidates. This is
  common where the head has been a regulator counterparty over
  several years.

The negotiation posture centres on three items:

- **Time commitment.** A public-company NED seat with audit-
  committee or risk-committee membership is typically 200-400
  hours per year. A private-company or non-profit seat is
  typically 80-200. The head negotiating the seat quantifies the
  hours against their existing commitments before accepting.
- **Compensation.** Public-company NED compensation is disclosed
  in the enterprise's proxy statement; the head reads the peer
  benchmark before accepting. Private-company seats are often
  paid in a combination of cash retainer, meeting fees, and
  equity or profits interests; the equity portion warrants legal
  and tax review.
- **Committee assignment.** The head negotiates the audit-
  committee or risk-committee seat as part of the acceptance
  rather than accepting a general-director seat and hoping the
  committee assignment materialises later. The AI-governance skill
  is what qualifies the head; the committee seat is where it is
  used.

### The prerequisite: independence and time

The board seat carries a fiduciary duty ([[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]]
chapter 07 discussed the sitting head's interaction with the audit
committee's fiduciary duty; the NED seat now puts the head *on the
other side* of that duty). The head who has accepted an NED seat
does not represent their employer's interests on the client
enterprise's board; the head owes their duty to the client
enterprise's shareholders (or, for non-profits, to the mission).

The head negotiating an NED seat verifies:

- **Their employer's outside-board policy permits the specific
  seat.** Most enterprise policies require CEO or board-chair
  approval for each seat and require disclosure of compensation.
- **The seats do not overlap.** A head who sits on multiple boards
  in the same sector, or on boards of enterprises that are
  competitors or business counterparts of the head's employer,
  creates a set of conflicts the head's employer will not
  approve.
- **The head has the time.** The NED seat's time-commitment is
  additive to the head's day-role. A head who accepts two seats
  and has not renegotiated their day-role scope produces a set
  of half-executed commitments.

### The anti-pattern

Accepting board seats as a status marker without the discipline of
the seat itself. The head who accepts an NED seat, reads the board
pack for the first time in the taxi to the meeting, asks
uninformed questions, and misses the material technical judgement
that the seat was meant to bring is a head whose subsequent
seats do not materialise. The board community reads NED performance
carefully; a badly-executed first seat closes the second.

The remedy: treating the NED seat as a professional discipline that
carries preparation time equal to a chair's, not as a badge of
standing. The head who takes fewer seats and executes them well
builds the portfolio; the head who accepts every seat and executes
none well ends the portfolio at seat one.

## Path 4 — Head of AI Governance → Public-Sector CAIO under OMB M-25-21

### When the path opens

The federal CAIO designation under OMB M-25-21 (and its parallel
public-sector-equivalents in the UK, EU, Canada, and Singapore) is
a specific role classification. The path opens when a federal
agency (or a comparable public-sector body) advertises a Chief AI
Officer position and the sitting head is a plausible candidate. In
the US, this typically means the SES (Senior Executive Service) or
a Schedule C political-appointee designation, depending on the
agency and the seat.

Two triggers for the path:

- **The sitting head has substantial public-sector engagement
  history.** They have advised the OMB, NIST, an agency's own AI
  Governance Board, or a Federal CAIO Council working group. The
  agency knows the head by name and has read their published work.
  The path is a natural extension of established relationships.
- **The sitting head is in a career phase where public-service
  compensation is acceptable.** Federal CAIO compensation is
  capped by SES scales and is materially below enterprise-side
  head-of-role compensation. The head accepts the compensation
  differential because the public-service mandate is the substantive
  interest.

### The move mechanics

Federal-sector hiring runs on USAJobs and on agency-specific
processes. Political-appointee positions (Schedule C, non-career
SES) are appointed by the agency head or the White House and do
not run through the competitive-hiring process; career SES positions
run through a Qualifications Review Board process.

The candidate reads themselves against:

- **The M-25-21 CAIO responsibilities.** Designating an accountable
  AI Governance Board chair, executing high-impact minimum
  practices, running the AI Use Case Inventory, coordinating with
  the agency's IG, participating in the Federal CAIO Council. The
  sitting head who has been running the analogous enterprise-side
  discipline reads these responsibilities line-by-line and
  identifies gaps (IG relations, congressional-oversight interface,
  Freedom of Information Act response) that are public-sector-
  specific.
- **The security-clearance requirement.** Many CAIO seats require
  Top Secret or SCI clearance. The clearance process runs six-to-
  eighteen months and is a background-check exercise the candidate
  is honest about at candidacy time.
- **The post-employment restrictions.** Federal-service departure
  triggers post-employment restrictions (18 U.S.C. § 207) on
  representing former clients, on communicating with the former
  agency, and on lobbying. The candidate reads the restrictions
  against their expected onward career at candidacy time.

### The prerequisite: a public-service network

The head with no prior standing engagement in the public-sector
community (no NIST AIRC working-group participation, no Federal AI
Council conference attendance, no published NIST AI RMF
contributions) is a head whose federal-CAIO candidacy will be a
first-time introduction rather than an extension of a known
professional relationship. The path is best walked by the head
whose community-leadership scope ([[04-community-leadership-scope]])
has deliberately included the public-sector surface for years
before the specific seat opens.

### The anti-pattern

Treating the federal-CAIO seat as a resume-builder. The candidate
who takes the seat with the intention of returning to enterprise-
side head-of-role at a compensation premium after 24 months is a
candidate whose public-service colleagues read as extractive. The
federal-CAIO community is small; the pattern is noticed; onward
enterprise-side roles that would have valued the federal service
now discount it. The remedy: taking the seat only when the public-
service commitment is the interest, and treating the tenure as a
substantive engagement rather than a resume line.

## Path 5 — Head of AI Governance → Industry-Body Chair or Academic Affiliation

### When the path opens

The industry-body-chair and academic-affiliation paths are the
institutional-leadership variant of the exit. Where the sitting
head has spent years contributing to an industry body or engaging
with academic institutions, the path is a natural progression.

Industry-body chair seats:

- **Frontier Model Forum executive-director-level roles** for the
  head whose sector engagement has been at the frontier lab side.
- **Partnership on AI board-of-directors or program-director
  roles** for the head whose community engagement has spanned
  multi-stakeholder AI governance.
- **IAPP AIGP body-of-knowledge chair or working-group lead** for
  the head whose credential-community involvement has been
  substantive.
- **ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 US TAG chair, mirror-committee chair, or
  working-group convenor** for the head whose standards-body
  engagement has been operationally substantive.
- **CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 delegate roles** for the head with European
  standards-body engagement.

Academic affiliations:

- **Executive-in-residence, distinguished fellow, or endowed chair
  at a university's business school, law school, or policy
  institute** — Stanford HAI, MIT CSAIL, CMU CyLab, Oxford Institute
  for Ethics in AI, Cambridge Leverhulme Centre for the Future of
  Intelligence, Georgetown Center for Security and Emerging
  Technology, and the analogous institutions in each jurisdiction.
- **Adjunct-faculty positions** teaching AI governance in
  professional or executive-education programmes.
- **Non-resident-fellow positions** at a think tank (Brookings,
  RAND, CSIS, CFR in the US; Chatham House, RUSI in the UK; the
  European Council on Foreign Relations in the EU; the Centre for
  International Governance Innovation in Canada).

### The move mechanics

Industry-body chair and academic-affiliation roles are typically
offered rather than applied for. The offer follows years of visible
contribution — position papers, keynote-cadence participation,
working-group leadership, published research, teaching. The
sitting head who is thinking about this route reads their own
external portfolio and asks: which body or institution knows my
work well enough to invite me?

The compensation range is variable. Some industry-body seats are
volunteer or nominal-honorarium roles; some are full-time salaried;
some are consulting-style engagement fees. Academic affiliations
range from unpaid honorary titles through modest fellowship stipends
to full-salary endowed-chair positions. The head negotiating this
route understands the compensation reality up front and reads it
against their own financial position.

### The prerequisite: a decade of visible contribution

This path is the slowest to open. It is not open to the head who
has held the role for two years, however talented. It opens after
the head has spent a decade or more building a visible external
contribution portfolio — the position papers, the standards-body
work, the published academic articles, the reference community
contributions. The head who begins the community-leadership work
in year one of head-of-role tenure and continues consistently is
the head to whom this path opens at year eight or year ten.

### The anti-pattern

Trying to accelerate the path by taking a "big" community role
before the contribution substrate is there. The sitting head who
accepts a Frontier Model Forum chair position after six months
of engagement, or an endowed chair at a university where they have
never taught, produces a mismatched appointment that reads as a
credentialling exercise. The community reads it; the invitation
does not extend to the next round. The remedy: playing the long
game. Consistent contribution over years is what makes the offer
substantive when it comes.

## Route selection — what shapes the head's own choice

The choice among the five routes turns on four questions the head
answers honestly:

- **What is my personal appetite for scope-expansion vs.
  focus-deepening?** Path 1 (CAIO) and path 2 (cross-functional
  elevation) are scope-expansions. Path 3 (NED portfolio) is
  scope-diversification. Paths 4 and 5 are focus-deepening. Heads
  in mid-career who still want operational-executive intensity
  gravitate toward paths 1 and 2. Heads later in career often
  choose 3, 4, or 5.
- **What is my personal financial position?** Path 1, 2, and 3
  preserve or increase compensation. Paths 4 and 5 typically
  reduce it, sometimes materially. The head is honest about the
  financial constraints on their choice.
- **What has my visible external portfolio been?** Paths 3, 4, and
  5 are gated by external visibility. The head who has been
  invisible externally is the head to whom paths 3, 4, and 5 are
  not open until the visibility is built.
- **What is the state of the enterprise's AI-governance function?**
  Paths 1 and 2 are typically internal moves. If the function is
  in a place a successor can inherit, the head can move. If not,
  the move is deferred until the succession slate is ready.

## The head's ongoing succession-slate discipline

Chapter [[02-career-paths-into-role]] closed with the succession-
slate discipline the head owes their board. This chapter closes
with the parallel discipline the head owes *themselves*:

- Name annually the two or three onward paths the head is
  actively preserving as options, and the community-leadership,
  credential, and public-portfolio investments that keep each
  path open.
- Track the paths' viability across the year — the CAIO seat that
  opens at the enterprise; the NED search that reaches out via
  the board community; the federal-CAIO posting that appears; the
  industry-body chair role that is discussed at a conference.
- Refresh the slate with the head's executive coach or a peer
  head-of-role annually. The head who has not named the routes is
  the head whose exit reads as reactive; the head who has named
  them is the head whose exit is authored.

## Summary

- Five distinguishable routes out of head-of-AI-governance: CAIO
  elevation (path 1); CRO / CCO / CPO cross-functional elevation
  (path 2); NED / board-portfolio (path 3); public-sector CAIO
  under OMB M-25-21 or equivalent (path 4); industry-body chair
  or academic affiliation (path 5).
- Each path has a distinct trigger (when it opens), a distinct
  move-mechanics posture (how it is negotiated), a distinct
  prerequisite (what the head must have built to qualify), and a
  distinct anti-pattern (how the path is squandered). The chapter
  names each explicitly so the head can read their own tenure
  against the paths and prepare deliberately.
- The credentials portfolio ([[01-certifications-portfolio]]),
  community-leadership scope ([[04-community-leadership-scope]]),
  and thought-leadership cadence ([[05-thought-leadership-cadence]])
  compose with these paths. The credentials preserve the option;
  the community-leadership and thought-leadership work is what
  makes the invitation substantive when it comes.
- Route selection turns on personal scope appetite, financial
  position, external portfolio visibility, and the state of the
  enterprise's AI-governance function. The choice is deliberate;
  the head who has not named the routes ends up on the reactive
  path.
- The head owes their own annual succession slate — the two or
  three routes they are actively preserving — with the head's
  executive coach and a peer head-of-role. The discipline the head
  runs on their function's succession is the discipline the head
  now runs on their own.
