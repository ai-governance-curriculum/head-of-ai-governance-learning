# Public Speaking and Thought-Leadership Discipline

## Motivation

The head-of-role's thought-leadership work is a distinct discipline
from the community-leadership work of chapter
[[04-community-leadership-scope]]. Community leadership is where the
head sits *inside* a body — a working group, a committee, an
advisory board. Thought leadership is where the head speaks
*publicly* under their own name: on a conference stage, in a
by-lined article, in a podcast interview, in a media appearance, in
a position paper the head signs as lead author.

The two disciplines compose. Community-leadership work produces the
substrate — the working-group deliberations, the standards-drafting
experience, the multi-stakeholder-consensus positions — that the
head then translates into public thought leadership. A head who
performs thought leadership without the community-leadership
substrate is a head speaking without the working-drafted authority;
a head who performs community leadership without the thought-
leadership translation is a head whose contributions are known to
20 working-group peers and no one else.

The chapter is a discipline chapter, not a tactic chapter. It
answers three questions:

1. What is the *annual editorial calendar* the head runs? The
   answer covers keynote cadence, by-lined-article cadence, media-
   appearance cadence, and position-paper cadence.
2. Who is the head's *editorial-and-communications counterpart*?
   The answer is the coordination pattern with the enterprise's
   Head of Communications ([[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]
   chapter 04 covered the CHRO/CCO/CCO/comms triad; this chapter
   is the operational cadence under that relationship).
3. What is the *board-of-advisors composition* the head accepts?
   The answer is the specific advisory-position portfolio the head
   holds outside the fiduciary-NED path of
   [[03-career-paths-out-of-role]].

The chapter's practical output is the head's *annual editorial
calendar*, negotiated with the CEO and the Head of Communications
at the start of each planning cycle. The exercise
([[exercises/exercise-05-public-speaking-and-thought-leadership-cadence]])
walks the calendar authorship.

## Core concept — thought leadership as a discipline

Thought leadership is not vanity publishing. The head who produces
thought leadership at cadence is executing four intertwined
objectives:

- **Institutional positioning.** The enterprise's external-positioning
  portfolio ([[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]])
  requires the enterprise to have a public voice on the topics the
  portfolio commits to. The head is that voice. If the enterprise
  is a signatory to the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, the head's
  public writing on capability-threshold evaluation is the visible
  execution of the signature.
- **Regulator- and policy-shaping.** Public writing that regulators
  and legislators read enters the head's regulator-engagement
  substrate ([[mod-104-regulator-engagement]]). A published
  position on the interpretation of an EU AI Act implementing act
  is a position the head has already made public before the
  regulator meeting; the regulator reads it and enters the meeting
  knowing the head's position.
- **Community and peer-network compounding.** Every published
  piece is a signal to the community that the head is engaged on
  the topic. Peers read it, invite the head to related
  conversations, cite the piece in their own work, and produce
  the compounding reputation the onward-path routes 3, 4, and 5
  ([[03-career-paths-out-of-role]]) depend on.
- **Team-signal and coaching-credibility.** The team sees what the
  head publishes. The head who models the discipline of writing
  under time constraint, defending a position in public, and
  incorporating critique with grace is the head whose team
  develops the same discipline. Thought-leadership is the head's
  visible tenure.

Thought leadership at cadence is not a vanity practice; it is a
professional practice. The head who treats it as vanity is the head
whose pieces are unread. The head who treats it as discipline is
the head whose pieces are cited.

## The annual editorial calendar

The editorial calendar is the head's operational discipline. It is
authored at the start of each planning cycle (typically at the
enterprise's fiscal-year kickoff) and coordinates with the
Communications function, the Legal function (through the GC), and
the CEO. It runs at three layers.

### Layer 1 — the flagship pieces

Two to four flagship pieces per year. Each is a substantive piece
authored under the head's name that the enterprise is comfortable
citing as the head's institutional position. Formats vary:

- **Position paper.** 3-10 pages, published on the enterprise's
  website or via a working-body imprint the head is affiliated
  with. Sometimes coincides with a regulator consultation or a
  standards-body milestone.
- **Long-form by-lined article** in a peer-reviewed or trade
  publication — Communications of the ACM, MIT Technology Review,
  Harvard Business Review, IAPP Privacy Tech, RAPS Focus,
  Journal of AI Research, and jurisdictional equivalents.
- **Chapter contribution** to an edited volume on AI governance,
  AI safety, or a sector-specific AI topic.
- **Keynote address** at a flagship conference (see below).

Flagship pieces are pre-cleared with the CEO and the GC. They
represent enterprise positions the enterprise will stand behind
under regulator or press cross-examination. The pre-clearance
protocol is worked with Communications and Legal as a standing
matter, not as ad-hoc-per-piece approval.

### Layer 2 — the sector- and community-specific pieces

Four to eight pieces per year at sector- or community-specific
depth. Each is authored for a specific readership — a sector
industry-body publication, a credential-body member magazine, a
standards-body newsletter, a policy institute's working-paper
series. Formats:

- **Sector-industry-body pieces.** A banking head publishes in
  RMA's Journal of Risk Management or in PRMIA's Intelligent Risk;
  an insurance head publishes in Contingencies or the CAS Forum;
  a pharma head publishes in DIA's Global Forum or RAPS Focus.
- **Credential-body pieces.** The IAPP Privacy Tech quarterly, the
  ISACA Journal, the IIA Internal Auditor.
- **Standards-body technical contributions.** ISO SC 42 working-
  group technical notes, IEEE Standards Association working-group
  outputs.
- **Policy-institute working papers.** A non-resident-fellow paper
  at Brookings, RAND, Chatham House, or the Ada Lovelace Institute.

These pieces do not require CEO pre-clearance in every case, but
they do run through Communications' review to ensure consistency
with the enterprise's ratified positions. Where a piece takes a
position the enterprise has not ratified, the head runs the
piece through the full pre-clearance protocol.

### Layer 3 — the responsive and short-form pieces

Six to twelve pieces per year in responsive or short-form formats.
Each responds to a current regulatory, technical, or industry event
in a form that reaches a wider audience than the flagship pieces
carry. Formats:

- **Blog posts** on the enterprise's blog or a personal professional
  blog. Typically 800-2000 words. Publishable within 72-96 hours of
  the triggering event.
- **LinkedIn long-form posts.** Similar length; higher reach among
  peer-network readers; lower reach into regulator and academic
  populations.
- **Podcast guest appearances.** 45-90 minutes typical; the head
  discusses a specific topic in interview format. Preparation is
  1-2 hours; the appearance itself is 60-90 minutes; the piece
  ships as the podcast episode.
- **Op-eds** in the Financial Times, the Wall Street Journal, the
  Guardian, or jurisdictional equivalents. These are shorter than
  by-lined articles (700-1200 words) and are typically responsive
  to a specific regulatory or industry event.
- **Threaded posts on X / Bluesky / Mastodon.** These are the
  fastest form; a head with community standing produces them
  regularly on specific technical or regulatory developments. Note
  that content on these platforms is public and archived, and the
  head's posts are read as the head's institutional position by
  peers and regulators regardless of any personal-capacity
  disclaimer.

Responsive pieces are handled by the head with a lightweight
Communications review — the head has typically pre-negotiated a
delegation with the Head of Communications that specific low-stakes
topics can be published without piece-by-piece review, and specific
high-stakes topics (incident-adjacent, regulatory-decision-adjacent,
counterparty-adjacent) always route through review before
publication.

## The keynote cadence — a specific calibration

Chapter 04 named the keynote cadence at a high level. This chapter
gives the operational discipline.

### Selection

The head accepts keynotes on three criteria:

- **Audience.** Does the audience read the head's subsequent work?
  Keynotes at conferences whose audience the head will not
  re-engage with are keynotes whose compounding value is one-off.
  The head prefers keynotes at conferences the head will speak at
  again in three to five years.
- **Substance.** Does the keynote require the head to say something
  worth saying? A conference program that has invited the head to
  deliver a generic "the future of AI governance" address is a
  program the head should either decline or renegotiate into a
  substantive-topic address. The keynote's substance is what makes
  the preparation compound; a generic keynote costs the same
  hours as a specific keynote but produces no compounding follow-
  through.
- **Time.** Does the keynote fit the head's calendar without
  eroding cluster 1 or cluster 2 commitments? A keynote scheduled
  the week of the audit-committee meeting is a keynote the head
  declines regardless of the invitation's prestige.

### Preparation

A flagship keynote requires 20-60 hours of preparation. The
discipline:

- **Draft the argument in prose first.** Slide construction before
  argument construction produces slide-driven talks whose argument
  is thin. The head writes the argument as if it were a by-lined
  article first; slides render the argument's structure.
- **Rehearse against a peer.** The head rehearses at least once
  against a peer head-of-role or a senior architect who will
  challenge the argument. First-pass keynotes that have not been
  peer-rehearsed produce blindspot-heavy Q&A performance.
- **Anticipate the Q&A.** The head lists the ten questions a
  hostile-but-fair questioner would ask, drafts a two-minute
  response to each, and rehearses the responses. Keynotes are
  read by the audience for the questioned segment as much as for
  the delivered segment; the head who has prepared the Q&A is
  read as substantive.
- **Publish the argument.** Every flagship keynote has a companion
  published piece — the position paper, the by-lined article, the
  blog post — that captures the argument in permanent form. A
  keynote whose argument is not published is a keynote that
  reaches only the audience in the room.

### Delivery discipline

The head is present on the day, not in the wings. The head arrives
early enough to hear the panel that precedes their keynote; the
head stays through the questions after their keynote and through
the panel that follows; the head takes 20-30 minutes of one-on-one
conversations at the reception. The head who arrives moments
before their keynote and leaves moments after is a head who has
executed the delivery but not the compounding follow-through.

## The board-of-advisors composition

Advisory-board positions (distinct from the fiduciary-NED positions
in [[03-career-paths-out-of-role]] path 3) are non-fiduciary roles
the head accepts at scale-appropriate depth.

### Typical advisory positions

- **Start-up advisory boards.** Small AI-governance or AI-safety
  companies, RegTech companies, AI-audit firms. The head advises
  on product-market fit, on regulator-facing positioning, and on
  hiring. Typical cost: 8-20 hours per quarter. Compensation is
  typically 0.1-1% of common stock or a fixed annual retainer.
- **Consultancy advisory councils.** The Big Four and adjacent
  consulting firms often run AI-governance advisory councils
  chaired by senior partners. The head advises on practice
  direction. Typical cost: 8-16 hours per year. Compensation
  varies widely; some are unpaid.
- **Research non-profit advisory boards.** AI-safety research
  non-profits (Center for AI Safety, Institute for Advanced Study,
  Alignment Research Center, Frontier Model Forum's research
  fellowship program, etc.). Typical cost: 12-24 hours per year.
  Typically unpaid or nominal honorarium.
- **Academic-institution advisory boards.** University AI-policy
  centres, business-school AI ethics programmes, policy-institute
  advisory councils. Typical cost: 8-20 hours per year. Typically
  unpaid.
- **Publication editorial boards.** AI & Ethics, Big Data &
  Society, IAPP Privacy Tech advisory board, etc. Typical cost:
  8-16 hours per year plus per-manuscript review time.

### Portfolio construction rules

The head applies the joint-seat protocol from
[[04-community-leadership-scope]] to every advisory position, and
in addition:

- **Conflict-of-interest management.** Advisory positions at
  enterprises or funds whose commercial interests intersect with
  the head's employer are cleared with the GC. Advisory positions
  at start-ups the head's employer is a customer of, an investor
  in, or a competitor to are cleared or declined.
- **Total portfolio ceiling.** More than five active advisory
  positions is a portfolio that begins to read as scattered. The
  head calibrates the portfolio against the enterprise's read on
  their focus.
- **Rotation.** Advisory positions are typically renewed on 12-24
  month cycles. The head reviews the portfolio annually and
  actively terminates positions where the substrate has drifted
  from the head's current interests, rather than allowing the
  portfolio to accrete indefinitely.

## Media strategy — the discipline

Media engagement — the print interview, the on-camera segment, the
podcast interview, the background call with a reporter — is the
highest-risk form of thought leadership. The head who is not
disciplined here produces the enterprise's next unfavourable
headline.

### The coordination pattern with Communications

The head's default posture is that all media engagement is
coordinated with the Head of Communications. The specific protocol:

- **Reporter identifies to the enterprise, not to the head
  directly.** The enterprise's press desk receives incoming
  reporter requests and routes them to Communications and the
  head. Where the reporter has reached the head directly (through
  a personal introduction or through the head's community
  network), the head loops Communications before responding.
- **Communications does the pre-interview brief.** Reporter's
  outlet, past coverage, current story angle, other sources
  reporter is speaking with, expected publication date, on-record
  vs. background vs. off-record posture. The head does not accept
  a live interview without the brief.
- **Communications is on the call.** For on-record interviews, a
  Communications colleague joins the call. For background
  conversations, the coordination is post-call rather than
  during-call.
- **The head does not confirm quotes.** The reporter's quote-
  confirmation practice varies by outlet; the head defers to
  Communications on whether to confirm and how.
- **Post-publication debrief.** After the story runs, the head
  and Communications review coverage, note lessons learned, and
  update the media-engagement register.

### On-record vs. background vs. off-record

The head is disciplined about the three postures:

- **On-record.** The head's words are quotable with attribution.
  Default for pre-scheduled interviews with a specific story
  focus.
- **Background.** The head's words inform the reporter's story
  without direct attribution — typically as "a senior executive
  at [enterprise]" or "a source familiar with the matter."
  Default for exploratory or context-setting conversations.
- **Off-record.** The head's words do not appear in the story at
  all. Reserved for genuinely sensitive conversations where the
  head is providing the reporter with orientation the reporter
  will use to shape questions asked of on-record sources.

The head who agrees to background at the start of a call and
then, mid-call, provides on-record-worthy material without
switching the posture explicitly is a head whose next reporter
call is coloured by the ambiguity. The head who is disciplined —
who names the posture up front and re-names it if the
conversation shifts — is a head reporters trust for repeat access.

### Crisis communication

Where the head is speaking during an incident (see
[[mod-110-incident-leadership]] chapter 06 on the public-
disclosure phase), the media discipline tightens further. The
head does not engage media individually during a live incident
without the Communications-led coordination pattern; the head does
not update prior statements without coordination; the head does
not speculate about causes or fixes in real time. The discipline
that carries the head through a routine media appearance is what
carries them through a crisis media appearance.

## The position-paper editorial calendar

Position papers are the most substantive of the layer-1 flagship
pieces. The chapter closes with the specific editorial-calendar
discipline for them.

### The annual selection

At the start of each planning cycle, the head selects one-to-three
position-paper topics for the coming year. Selection criteria:

- **Regulatory-timing alignment.** A position paper published in
  the six months before a regulator consultation deadline is a
  paper that shapes the consultation. A paper published after the
  deadline is a paper that shapes the retrospective analysis but
  not the decision.
- **Standards-body-milestone alignment.** A paper published as an
  ISO or CEN working-draft moves into DIS or FDIS review is a
  paper that shapes ballot debate. Timing to the standards-body
  milestone matters.
- **Enterprise-portfolio alignment.** Papers that touch topics the
  enterprise has committed to publicly (through
  [[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]]) compound
  the enterprise's institutional position. Papers on adjacent
  topics dilute it.

### The drafting cadence

A position paper takes 3-6 months to author well:

- **Month 1 — outline and evidence gathering.** The head drafts an
  outline, identifies the three-to-five substantive claims the
  paper will make, and gathers the evidence for each claim.
- **Month 2 — first draft.** 3-10 pages of the head's argument.
  Circulated to three-to-five peer reviewers (community
  colleagues, external counsel where the paper touches legal
  claims, technical peers where the paper touches technical
  claims).
- **Month 3 — revised draft.** Incorporating peer feedback.
  Circulated to Communications and the GC.
- **Months 4-5 — pre-clearance and refinement.** Communications
  and GC review, revised drafts, CEO sign-off if the paper is
  taking a position the enterprise will stand behind under
  regulator or press cross-examination.
- **Month 6 — publication and follow-through.** Publishing the
  paper, the accompanying blog post, the LinkedIn announcement,
  the media outreach. Following through on responses, published
  commentary, and consequent invitations.

The head who tries to compress this cycle produces papers whose
arguments are thinner than the head's peers publish; the head who
extends it produces papers whose relevance has passed by the time
of publication.

### Co-authorship

Position papers may be single-authored or co-authored. Where the
paper is authored under a working-body imprimatur (SC 42, PAI,
FMF), it is inherently co-authored. Where the paper is authored
under the enterprise's imprimatur alone, the head is the lead but
often has a co-author from the team or a peer at another
enterprise.

The head who co-authors deliberately compounds the community-
leadership work of chapter [[04-community-leadership-scope]] — a
paper co-authored with a peer at a partner enterprise is a paper
whose reach doubles and whose community credibility compounds.

## Summary

- Thought leadership is a discipline, not a vanity practice. The
  head executes four objectives — institutional positioning,
  regulator- and policy-shaping, community and peer-network
  compounding, and team-signal / coaching-credibility — through
  the annual editorial calendar.
- The calendar runs at three layers: two-to-four flagship pieces
  per year (position papers, long-form articles, chapter
  contributions, flagship-conference keynotes); four-to-eight
  sector- or community-specific pieces per year; six-to-twelve
  responsive or short-form pieces per year. Each layer has its own
  pre-clearance protocol and Communications-coordination pattern.
- The keynote cadence is calibrated by audience, substance, and
  time; each flagship keynote costs 20-60 hours of preparation
  and has a companion published piece. Delivery discipline includes
  arriving early, staying late, and taking one-on-one
  conversations at the reception — the compounding is in the room
  after the talk, not in the talk itself.
- The board-of-advisors composition is a distinct portfolio from
  the fiduciary-NED portfolio in [[03-career-paths-out-of-role]].
  It includes start-up advisory boards, consultancy advisory
  councils, research non-profit boards, academic-institution
  advisory boards, and publication editorial boards. The joint-
  seat protocol and a five-position portfolio ceiling apply.
- Media strategy is the highest-risk form of thought leadership.
  The default posture is coordination with the Head of
  Communications: reporter identifies to the enterprise, not the
  head; Communications does the pre-interview brief and joins the
  call; the head is disciplined about on-record / background /
  off-record postures. During incidents
  ([[mod-110-incident-leadership]]) the discipline tightens
  further.
- The position-paper editorial calendar is the most substantive
  layer-1 output. The head selects one-to-three papers per year
  based on regulatory-timing, standards-body-milestone, and
  enterprise-portfolio alignment; each paper takes 3-6 months to
  author well, running from outline through peer-review, pre-
  clearance, CEO sign-off, and publication-and-follow-through
  phases. Co-authorship compounds community-leadership work and
  is used deliberately.
