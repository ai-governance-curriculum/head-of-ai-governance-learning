# Career Paths *Into* the Head of AI Governance Role

## Motivation

There is no single career path into head-of-AI-governance. In the
research window this module was authored against, the sitting
heads-of-role at the enterprises that shape the market — the
frontier labs, the Global-Systemically-Important-Banks, the largest
insurers, the flagship health systems, the federal agencies with
CAIO designations, the Big Four consulting practices — arrived from
at least seven distinguishable prior roles. Each prior role brought
a different substrate: a different professional vocabulary, a
different set of standing regulator relationships, a different
credential portfolio, a different set of blind spots.

The head who understands the multiplicity of paths reads their own
path against the others. The head who came through the technical
route reads themselves against the audit-track head and knows where
their audit-committee craft is thinner. The head who came through
Model Risk Management reads themselves against the technical-track
head and knows where their foundation-model-technical depth is
thinner. The head who came through Big Four consulting reads
themselves against the operator-track head and knows where their
day-two operating craft is thinner. Naming the gap is the first
step to closing it, and closing it — through the credential
sequencing in [[01-certifications-portfolio]], the coaching
relationships in [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter
03, and the community engagements in [[04-community-leadership-scope]]
— is what turns a first-year head into a fifth-year head.

The chapter also serves the *pipeline* function. A sitting head is
often asked, by their CEO or their board, to think about succession.
Naming the seven routes into the role — and being honest about
which candidate populations the enterprise's next head will most
plausibly come from — is the head's own contribution to the
succession planning ([[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
chapter 08 covers the successor-readiness dimension of function
leadership; this chapter is the *market view* the head reads
against).

## The seven paths — an overview

Seven distinguishable routes, in rough order of frequency in the
research window's observed placements:

1. **Senior AI Governance Architect (level 50) → Head of AI
   Governance (level 60).** The natural technical route. The
   architect who authored the enterprise's AIMS design,
   evaluation methodology, and standards-body engagement is
   promoted into the head-of-role either at the same enterprise
   or by moving to another enterprise ready to promote them.
2. **Head of Model Risk Management (with AI/ML scope) → Head of
   AI Governance.** The banking-sector-native route. The Head of
   MRM whose portfolio has grown to include substantial AI/ML
   models transitions into the AI-governance head role either as
   a distinct promotion or as a scope-broadening reclassification
   of their existing seat.
3. **Chief Compliance Officer (with AI-heavy remit) → Head of AI
   Governance.** The compliance-track route. The CCO whose
   enterprise's AI portfolio has grown to demand a specialist
   focus is asked to either (a) subordinate AI to the compliance
   function under their leadership, or (b) hand it to a new head
   of role whose credentials they helped select.
4. **Chief Privacy Officer (with AI-heavy remit) → Head of AI
   Governance.** The privacy-track route. The CPO whose AIA / DPIA
   interface has grown into a substantive AI governance programme
   is asked to broaden into the head-of-AI-governance seat or to
   sponsor a new peer.
5. **Chief Audit Executive (with AI-heavy remit) → Head of AI
   Governance.** The audit-track route. The CAE whose third-line
   AI audit programme requires deep AI-specific technical depth
   the CAE role does not provide moves into (or sponsors a new)
   AI-governance head-of-role seat.
6. **Senior Legal Counsel (with AI-regulatory specialisation) →
   Head of AI Governance.** The rarer legal-track route. The
   senior counsel who authored the enterprise's EU AI Act,
   Colorado AI Act, and CPRA/CPPA compliance opinions transitions
   into the operational head-of-role.
7. **Big Four Consulting Partner (with AI-governance practice) →
   Head of AI Governance.** The increasingly common
   consulting-partner route. The partner whose external client
   engagements built AIGP and ISO/IEC 42001 depth crosses into
   an operator-side head-of-role position.

The rest of the chapter unpacks each path — the substrate the
candidate brings, the specific gaps they typically need to close in
year one, the credential and coaching investments the enterprise
funds during onboarding, and the anti-pattern the enterprise avoids
in promoting from each source.

## Path 1 — Senior AI Governance Architect (level 50) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The senior architect authored the enterprise's AIMS design against
ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894, and ISO/IEC 42005; authored the
evaluation and monitoring methodology; wrote the technical portions
of the enterprise's EU AI Act, Colorado AI Act, and NIST AI RMF
gap analyses; and led the technical engagement with the standards
bodies. The candidate typically holds ISO/IEC 42001 Lead
Implementer, often Lead Auditor, holds AIGP, and holds at least
one governance-adjacent credential (CGEIT or CIPT).

The architect brings deep technical craft — model risk assessment
methodology, evaluation-harness design, foundation-model dependency
management, third-party AI due diligence, ISO 42001 audit
preparation. They speak the vocabulary of the technical adjacencies
in [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] chapter 01 (CISO
coordination) and chapter 02 (CIO / CTO / CDO coordination)
natively.

### The typical gaps in year one

The architect's craft was authored, not politicised. Their
promotion introduces four gaps the enterprise's onboarding plan
addresses explicitly:

- **Audit-committee-craft depth.** The architect has co-authored
  cluster 2 of the board pack ([[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]])
  but has not personally presented it. The muscle of walking into
  the audit committee, taking the tough question, and responding
  in the disciplined register the committee expects is a muscle
  the architect builds in the first three quarters. Deliberate
  investment: shadow the CRO or the CAE in one full audit-committee
  cycle before independently presenting; the CFO or the CRO
  sponsor the debrief.
- **CxO peer relationships.** The architect has worked as a
  senior individual contributor with the CxO peers, not as a peer.
  The new head is now weekly-standing with the CRO, monthly with
  the CFO, quarterly-plus with the audit committee chair.
  Deliberate investment: an executive coach for the first year;
  scheduled one-off coffees with each CxO peer in the first sixty
  days.
- **Regulator-engagement front-of-room craft.** The architect has
  written the substantive portions of the enterprise's EU AI Act
  filings but has not fronted the regulator meeting. The new
  head learns to hold a regulator meeting in the disciplined
  register [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 07 teaches.
  Deliberate investment: co-attend a regulator meeting with the
  outgoing head or a peer-sector counterpart before independently
  fronting one.
- **People-leadership depth at scale.** The architect may have
  managed two or three engineers; the head runs a function of
  six-to-nine. The people-leadership craft in
  [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 03 is a
  discipline the architect learns through onboarding — a formal
  first-time-people-leader coaching relationship is worth its
  weight.

### The onboarding investment

The enterprise that promotes an architect into head-of-role budgets
a 90-day intensive onboarding: audit-committee shadowing (30
days), CxO peer coffees (60 days), regulator co-attendance (60
days), executive coach retained (12 months), first-time-manager
coaching (6 months). The credential portfolio is largely already in
place; the investment is in the leadership craft rather than the
credentials.

### The anti-pattern

Promoting the architect and treating the promotion as recognition
without an onboarding investment. The new head is thrown into the
first audit-committee presentation without shadowing, fronts a
regulator meeting on their fourth week without co-attendance, and
begins running the function on default instincts. First-quarter
performance suffers, and the audit committee reads it as a bad
promotion decision. The remedy is the deliberate onboarding
investment above — the recognition is the promotion; the readiness
is the investment.

## Path 2 — Head of Model Risk Management (with AI/ML scope) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The Head of MRM at a bank or a large insurer has run the enterprise
model inventory under SR 11-7 / OCC 2011-12 (US bank), OSFI E-23
(Canadian bank), PRA SS1/23 (UK bank), or NAIC AI Model Bulletin
adopted by state DOIs (insurer). They have signed into model
validation independence, chaired the enterprise model risk
committee, and fronted the model-risk portion of the CCAR / DFAST
submissions where applicable. They typically hold GARP FRM or PRMIA
PRM, may hold ISACA CRISC, and know the sector's model-risk
vocabulary at operating depth.

Their AI/ML scope has grown organically. The bank's foundation-model
adoption produced a class of models the MRM function was already
supervising; the Head of MRM has been the *acting* head of AI
governance for those models even before the role was reclassified.

### The typical gaps in year one

The MRM head's craft is quantitative-model-supervision; their gaps
are on the *non-model AI* surface and on the *AI-specific
regulatory* surface:

- **Foundation-model-technical depth.** The MRM head knows the
  quantitative-model vocabulary. Foundation models present risks
  the MRM framework was not designed for — data-leakage,
  adversarial prompting, prompt-injection, hallucination
  characterisation, multi-modal evaluation, agentic-workflow
  risk. The MRM head reads themselves against the technical
  chapters ([[mod-102-running-the-program]] chapters 06-08 on
  evaluation and monitoring) and often takes an ISO/IEC 42001
  Lead Implementer credential in year one to cover the AIMS
  operating vocabulary that MRM does not natively include.
- **Non-model AI programme scope.** The MRM framework does not
  extend to internal-productivity AI, customer-service chatbots
  where no acted-upon quantitative estimate is produced, or
  marketing-personalisation systems. As head-of-AI-governance the
  candidate now owns that surface, and the coordination pattern
  with the enterprise's Chief Compliance Officer
  ([[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] chapter 09) becomes
  central.
- **AI-Act and cross-jurisdictional depth.** The US bank Head of
  MRM has operated primarily under Federal Reserve / OCC / FDIC.
  The head-of-AI-governance role's regulator surface is broader —
  the EU AI Act if the bank has EU exposure, state AI laws
  (Colorado, California, Texas) where applicable, sector
  regulator overlays outside banking (insurance affiliate, wealth
  management affiliate). The candidate takes AIGP if they do not
  hold it, and often takes CIPP/E if the enterprise's EU exposure
  is substantive.
- **Standards-body engagement.** The MRM head has typically
  engaged Federal Reserve / OCC / FDIC bilaterally. The
  head-of-AI-governance role engages the standards ecosystem
  (ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, CEN-CENELEC JTC 21, NIST AI RMF working
  groups). The candidate reads themselves against the community-
  leadership scope in [[04-community-leadership-scope]] and picks
  a working-group commitment to build the community standing.

### The onboarding investment

The enterprise budgets AIGP + ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer as
year-one credential investments; a coaching relationship with a
sitting head-of-AI-governance from another sector (technology
frontier lab or health system) as a cross-sector broadening; and
a specific working-group placement at NIST AIRC or ISO SC 42 mirror
committee.

### The anti-pattern

Treating the promotion as a reclassification and not investing in
the AI-specific surface. The new head runs the AI programme as an
extension of the MRM programme, misses the non-model AI surface,
and produces a first regulator engagement outside the sector
supervisor (a state DOI cross-line, or an EU AI Office letter) where
their sector-native vocabulary reads as insufficient. The remedy is
the deliberate AI-specific credential and community investment
above.

## Path 3 — Chief Compliance Officer (with AI-heavy remit) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The CCO has run the enterprise compliance function — the compliance
policy library, the enterprise's regulator-engagement register, the
whistleblower and complaint intake, the compliance-training
programme, the compliance-monitoring reviews. The CCO has typically
held ISACA CGEIT or CRISC, sometimes IIA CIA, may hold IAPP CIPM,
and has fronted the enterprise's non-AI regulator engagements at
audit-committee depth for years.

The AI-heavy remit means the CCO's compliance monitoring has
increasingly turned on AI systems — the AI-in-HR programme under
NYC LL 144, the fair-lending programme under Regulation B for
credit-decisioning AI, the consumer-facing AI under the FTC's
UDAP authority, and (at multinationals) the EU AI Act, CPRA/CPPA,
DPDPA, and jurisdictional-privacy compliance where AI is the
central mechanism. The CCO has been running AI-adjacent compliance
without a dedicated AI-governance function.

### The typical gaps in year one

The CCO's craft is compliance monitoring — the second-line
independence read on first-line adherence. Their gaps are on the
*technical operator* surface and the *AIMS-specific* surface:

- **Technical operating vocabulary.** The CCO reads AI systems
  through compliance-monitoring lenses. As head-of-AI-governance
  they are now signing into technical artefacts — evaluation
  reports, monitoring signals, model cards, third-party AI due
  diligence packages. The candidate takes ISO/IEC 42001 Lead
  Implementer in year one and reads deeply against the running-
  program chapters in [[mod-102-running-the-program]] to build
  the technical vocabulary.
- **AIMS operator craft.** The CCO has run a compliance function
  but not an AIMS. The distinction matters — the AIMS is the
  operator system the AI portfolio runs through; compliance is
  the monitoring lens over the operator system. The candidate
  learns to distinguish the two so that the head-of-AI-governance
  role does not collapse into a compliance-monitoring role that
  loses the operator craft.
- **Peer-with-CCO reset.** The candidate's promotion into
  head-of-AI-governance often means their old CCO seat is filled
  by a successor. The new head coordinates with the new CCO
  ([[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] chapter 09), and the
  head does not treat the CCO seat as still theirs. This is a
  role-boundary discipline that some CCO-track candidates struggle
  with in the first six months.

### The onboarding investment

ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer in year one; a coaching relationship
with a technical-track head-of-AI-governance to accelerate the
technical operating vocabulary; the deliberate handoff protocol
with the new CCO documented at the head's start date.

### The anti-pattern

Running the AI-governance function as a compliance-monitoring
function. The new head does not build an operator-side AIMS; they
audit an operator-side AIMS that does not exist. First-line product
teams have no operating framework to work against; second-line
compliance-monitoring produces findings without a first-line
target. The remedy is the AIMS Lead Implementer investment and the
deliberate operator-side focus in year one.

## Path 4 — Chief Privacy Officer (with AI-heavy remit) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The CPO has run the enterprise privacy programme — the ROPA, the
DPIA cadence, GDPR / CPRA / CPPA / DPDPA compliance, the DPA
relationship. The CPO holds CIPP (jurisdictional variant), CIPM,
often CIPT, and may hold ISO/IEC 27701 Lead Implementer as the
privacy management system credential.

The AI-heavy remit means the CPO's AIA and DPIA scopes have merged
in practice — the privacy programme has been signing into AI
impact assessments as part of the DPIA cadence, and the CPO has
fronted the enterprise's AI-adjacent privacy regulator engagements
(EDPB, ICO, CNIL, CPPA).

### The typical gaps in year one

- **Non-privacy AI risk surface.** The CPO has read AI systems
  through privacy risk lenses (data minimisation, purpose
  limitation, transparency, individual rights). AI governance is
  broader — safety, robustness, security, fairness, environmental
  impact, cross-sector regulatory. The candidate reads against
  the AI-specific chapters ([[mod-102-running-the-program]] and
  [[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]]) to broaden the
  risk lens.
- **AIMS operator craft.** As with the CCO route, the CPO has
  run a management system (a PIMS under ISO/IEC 27701) but not
  an AIMS. Lead Implementer for ISO/IEC 42001 is the year-one
  credential.
- **AI-technical vocabulary.** The CPO reads AI systems as
  data-processing operations. The AI-governance head reads them
  as models with training data, evaluation performance, drift
  characteristics, adversarial robustness properties. The
  candidate takes AIGP if not already held, and often takes IAPP
  CIPT if it is not already in portfolio.

### The onboarding investment

ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer + AIGP as year-one credentials; a
technical coaching relationship (with a level-50 architect or with
a peer head-of-AI-governance from a technical route); an explicit
handoff protocol with the successor CPO where privacy-AI overlap
now sits with the new CPO rather than the head.

### The anti-pattern

Extending the privacy programme's AIA into an AI-governance
programme without broadening the risk lens. The new head produces
an AI-governance framework whose fairness, safety, robustness, and
security dimensions are thin — because the CPO framework did not
have them. The audit committee reads the framework as
privacy-with-AI-added, and the enterprise's non-privacy AI regulator
engagements (sector regulator, EU AI Office, US AISI) find the
framework insufficient. The remedy is the deliberate broadening in
year one with technical coaching and AI-specific credentials.

## Path 5 — Chief Audit Executive (with AI-heavy remit) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The CAE has run the third-line audit function under the IIA Three
Lines Model and the IIA IPPF. The CAE holds IIA CIA, often ISACA
CGEIT or CRISC, sometimes AICPA CPA (in US markets). The CAE has
audited the enterprise's AI programme from third-line independence
and knows the enterprise's operating stance at examiner-quality
depth.

The AI-heavy remit means the CAE's audit plan has increasingly
focused on the AI portfolio — third-line reviews of the AI
governance council, of the model inventory, of foundation-model
third-party AI oversight, of AI incident response. The CAE has
fronted audit-committee findings on AI-programme deficiencies and
has established credibility with the audit committee at
head-of-role depth.

### The typical gaps in year one

- **Operator-side craft.** The CAE has audited operator craft, not
  run it. The transition from independent-audit to operator is the
  central gap — the discipline of *signing into* an evaluation
  result rather than *auditing* an evaluation result is a
  different craft. The candidate takes ISO/IEC 42001 Lead
  Implementer and works with a technical mentor to build
  operator-side depth.
- **AI-technical vocabulary at operating depth.** The CAE has
  read technical evidence for audit purposes but has not
  authored it. The candidate reads deeply against the running-
  program chapters and often takes AIGP as the AI-governance
  baseline.
- **Third-line handoff protocol.** The CAE's successor now audits
  the AI programme the candidate now runs. The successor's
  independence from the head is central — the candidate does not
  attempt to influence the audit plan, does not resist third-line
  findings on the AI programme, and helps the audit committee
  establish that the transition has not compromised third-line
  independence. This is a role-boundary discipline the audit
  committee watches carefully in the first year.

### The onboarding investment

ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer + AIGP; a technical coaching
relationship with a level-50 architect (internal or external
peer); a formal third-line-independence protocol authored and
ratified by the audit committee at the candidate's start date;
declining to attend audit committee sessions where the third line
is presenting on the AI programme, unless explicitly invited as
the accountable head.

### The anti-pattern

Continuing to influence the audit function's AI-programme plan
after transition. The successor CAE finds the third-line
independence compromised, the audit committee notices, and the
transition unwinds. The remedy is the explicit protocol and the
disciplined self-restraint.

## Path 6 — Senior Legal Counsel (with AI-regulatory specialisation) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The senior counsel has authored the enterprise's legal opinions on
EU AI Act, Colorado AI Act, CPRA/CPPA ADMT, NYC LL 144, EEOC
guidance, FTC UDAP enforcement, and the sector-specific instruments
in [[mod-111-sector-program-leadership]]. They hold a JD from a
jurisdiction with an active AI bar (US, UK, EU, Canada, Singapore),
often hold LLM in a related discipline (technology, IP, privacy),
and may hold IAPP CIPP as an adjacent credential.

They speak the regulator's own vocabulary at legal-opinion depth,
and they typically have relationships with the enterprise's
external counsel and with regulator counterparts through the bar
association route.

### The typical gaps in year one

This is the *rarer* route (as the module's learning objectives
notes), because the counsel-to-head transition crosses a
disciplinary boundary that most senior counsel do not choose to
cross. The gaps are substantial:

- **Operator craft, wholesale.** The counsel has not run an
  operational programme. Every dimension of the running program
  ([[mod-102-running-the-program]]) is new craft. The candidate
  takes ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer and reads deeply against
  the running-program chapters; the first six months are a
  focused technical-onboarding exercise.
- **Legal-opinion versus program-artefact boundary.** The counsel
  is trained to produce legal opinions bounded to specific asked
  questions. The head produces program artefacts that live in
  operational cycles. The boundary between engagement (head owns)
  and legal opinion (GC owns) in [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]]
  chapter 07 is now a boundary the candidate is on the *engagement*
  side of, not the *opinion* side of. This is a professional
  identity transition the candidate works through with a coach.
- **Team leadership depth.** The counsel may have led a small
  legal team; the head runs a function of six-to-nine with cross-
  functional dependencies. The people-leadership craft is new
  scale.

### The onboarding investment

ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer + AIGP; a technical coaching
relationship with a level-50 architect; an executive coach to
support the professional-identity transition; the deliberate
handoff protocol with the General Counsel documenting the new
head-vs-GC boundary ([[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]
chapter 06); first-time-manager coaching where the counsel has not
previously run a functional team of the head-of-role's scale.

### The anti-pattern

Continuing to write legal opinions. The candidate's first six
months produce a series of "legal analyses" of AI regulations that
the General Counsel's office reads as boundary-encroaching and the
regulator counterparties read as the head not understanding the
head-versus-GC boundary. The remedy is the explicit boundary and
the deliberate professional-identity coaching. The candidate learns
to package artefacts for the GC to opinion-on, rather than to
produce legal opinions themselves.

## Path 7 — Consulting Big Four Partner (with AI-governance practice) → Head of AI Governance

### The substrate the candidate brings

The Big Four (or Big Four-adjacent — Accenture, EY, KPMG, Deloitte,
PwC, plus BCG/McKinsey/Bain and the boutique-AI advisory firms)
partner has built and led an AI-governance advisory practice at
their firm. They have advised multiple enterprises on ISO/IEC
42001 implementation, EU AI Act preparation, NIST AI RMF
adoption, and sector-specific AI governance. They hold AIGP, often
ISO/IEC 42001 Lead Implementer and Lead Auditor, and typically
hold their firm's specific quality-management credential (PMP,
CBAP, or firm-internal senior manager / director / partner
qualifications).

They have breadth across many enterprises' operating patterns and
often deeper technical range than any single operator-side head
because they have seen many enterprises' evaluation methodologies,
model inventories, and third-party AI due diligence packages.

### The typical gaps in year one

The consulting-track candidate's gaps are on the *day-two operating*
craft that consulting engagements do not exercise:

- **Standing accountability.** The consulting engagement ends;
  the operator-side head-of-role does not. The candidate learns
  to design for durability — programs that a successor can
  inherit, artefacts that survive personnel change, cadences that
  run without champion-dependency. The head-of-role is not
  designing a two-year transformation programme; they are
  designing the operating platform for a ten-year regulatory
  arc.
- **Internal-political depth.** Consulting engagements protect
  their sponsor and manage political tension at arms length. The
  head-of-role is inside the political dynamics of the C-suite,
  the board, and cross-functional peers. The candidate builds the
  relationship craft in [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]
  at operator-side depth, which is a different practice from
  advisory-relationship craft.
- **Multi-year budget defence.** The consulting partner priced
  engagements; the head-of-role defends a multi-year budget
  against the CFO ([[mod-113-budget-vendor-p-and-l-and-investment-case]]).
  The candidate learns the internal-budget craft, which is a
  distinct discipline from external pricing.
- **Personal accountability under incident.** The consulting
  partner writes an advisory memo when the client has an
  incident. The head-of-role fronts the incident to the audit
  committee, to the regulator, and (where the incident is
  disclosed) to the public. The [[mod-110-incident-leadership]]
  chapters are lived-out craft the candidate has not personally
  practised at operator-side depth.

### The onboarding investment

An executive coach to support the consultant-to-operator identity
transition; a formal peer relationship with a sitting
head-of-AI-governance at a peer enterprise, arranged through the
credential body community; scheduled shadowing of at least one
incident-response exercise before the enterprise's first actual
incident tests the candidate; deliberate refusal to fill the year
with new-programme initiatives, in favour of learning the operator
craft on the programme that already exists.

### The anti-pattern

Running the head-of-role as an internal consultant. The candidate
produces a series of transformation programmes, launches
initiatives that require sustained follow-through, and moves on
to the next initiative before the previous one has stabilised.
The function fatigues under continuous transformation, the audit
committee reads the pattern as busy-work rather than substantive
progress, and the candidate loses the standing they built as a
consultant. The remedy is the deliberate focus on operator craft
and the discipline of leaving initiatives that already work alone
long enough to compound.

## Route selection — what shapes the enterprise's choice

The enterprise's choice between the seven routes turns on four
factors the head reads back against for their own succession
planning:

- **Sector.** Banks and insurers weight the MRM route heavily
  because the sector's own operating vocabulary favours it.
  Frontier labs and technology enterprises weight the technical
  (architect) route heavily because their AI portfolio is the
  business. Health systems weight the CCO or CPO route where
  HIPAA and clinical-governance shape the seat. Public sector
  weights the audit or legal route where the CIGIE or IG
  relationship shapes the seat.
- **Reporting line.** An enterprise that positions the head under
  the CRO leans MRM or ISACA-credentialed. An enterprise that
  positions under the CPO or GC leans privacy or legal. An
  enterprise that positions under the COO or CEO takes any of the
  seven routes depending on other factors.
- **AI portfolio maturity.** Early-stage AI programmes weight the
  compliance or privacy route (they need someone who can build
  the second-line framework from the enterprise's existing
  functions). Mature AI programmes weight the technical or
  consulting route (they need someone who can operate the
  standing AIMS at scale).
- **Successor pool visibility.** Where the enterprise has a
  visible level-50 architect on the succession slate, the
  architect route is the default. Where the enterprise does not
  have that architect, the enterprise reaches into the CxO peer
  set or the external market and selects one of the other six
  routes.

## Succession planning — the head's own contribution

The sitting head owes their board a documented succession slate.
The chapter's final practical output is the head's own
succession-slate discipline:

- Name the internal candidates on each of the seven routes who
  could plausibly succeed the head within a defined window (12-24
  months as ready-now, 24-48 months as developmental).
- Name the specific development gaps each candidate needs to
  close, mapping to the credential and coaching investments in
  [[01-certifications-portfolio]] and
  [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 03.
- Name the external-market candidates the enterprise would
  consider if internal succession were not viable, with the
  route the external candidates come from.
- Refresh the slate annually with the head's peer at CHRO
  (executive-succession discipline is a CHRO-standing artefact).

The head who does not maintain the slate is the head whose
departure produces an unplanned external search under time
pressure. The head who does maintain it is a head the board reads
as running their own function with the discipline the head demands
of everyone else's function.

## Summary

- There is no single career path into head-of-AI-governance.
  Seven distinguishable routes recur in the research window:
  senior AI governance architect (level 50, the technical route);
  Head of Model Risk Management (the banking-native route); Chief
  Compliance Officer with AI-heavy remit; Chief Privacy Officer
  with AI-heavy remit; Chief Audit Executive with AI-heavy remit;
  senior legal counsel with AI-regulatory specialisation (rarer);
  and consulting Big Four partner with AI-governance practice
  (increasingly common).
- Each route brings a distinct substrate — technical craft,
  quantitative-model supervision, compliance monitoring, privacy
  risk lens, third-line independence, legal-opinion authority, or
  cross-enterprise advisory breadth — and each brings distinct
  year-one gaps that the enterprise's onboarding investment
  addresses through targeted credentials
  ([[01-certifications-portfolio]]) and coaching relationships
  ([[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]] chapter 03).
- The enterprise's route selection turns on sector, reporting
  line, AI-portfolio maturity, and internal-succession-pool
  visibility. There is no globally-correct route; there is a
  best-fit route for a specific enterprise.
- The sitting head owes their board a documented succession
  slate covering all viable routes and the specific development
  gaps each internal candidate needs to close. The slate is
  refreshed annually with the CHRO. The head who does not
  maintain the slate produces an unplanned external search.
- Every route pairs with a specific anti-pattern that year-one
  onboarding is designed to prevent. Naming the anti-pattern —
  the promotion-without-onboarding for the architect route, the
  MRM-scope-extension for the MRM route, the compliance-lens
  collapse for the CCO route, the privacy-narrowing for the CPO
  route, the third-line-influence for the CAE route, the
  continued-legal-opinion for the counsel route, and the internal-
  consultant behaviour for the partner route — is how the head's
  own onboarding avoids them.
