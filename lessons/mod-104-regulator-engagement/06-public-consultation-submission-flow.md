# Owning the Public-Consultation Submission Flow

## Motivation

An enterprise's regulatory posture is shaped as much by what it
files into a public-consultation docket as by what it says on a
call with a supervisor. Direct engagement (chapters 02 through 04
of this module) determines how a regulator experiences the
enterprise as a counterparty; consultation submissions determine
what the rules the regulator later enforces actually say. The
first is relational and slow-building; the second is textual and,
once the comment window closes, permanent.

Missing a consultation cycle on a rule the enterprise will be
bound by is missing the last formal, on-the-record opportunity to
shape that rule before it is set. It is a forfeiture. The
enterprise then either lives with a rule it could have influenced,
or spends multiples of the consultation effort trying to influence
it through implementing guidance, informal engagement, or
litigation.

Poorly-drafted submissions are worse than missing the cycle
altogether. A submission is a public commitment. Regulators, GC,
industry peers, journalists, plaintiffs' counsel, and future
examination teams will read what the enterprise put on the record
and treat it as the enterprise's position — years later,
regardless of who wrote it and how carefully the enterprise now
wishes it had been phrased. A submission that contradicts a prior
submission is a credibility incident. A submission that reveals a
system the enterprise had not previously disclosed is a discovery
event. A submission written in a personal register by a team lead
is an attributable enterprise position.

The head of AI governance does not draft consultation responses.
The head owns the *process discipline* — the pipeline that turns a
request-for-comment into a signed, defensible, on-time enterprise
submission, and archives it in a form a future reader can trust.
This chapter is that discipline.

## Core concept: the consultation portfolio as a pipeline

The head runs consultations as a portfolio, not as a queue of
one-offs. Every open request-for-comment gets a **stance decision**
early, and every consultation the enterprise elects to submit into
runs through a **locked internal-drafting cycle** with named
owners, named reviewers, and dates.

The stance decision has four options and only four:

- **Submit** — the enterprise files its own submission under its
  own name, through the head's process.
- **Co-sign** — the enterprise signs onto an industry-body
  submission (BSA, ITI, Chamber of Digital Commerce, INCOMPAS, a
  sector trade association) as a named signatory.
- **Observe** — the enterprise takes no public position but
  tracks the docket, reads competitor submissions when published,
  and captures the intelligence in the regulatory-change function.
- **Decline** — the enterprise consciously declines to engage,
  with a recorded rationale (out of scope, no exposure, position
  would prejudice a live matter, etc.).

The decline option is not a default for inaction. A recorded
"decline with rationale" is a governance event; an unrecorded
missed window is a control gap. The head enforces the difference.

For every submission stance, the drafting cycle is locked before
the substantive drafting begins. That means the drafting owner,
the reviewer list, the CxO sign-off list, the sign-off threshold,
and the wire dates are agreed *before* anyone starts writing
paragraphs about model classes or fairness testing. Cycles that
start with content and negotiate process late are cycles that miss
the window.

## The consultation portfolio

The head tracks a recurring set of consultation surfaces. The list
is not exhaustive — new surfaces appear as new regulators mature —
but the following are the standing ones the head's portfolio
should have named owners on.

### Standards and framework consultations

- **NIST AI RMF, Playbook, GenAI Profile, and crosswalks.** NIST
  runs public-comment windows on AI RMF revisions, on the
  Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1), on new profiles, and on
  crosswalks to ISO/IEC 42001 and to sector frameworks. Windows
  are announced on the NIST AI Resource Center (NIST AIRC) and via
  Federal Register notices. <!-- needs-research: verify current
  open NIST AI RMF comment windows against nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-
  management-framework -->
- **NIST AI Safety Institute (US AISI) — evaluation methodology
  consultations** on model-evaluation, red-teaming, and safety-
  testing methodology when opened. <!-- needs-research: verify
  current US AISI open consultations against nist.gov/aisi -->
- **CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 — harmonised standards** written in
  response to the Commission's standardisation request under the
  EU AI Act. These standards, once cited in the Official Journal,
  provide a presumption of conformity for high-risk AI systems.
  Direct enterprise engagement with JTC 21 is largely mediated:
  the head engages via national mirror committees (BSI in the UK,
  DIN in Germany, AFNOR in France, ANSI-accredited routes for
  US-headquartered enterprises with an EU presence) and via
  industry associations that hold JTC 21 seats.
- **ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 — international AI standards** including
  ISO/IEC 42001 (AIMS), ISO/IEC 42005 (impact assessment), ISO/IEC
  42006 (auditor requirements), ISO/IEC 23894 (risk management),
  ISO/IEC 24028 (trustworthiness overview), ISO/IEC 38507
  (governance implications for boards), among others. Engagement
  is via national-body membership on the mirror committee. Comment
  windows on Committee Drafts and Draft International Standards
  run on defined ISO cycles.

## The internal-drafting cycle

A defensible submission goes through a fixed cycle. The head does
not innovate on the cycle for individual consultations; the
consistency is the discipline.

### Trigger and stance

The regulatory-change function surfaces the consultation with the
docket reference, the comment window, and a first-cut exposure
assessment. The head convenes a short stance conversation —
typically the head, the level-50 architect, GC, and a senior
government-affairs partner — and records the stance decision
(submit / co-sign / observe / decline) with rationale. The stance
memo is one page and it is archived.

### Named draft owner

Every submission has a single named draft owner. Usually the
level-50 senior AI governance architect or a domain lead (fairness,
safety, sector-specific compliance). Not a committee. Not "the
team". A committee-drafted submission reads as a committee-drafted
submission and regulators discount it accordingly.

The draft owner is responsible for producing the working draft,
running the subject-matter contributions, incorporating peer
review, and holding the version history. The head is the
accountable signatory; the draft owner is the responsible drafter.

### Subject-matter contributions

The working draft pulls contributions from named contributors:

- **Product** — what the enterprise actually builds and ships in
  scope of the consultation. Statements about capability or
  practice that product cannot corroborate do not enter the draft.
- **Engineering** — what is technically feasible and what a
  proposed rule would cost or preclude. Engineering claims about
  infeasibility need to survive later scrutiny; the draft owner
  captures the engineering rationale, not just the conclusion.
- **Data science and research** — what evidence supports the
  positions the enterprise takes on model behaviour, evaluation
  methodology, bias measurement, safety testing, or capability
  claims. Empirical claims cite internal or public evidence.
- **Sector experts** — industry context, precedent from adjacent
  regimes, competitive-dynamic implications of proposed rules.
- **Risk** — portfolio implications of the position, second-order
  consequences on the AI risk register, appetite consistency.

Contributions arrive on a schedule the draft owner locks. Late
contributions do not enter the draft; the discipline is that
contributors know the schedule and meet it.

### CxO peer review

Before head sign-off, the near-final draft goes through named CxO
peer review:

- **General Counsel** — legal exposure of the positions taken,
  inconsistency with prior filings, litigation-adjacent risk,
  privileged-material leakage. GC has a veto on legal-exposure
  grounds. Chapter 07 covers the GC boundary in detail.
- **Chief Technology Officer** — technical accuracy of claims
  about what the enterprise builds and what is feasible.
- **Chief Risk Officer** — consistency with the enterprise risk-
  appetite statements and the AI risk register.
- **Communications** — external-facing tone, phrasing that will
  read poorly in press coverage, terminology consistent with
  external messaging.
- **Government Affairs** — political-relationship consistency,
  awareness of what the enterprise has said in adjacent policy
  venues, coordination with any active lobbying position.

Peer review is time-boxed. Reviewers who do not respond by the
deadline are recorded as having no comment; the draft does not
wait.

### Head sign-off

The head is the accountable signatory for the enterprise's
regulatory-facing position on the substance. Head sign-off
confirms the process ran, the reviewers cleared, and the position
is one the head will defend under later scrutiny.

### CEO or GC sign-off for high-visibility submissions

For submissions above a pre-agreed threshold — front-page
regulations (EU AI Act implementation, headline federal
rulemakings), submissions taking a position materially different
from industry consensus, submissions on matters the board has
tabled — the sign-off escalates to CEO or GC or both. The
threshold is agreed once, in advance, with the CEO's office and
GC, and captured in the head's operating charter (see
[[mod-101-role-scope-and-leadership-shape]]). The head does not
negotiate the threshold submission-by-submission.

### Submission and archive

The submission goes out through the docket-appropriate channel
(regulations.gov for federal US dockets, EU Have Your Say for EU
Commission consultations, the CPPA website, the ICO consultation
form, the ISO national-body route for standards work). The head
owns the archive. Every submission is versioned, dated, cross-
referenced to the docket, and retrievable — cold — by a future
reader.

## Position discipline: the three lenses

Every submission is consciously written in one of three positions,
and the head is explicit inside the enterprise about which:

- **Proprietary interest** — the enterprise advocates for a rule
  that suits its business. Legitimate, common, and unremarkable
  when signposted. Damaging when disguised as principled position.
- **Industry-common position** — the position the enterprise
  shares with peers via industry bodies (BSA, ITI, Chamber of
  Digital Commerce, INCOMPAS, sector trade associations). Aligned
  with a co-signed industry submission or explicitly consistent
  with one.
- **Principled position** — the position the enterprise takes
  because it believes it right on the merits, regardless of
  immediate commercial impact. Rare, credibility-building when
  used sparingly, credibility-destroying when overused.

Mixing lenses within a single submission is the credibility
failure mode. A submission that opens on principle, argues from
proprietary interest in the middle, and closes on industry
consensus reads as none of the three. The head enforces lens
discipline: one submission, one lens, signposted early.

The lens choice is a head-level decision, recorded on the stance
memo and re-confirmed at head sign-off.

## The industry-body composition

Most enterprises file both directly and through industry bodies on
any given consultation of consequence. The head governs the split.

Direct submissions are more visible. The enterprise's name is on
the docket. Regulators read them. Journalists cite them.
Competitors and plaintiffs read them. Direct submissions commit
the enterprise's name to a position in a way industry submissions
do not.

Industry-body submissions dilute the enterprise's individual
voice — the position becomes the industry position, not the
enterprise's — but pool the drafting effort across members, gain
legitimacy from the roster of signatories, and provide cover on
positions the enterprise supports but does not want to lead on.

The head decides the composition per consultation:

- Direct submission alone — when the enterprise's position is
  distinctive or when leadership on the issue matters.
- Industry submission alone — when the enterprise's position is
  aligned with peers and no marginal value comes from a separate
  filing.
- Both, aligned — the enterprise files directly and co-signs the
  industry submission, with the two consistent. This is the common
  case for material consultations.
- Both, divergent — the enterprise co-signs the industry
  submission on the common positions and files directly on the
  points where the enterprise's position differs. Requires
  explicit signposting inside the direct submission that the
  enterprise is also a signatory to the industry filing.

The composition decision is captured on the stance memo. Divergent
composition without signposting is the credibility failure mode
regulators notice.

## Archive discipline

Every submission is retrieved later. The head builds the archive
assuming four cold readers:

- **A future internal examination team** — the head's successor
  reconstructing the enterprise's regulatory-facing position on a
  topic that has become live.
- **A plaintiff's counsel in discovery** — an adversarial reader
  looking for admissions, contradictions with other filings, or
  positions the enterprise cannot now defend.
- **A journalist** — a reader looking for narrative and for
  positions the enterprise has taken that read poorly in the
  current context.
- **An incoming regulator** — a supervisor forming a view of the
  enterprise's regulatory posture from the corpus of its
  submissions.

The archive supports all four. Concretely:

- **Docket cross-reference** — every archived submission is
  linked to the docket entry (regulations.gov docket number, EU
  Have Your Say reference, CPPA rulemaking reference, ISO project
  number) so the archive can be reconciled against the public
  record.
- **Version history** — the drafting versions, the reviewer
  comments, and the final filed version are all retained. Peer-
  review comments are archived under legal-hold-appropriate
  handling coordinated with GC.
- **Stance memo** — the original stance decision and lens choice
  are archived with the submission so a future reader can
  reconstruct not just what was filed but why.
- **Signatory record for industry submissions** — where the
  enterprise co-signed an industry filing, the archive records the
  filing, the signatory roster at the time of filing, and the
  enterprise's internal decision to co-sign.
- **Retention schedule** — coordinated with GC. Submissions are
  public record; the internal drafting artefacts follow the
  enterprise's records-management schedule.

An archive that a cold reader cannot navigate is an archive that
generates audit findings and, in adversarial contexts, adverse
inferences. The head owns the navigability, not just the
completeness.

## Summary

- Consultation submissions shape the rules the enterprise will
  later be bound by; missing a window is a forfeiture, and a
  poorly-drafted submission is worse than none.
- The head runs consultations as a portfolio with four stance
  options — submit, co-sign, observe, decline — and a recorded
  rationale for each.
- The portfolio spans NIST AI RMF and AISI, EU AI Office GPAI Code
  and guidance, CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42
  (largely via mirror committees), CPPA ADMT rulemaking, FDA
  dockets via regulations.gov, the UK ICO, state AGs, and sector
  regulator consultations.
- The internal-drafting cycle is locked before drafting starts:
  named draft owner, named subject-matter contributors, named CxO
  peer reviewers, head sign-off, escalated CEO or GC sign-off
  above a pre-agreed threshold.
- Every submission is written in one of three lenses —
  proprietary, industry-common, or principled — and the lens is
  signposted; mixed lenses destroy credibility.
- Direct and industry-body composition is a head-level decision
  per consultation; divergent filings require explicit signposting.
- The archive is built for four cold readers — successor,
  plaintiff, journalist, incoming regulator — with docket cross-
  references, version history, stance memos, and signatory
  records, on a GC-coordinated retention schedule.
- Horizon-scanning that surfaces consultations lives in
  [[mod-105-regulatory-change-management]]; broader voluntary-code
  engagement (Frontier Model Forum, Partnership on AI, MLCommons)
  lives in [[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]];
  chapter 07 covers the GC boundary on positions taken.
