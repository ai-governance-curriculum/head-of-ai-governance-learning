# The Standards-Body Participation Portfolio at Leadership Scope

## Motivation

Standards-body work is where the rules the enterprise will later
comply with are drafted. A CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 harmonised standard
becomes, on OJEU citation, a presumption of conformity for the
EU AI Act obligations it covers ([[mod-104-regulator-engagement]]
chapter 01, chapter 06). An ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 standard becomes,
on publication, the reference the enterprise's certification body
tests against under ISO/IEC 42006. An IEEE 7000-series standard
becomes the vocabulary a customer procurement specification
inherits without knowing where the vocabulary came from. The
enterprise that has been at the drafting table has shaped what
these standards say, and the enterprise that has not been at the
table lives with what other enterprises shaped.

The head of AI governance is the accountable executive for the
enterprise's standards-body participation portfolio. The head
does not do the technical drafting — the level-50 senior AI
governance architect and named technical contributors do. The
head owns the *portfolio*: which bodies the enterprise
participates in, at what depth, with which of the enterprise's
seats occupied by which of the enterprise's people, and with
what feedback loop from the standards work back into the
enterprise's own operating disciplines.

The failure mode this chapter opens with is the enterprise being
"a member of ISO SC 42" in the sense that the enterprise paid the
national-body fee, but with no attendance, no drafting
contribution, no vote cast at a Committee Draft ballot, and no
back-flow of the standards work into the enterprise's own
program. That is not participation; that is subscription. It
costs the enterprise the fee and returns no value. Regulators
who look at the enterprise's standards-body claims and see
subscription-only participation form a view of the enterprise's
technical seriousness that the head does not want.

This chapter is the portfolio discipline that turns subscription
into participation. Chapter 01 covered the sign-on decision for
voluntary codes; the discipline here is different — standards-
body participation is continuous, procedural, and depends on
sustained technical contribution rather than public commitment.

## Core concept — the participation depth ladder

Participation in a standards body is not binary. Every body
supports a ladder of engagement depths, and the head decides,
per body, where on the ladder the enterprise sits. The ladder
has five rungs.

- **Observer** — the enterprise monitors the body's outputs via
  public channels (published standards, minutes where public,
  press releases). No membership, no fee, no contribution.
- **Member** — the enterprise (via a national body or directly,
  depending on the body's rules) holds membership, receives
  working documents, and can vote on ballots. Passive membership
  — no drafting contribution — is the subscription failure mode.
- **Contributor** — the enterprise has named technical
  contributors attending working-group meetings, submitting
  editorial comments and substantive contributions on drafts,
  and answering national-body ballot questions. The
  contribution is documented in the body's own records.
- **Working-group convenor or editor** — the enterprise
  provides the convenor of a specific working group or the
  editor of a specific work item. This is a named leadership
  role with procedural responsibilities (agenda setting,
  document maintenance, resolution of ballot comments).
- **Committee chair** — the enterprise provides the chair of a
  full technical committee or subcommittee. This is the top of
  the ladder and it is a multi-year, meaningful commitment of
  the chair's time and the enterprise's political capital.

The head's decision, per body, is which rung the enterprise
occupies. The decision is deliberate. An enterprise that is
"a contributor" on paper because the level-50 architect
occasionally reads a working document is at member on the
ladder, not contributor — the head reads the actual
contribution record before assigning the depth label.

## Who represents the enterprise — the head-versus-architect split

The specific question the head faces on every body is: does the
head personally hold the enterprise's seat at the leadership
level, or does the senior AI governance architect (level 50)
represent in technical-body sessions?

The split is not a matter of preference. The two roles are
different, and the wrong choice produces wrong outputs.

The **head** operates in leadership-scope venues — full
committee plenaries, chair-level meetings, cross-body
coordination bodies (e.g. the ISO/IEC / CEN-CENELEC Vienna
Agreement coordination), and any venue where the counterpart is
a peer head-of-standards at another enterprise or a national
representative. The head does not sit through technical
working-group sessions on control-family taxonomies. When the
head does, the head is representing the wrong role — the
technical detail belongs to the architect, and the head's
attendance signals that the enterprise does not have technical
depth to send.

The **level-50 architect** operates in working-group sessions,
technical editorial meetings, ballot-comment resolution
sessions, and any venue where the counterpart is a peer
technical contributor at another enterprise. The architect does
not sit in on chair-level political discussions about the
committee's forward plan — that is not the architect's
authority to speak into, and it is the head's meeting.

The head-versus-architect split is what allows the enterprise
to occupy leadership rungs (convenor, editor, chair) without
the head personally attending every meeting. The convenor role
is procedurally the enterprise's; the enterprise fields the
convenor from among its senior technical staff, with the head
providing the leadership-level backing and the accountability.
The head's involvement is the *portfolio ownership* — deciding
which convenor role the enterprise accepts, coordinating with
the architect on the technical resourcing, and holding the
convenor accountable for the deliverables.

The head *personally chairs* a working group — takes the
leadership rung directly — in a small number of cases: bodies
where the head's public profile is a strategic asset for the
enterprise (a high-visibility Union-level body); bodies where
the head has specific technical depth in a governance-focused
work item (an AIMS governance work item, for instance); bodies
where the enterprise's participation was previously chaired by
the head's predecessor and continuity matters. Otherwise, the
head backs the architect and does not front the working group.

## The standing standards-body portfolio

The bodies below are the current landscape as of the research
window. The portfolio is not fixed — new bodies emerge, existing
bodies restructure — but the shape of the portfolio is durable.

### ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42 — international AI standards

Subcommittee 42 of Joint Technical Committee 1 of ISO and IEC is
the international standardisation body for AI. SC 42's published
standards include ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management system), ISO/IEC
42005 (AI system impact assessment), ISO/IEC 42006 (requirements
for bodies providing audit and certification of AI management
systems), ISO/IEC 23894 (guidance on AI risk management), and
ISO/IEC 38507 (governance implications of the use of AI by
organisations), among others. Active work items cover
trustworthiness, evaluation, data quality, robustness, and
adjacent topics.

Engagement in SC 42 is through the enterprise's national body —
ANSI in the US, BSI in the UK, DIN in Germany, AFNOR in France,
JISC in Japan, and the equivalents elsewhere. Membership is
established via the national body's mirror committee (in BSI's
case, ART/1; in ANSI's case, INCITS AI as the primary
US Technical Advisory Group). The head's decision is which
mirror committee(s) to seat the enterprise on, at what depth,
and which SC 42 working groups the enterprise contributes to.

For enterprises whose AIMS is built against ISO/IEC 42001,
SC 42 participation is not optional at the *observer* rung; it
is the venue where the standard the enterprise's own AIMS is
tested against evolves. A member enterprise that is not
represented at SC 42 (via any national body) is downstream of
decisions that shape the enterprise's own certification exposure.

### CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 — European AI standards

Joint Technical Committee 21 of CEN and CENELEC produces the
European AI standards, including the harmonised standards
requested by the European Commission under standardisation
request M/593 in support of the EU AI Act. Harmonised standards,
once cited in the Official Journal of the EU, create a
presumption of conformity for the AI Act obligations they cover.

Engagement in JTC 21 is through the enterprise's national body,

non-EU-headquartered enterprises, participation is possible via
BSI (a full CEN-CENELEC member despite Brexit — CEN-CENELEC

subsidiaries' national bodies.

JTC 21 is a leadership-scope body for enterprises operating
in the EU. A harmonised standard that lands in the OJEU changes
what the enterprise's high-risk AI systems have to demonstrate
for conformity. The head who has the level-50 architect on the
relevant JTC 21 working group has visibility into the standard
six to twelve months before the standard is cited; the head who
does not learns about the standard from the OJEU citation and
from the industry press.

### IEEE 7000-series ethically-aligned design standards

The IEEE 7000-series is a family of standards on ethically-
aligned design of autonomous and intelligent systems, published
by the IEEE Standards Association. Published standards in the
series include IEEE 7000 (a process for engineers to address
ethical concerns during system design), IEEE 7001 (transparency
of autonomous systems), IEEE 7002 (data privacy process), IEEE
7003 (algorithmic bias considerations), IEEE 7010 (well-being
metrics for ethical AI), among others.

IEEE participation is through IEEE-SA individual or entity
membership; working-group participation is generally on an
individual basis. For enterprises whose products or services
are procured against IEEE-referenced specifications (common in
industrial, defence, and transportation sectors), participation
in the relevant working group is a supply-side discipline. For
enterprises whose exposure to IEEE 7000-series standards is
through customer-side procurement language, IEEE membership is
about *reading* the standards in draft to anticipate what
customers will start asking for.

### NIST public workshops and comment windows

NIST is not a standards body in the ISO / IEC / IEEE sense — it
is a US federal measurement science and standards agency — but
NIST's AI publications carry the practical weight of standards
in the US operating environment. The AI Risk Management
Framework (NIST AI 100-1), the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI
600-1), profile crosswalks, and the AI Safety Institute's
evaluation methodology publications all pass through public
workshop and comment cycles the enterprise can engage.

Engagement in NIST work happens on three surfaces:

- **Public workshops.** NIST convenes workshops (on-site,
  virtual, and hybrid) around specific work products. Attendance
  is generally open; substantive contribution (panel
  participation, submitted position papers) is at NIST's
  discretion.
- **Formal public-comment windows.** NIST publishes drafts for
  public comment via the AI Resource Center (AIRC) and Federal
  Register notices. Submitted comments are docketed publicly.
  The submission discipline is the one in
  [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 06.
- **AISIC working-group participation.** For enterprises that
  are AISIC members (chapter 01), the working-group structure
  is where technical contribution actually happens. AISIC
  membership is the sign-on decision; working-group
  participation is the execution against the sign-on.

The head's portfolio decision on NIST is *depth per work
product*, not blanket membership. The enterprise participates
in the workshops on work products directly relevant to its
operating exposure and observes the rest.

### OECD.AI Network of Experts on AI (ONE AI) and adjacent

The OECD.AI Policy Observatory hosts the Network of Experts on
Artificial Intelligence (ONE AI), which is composed of
government, industry, academia, and civil-society experts and
supports the OECD's AI work programme. ONE AI's expert groups
produce policy papers, contribute to G7 and other international
processes, and monitor implementation of the OECD AI
Principles.

### National mirror committees and the national-body layer

Every ISO / IEC / CEN-CENELEC standards work item is
represented, at the national level, by a mirror committee at
the national body — BSI's ART/1 for AI (mirror to SC 42 and to
JTC 21), DIN's NA 043-01-42 AA and adjacent committees, AFNOR's
CN CEN/CLC JTC 21 mirror committee, ANSI's INCITS AI (US TAG
to SC 42, and coordinating with the US position on JTC 21
outputs where applicable through the Vienna Agreement / Frankfurt
Agreement mechanisms), JISC's Japan mirrors, and equivalents in
other jurisdictions.

Engagement at the national mirror committee is often lower-cost
and more responsive than direct participation at SC 42 or JTC 21
plenaries. A national mirror committee seat gives the enterprise
a vote in shaping the national-body position that goes to the
international body, plus visibility into the pre-plenary
positioning of the national body. The head's default posture is
to have at least one named mirror-committee seat per body per
material operating jurisdiction; direct international
participation (plenary attendance, working-group convenor
roles) sits on top of the mirror committee seats, not instead of
them.

## The portfolio decision matrix

For each body on the portfolio, the head makes four decisions
and records them in the register.

1. **Depth on the ladder.** Observer / member / contributor /
   convenor or editor / chair. The choice is deliberate; it is
   revisited annually.
2. **Represented by.** Head personally, level-50 architect
   personally, named technical contributor with the architect's
   backing, or industry-body proxy (for bodies where the
   enterprise contributes through an industry association).
3. **Named seats and roles.** The specific working groups,
   editor roles, convenor roles, and mirror-committee seats the
   enterprise occupies. Named, not "one or two working groups".
4. **Feedback loop into the enterprise.** How outputs from the
   body flow back into the enterprise's own operating
   disciplines — the level-50 architect's crosswalk, the AIMS
   management review, the risk register, the audit-committee
   pack, the regulatory-change register. If the answer is
   "we're on the body but nothing flows back", the enterprise
   is at subscription, not participation.

The register lives alongside the voluntary-code register from
chapter 01. Together they are the enterprise's *external-
engagement portfolio* — the record that answers "what does the
enterprise participate in externally, at what depth, with
whom".

## Sustaining participation — the operating discipline

Standards work is a multi-year commitment. A working group that
publishes a Committee Draft in year one, resolves comments in
year two, votes to Draft International Standard in year three,
and publishes International Standard in year four is a normal
cadence. The head's participation discipline has to be
compatible with that timeline.

Concretely, the head runs the participation discipline against
three cadence points:

- **Annual portfolio review.** Once a year, the head reviews
  every body on the portfolio against the four decisions above:
  is the depth still right, is the representation still right,
  are the named seats still occupied, is the feedback loop
  running. Bodies drop off the portfolio (the standard is
  published, the working group closes, the enterprise's
  exposure has moved); new bodies come on. The review output
  goes to the audit committee as part of the annual external-
  engagement briefing.
- **Quarterly technical-contribution digest.** The level-50
  architect produces a quarterly digest of the enterprise's
  standards-body contributions — which drafts the enterprise
  commented on, which ballots the enterprise voted on, which
  working-group meetings named contributors attended, which
  work items are moving toward closure. The digest is one
  page. The head reads it. The digest is also the head's
  evidence when a certification body under ISO/IEC 42006 asks
  "does the enterprise engage in the standards work its AIMS
  is built against".
- **On-milestone briefing.** When a standard the enterprise is
  materially engaged with hits a governance milestone —
  Committee Draft ballot open, Draft International Standard
  ballot open, publication, OJEU citation (for JTC 21
  standards) — the level-50 architect briefs the head on the
  implications for the enterprise's own operating disciplines
  and the head decides whether the milestone triggers a
  reporting to the audit committee, an update to the crosswalk,
  or a communication to the CxO peer layer.

## The public-facing artefact

The enterprise's standards-body participation is a legitimate
public-trust asset (chapter 03 covers the public-trust posture
in detail). The head maintains a public-facing summary of the
portfolio — typically on the enterprise's AI-governance web
page or in the annual AI transparency report — that lists the
bodies the enterprise participates in, at what depth, and
with what named seats. The public summary is a subset of the
register (private-register fields like internal cost or
individual contributor names may or may not be in the public
summary), and it is honest: the enterprise does not claim
"leadership" in a body where it is at member.

Regulators, procurement counterparties, and customers read the
public summary. A public summary that overstates the
enterprise's participation is a credibility incident waiting
for the right journalist. A public summary that understates
the enterprise's participation is capital left on the table.
The head owns the accuracy of the summary the same way the
head owns the accuracy of an Article 73 filing.

## The one-sentence summary

Standards-body participation is a portfolio of depth choices
across bodies, each with a specific rung on the engagement
ladder, a specific representative pattern (head, architect,
contributor, or proxy), and a specific feedback loop into the
enterprise's operating disciplines. The head owns the
portfolio; the level-50 architect and named technical
contributors do the work; the portfolio is auditable and
publicly-defensible.

## Summary

- Standards bodies drafts the rules the enterprise later
  complies with. The head who has been at the drafting table
  shapes the rules; the head who has not lives with what
  others shaped.
- The engagement ladder has five rungs — observer, member,
  contributor, convenor or editor, chair — and the head's
  decision per body is deliberate, not default.
- The head does not personally attend technical working
  groups except in narrow cases (leadership-visibility bodies,
  governance-specific work items, continuity). The level-50
  senior AI governance architect and named technical
  contributors do the drafting; the head runs the portfolio
  and backs the working-group convenor when the enterprise
  fields one.
- The standing portfolio as of the research window covers
  ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, CEN-CENELEC JTC 21, IEEE 7000-series,
  NIST workshops (AI RMF, AISI, AISIC working groups), OECD.AI
  ONE AI, and the national mirror committees at BSI, DIN,
  AFNOR, ANSI, JISC, and equivalents.
- For each body, the head decides four things — depth on the
  ladder, represented by whom, named seats and roles, and the
  feedback loop into the enterprise's operating disciplines —
  and records them in the standards-body register.
- Sustaining participation runs on three cadences — annual
  portfolio review, quarterly technical-contribution digest,
  on-milestone briefing — that turn multi-year standards work
  into a governable operating rhythm.
- A public-facing summary of the portfolio is a legitimate
  trust asset; the summary is honest to the register (no
  overstatement, no understatement).
- Chapter 03 turns to the public-trust posture the standards-
  body portfolio is one input into; chapter 04 to the RFI and
  RFP response discipline that runs adjacent; chapter 05 to
  the harder decisions where the enterprise disagrees
  publicly with a body's output.
