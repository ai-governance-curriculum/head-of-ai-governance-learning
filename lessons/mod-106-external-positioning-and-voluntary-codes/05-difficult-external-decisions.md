# The Difficult External Decision — Declining, Withdrawing, and Disagreeing

## Motivation

The three preceding chapters covered the ordinary decisions:
signing a voluntary code that fits, taking a standards-body
seat that pays for itself, standing up a public-trust posture
that accumulates credibility, and responding to inbound
requests through a settled process. Those decisions are the
core of the head's external posture. But the harder decisions —
declining to sign a code peers are signing, withdrawing from a
code the enterprise had signed, and publicly disagreeing with a
regulator's methodology on a question the enterprise cares about
— are the ones that determine whether the enterprise's external
posture is coherent under pressure or performative under
pressure.

The head who has only run the ordinary decisions has not been
stress-tested. The first time the enterprise has to decline the
next iteration of a voluntary code because the code has moved
beyond what the enterprise can honestly commit to, or the first
time a code the enterprise signed a year ago has evolved in a
way the enterprise can no longer defend, or the first time a
regulator publishes a methodology the enterprise's evaluation
function has substantive objection to — the head has to have a
disciplined pattern to reach for. This chapter is that pattern.

The failure mode this chapter opens with is not the difficult
decision itself. It is the difficult decision made without
discipline: the enterprise quietly declining a code without
public rationale (which reads as opacity or as unwillingness),
the enterprise withdrawing from a code with a two-sentence
statement (which invites all the wrong inferences), or the
enterprise publicly criticising a regulator's methodology in a
way that reads as adversarial and burns the working
relationship [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapters 01
through 05 spent building. All three are avoidable, and all
three are avoided by the same discipline: name the position
substantively, frame the disagreement without adversarial
tone, and preserve the ordinary engagement surface.

## Core concept — the disagreement discipline

The head's discipline in a difficult external decision has four
moves. The moves apply in all three scenarios (declining,
withdrawing, disagreeing) with variations.

- **Name the position substantively.** The head's public
  position is a specific, technical, defensible substantive
  statement. Not "we disagree" or "we cannot support this at
  this time". A specific description of what the enterprise
  disagrees with, why, and what alternative the enterprise
  supports.
- **Frame the disagreement without adversarial tone.** The
  head disagrees with the position, not with the body or the
  regulator. The enterprise praises the body's work overall,
  identifies the specific point of disagreement, and closes
  with the enterprise's continued willingness to engage. The
  regulator or body reads the disagreement as substantive
  input, not as opposition.
- **Preserve the ordinary engagement surface.** The head's
  disagreement on one specific matter does not close the
  head's engagement across the rest of the surface. The head
  keeps the standing meetings, keeps the standing information
  flow, and keeps the standing collaboration. Regulators and
  peer bodies notice when an enterprise's disagreement on
  matter A becomes a general chill on the engagement; they
  notice when it does not. The head's discipline is to keep
  matter A specific to matter A.
- **Coordinate internally before disclosing externally.** The
  head's public disagreement is coordinated with the CEO, GC,
  CRO, and — where the disagreement is with a regulator — with
  Government Affairs, before the disagreement is made public.
  Internal coordination is not delay; it is the reason the
  external disagreement holds up under pressure. The head who
  disagrees publicly without internal alignment is a head who
  will be publicly walked back by another executive.

The four moves are the same across the three scenarios. What
differs is the ratification requirement (declining is a lighter
sign-off than withdrawing), the public-facing artefact
(declining is often silent-with-rationale-on-file; withdrawing
is public with statement; publicly disagreeing with a
regulator is public with substantive alternative), and the
follow-on operating cost (a decline needs monitoring; a
withdrawal needs an unwind plan; a regulator disagreement needs
a sustained engagement follow-up).

## Scenario 1 — Declining to sign a voluntary code

The head's discipline for a decline decision builds on the
chapter-01 process (attachment analysis, cost estimate,
appetite check, legal exposure, public-positioning read, head
recommendation, sign-off). The additional moves for the
difficult case — where peer enterprises are signing and the
enterprise's decline is publicly visible — are three.

### The substantive rationale

The head's rationale for declining has to be substantive, not
procedural. "We are not signing because we have not yet
completed our review" is procedural (and true only for a
window; if it is still true a year later, it has become the
disguised rationale for a rejection). "We are not signing
because commitment C in the code would attach to system class
X in our portfolio in a way we do not currently have the
evaluation methodology to defend" is substantive.

Substantive rationales fall into a small set of families:

- **Attachment mismatch.** The code's commitments attach to
  scope the enterprise does not currently operate in, or
  attach in a way the enterprise's specific operating shape
  cannot cleanly meet. Common for enterprises whose product
  shape is atypical of the code's imagined signatory.
- **Evaluation-methodology gap.** A commitment requires
  evaluation the enterprise does not currently run and cannot
  add before the code's expected reporting cadence starts.
- **Cost / opportunity conflict.** The commitment is honestly
  costly enough that the enterprise cannot fund it without
  displacing work the enterprise's other governance
  disciplines require. The CRO and CFO make this call, not the
  head.
- **Governance-conflict with existing commitments.** The
  commitment would require the enterprise to take a public
  position that contradicts a commitment the enterprise has
  already made (in another voluntary code, in a regulatory
  filing, in a customer contract).
- **Principled disagreement with the code's approach.** The
  enterprise disagrees with the code's methodology or approach
  on grounds the enterprise is willing to defend publicly.
  This is the rarest and hardest family; chapter 03's principled-
  position lens applies.

The head selects the family, drafts the substantive rationale
in the chosen family's terms, and records the rationale on the
register. If the decline attracts external questioning, the
rationale is what the head speaks to.

### The public-facing artefact

Declines are, by default, silent on the register — the
enterprise does not publicly announce every code it did not
sign, because doing so would read as attention-seeking. But
declines *become public* the moment a journalist, a customer,
or a peer asks. The head prepares for that moment before it
happens.

The public-facing artefact for a decline is:

- **A one-paragraph statement**, ready to be issued if asked,
  that names the code, states that the enterprise considered
  it and declined, gives the substantive rationale in one
  sentence, and closes with the enterprise's continued
  engagement on the underlying subject matter through other
  surfaces (regulator engagement, standards-body work, other
  codes the enterprise is signed to).
- **A longer explanatory position paper**, if the decline is
  material enough that the one-paragraph statement will
  invite follow-up. Not required for every decline; required
  when the enterprise expects follow-up.
- **The public-facing register entry**, if the enterprise's
  voluntary-code register (chapter 01) is publicly summarised
  on the enterprise's AI-governance channel (chapter 03), the
  decline appears on the summary with the rationale in
  compressed form.

The one-paragraph statement is head-approved and pre-cleared
with Communications and GC. Communications delivers it if the
question arrives; the head does not draft on the spot.

### The internal monitoring

A decline is not a one-time decision. The code will evolve;
peer signatory rosters will change; the underlying substance
may become mandatory in a jurisdiction the enterprise operates
in. The head's decline decision carries a *monitoring
commitment*: the head, or the level-50 architect, tracks the
code's evolution and revisits the decision on a scheduled
cadence (annual by default, more often if the code is moving
fast) or on a scheduled trigger (a peer with similar operating
shape signs; the underlying substance becomes regulatorily
material).

The register carries the monitoring commitment. A decline
decision that has not been revisited in three years is a
decline the head has to defend against the counterfactual of
"has the underlying context changed enough that the decline is
no longer the right decision"; the register makes that defence
possible.

## Scenario 2 — Withdrawing from a voluntary code

Withdrawal is harder than declining. The enterprise signed at
one point, made public commitments, and now has to reverse the
public commitment. Peers, regulators, journalists, and
customers who read the sign-on now read the withdrawal, and the
read is inherently sceptical: what changed, what did the
enterprise fail to deliver, is this a signal about the
enterprise's governance program overall.

The head's discipline for a withdrawal decision has four moves
on top of the general disagreement discipline.

### The trigger

Withdrawal is triggered by a specific set of conditions the
head recorded on the sign-on register (chapter 01, step 6 of
the internal decision process — the *condition set*). Common
triggers:

- **The code has evolved beyond what the enterprise committed
  to.** The signatory community has voted in additional
  commitments the enterprise did not originally commit to and
  cannot honestly commit to now.
- **The enterprise's operating shape has changed.** A
  divestiture, an acquisition, a strategic pivot has moved
  the enterprise out of the scope the code was designed for.
- **The regulatory context has changed.** A mandatory
  regulation has landed that covers the same substance the
  code covered; continued voluntary signatory status adds no
  marginal value and creates confusion about which surface
  is authoritative.
- **The enterprise has honestly failed to deliver on a
  commitment.** The enterprise cannot meet a specific
  commitment on the code's reporting cadence and, rather than
  concealing the failure, is withdrawing in advance of the
  reporting.
- **The signatory community has moved in a direction the
  enterprise disagrees with.** A body's leadership or member
  decisions have taken the community to positions the
  enterprise cannot defensibly stand with.

Each family has a different public-facing implication. The
enterprise-shape-change family is the easiest to explain (the
enterprise's operating shape has moved, and the code no
longer fits). The failure-to-deliver family is the hardest
(the enterprise has to be honest about the failure without
inviting broader inference); chapter 05 of
[[mod-110-incident-leadership]] governs the failure-
disclosure discipline where the failure has incident
character.

### The unwind plan

Withdrawal is not instantaneous. A code with a public
reporting cadence has cycles the enterprise's withdrawal has to
be sequenced against. A code with member obligations (data-
sharing, committee attendance, review responsibility) has
handoffs the enterprise's withdrawal has to complete. A code
whose sign-on carried a commitment period (some codes have
minimum membership terms) has the term to satisfy or the exit
provisions to invoke.

The unwind plan is drafted by the level-50 architect with the
operating owner of the code (chapter 01, step 6), reviewed by
GC (contractual and public-facing exposure), and approved by
the head. The plan has:

- **The last cycle** — the last reporting cycle the enterprise
  participates in, the last committee meeting, the last data-
  sharing round.
- **The handoffs** — what the enterprise passes back to the
  body's secretariat or to the successor entity if any.
- **The public timing** — when the withdrawal is announced,
  synchronised with the last cycle so the announcement is
  neither premature (leaving the enterprise mid-cycle with a
  known lame-duck posture) nor delayed (leaving the enterprise
  bound in effect after it has decided).
- **The customer-facing communication** — for customer
  contracts that referenced the enterprise's signatory status
  (increasingly common in AI-governance-forward procurement),
  the specific communication to those customers.
- **The public-facing artefact** — the withdrawal statement,
  in the form described below.

### The public-facing artefact

The withdrawal statement is the head-authored, CxO-signed,
CEO-approved public-facing document. It has five components:

- **Recognition of the code's value.** The enterprise
  acknowledges what the code has contributed and honours the
  work of its convenors and other signatories.
- **The specific reason for withdrawal.** The trigger family
  named substantively. "Following the enterprise's divestiture
  of business unit X in Q3, the enterprise's remaining AI
  system portfolio no longer includes the scope commitment C
  was designed for" is substantive. "Following recent changes
  in the enterprise's strategic priorities" is not
  substantive.
- **What the enterprise will continue to do.** The commitments
  the enterprise upholds through other surfaces — regulatory
  compliance, other voluntary codes, standards-body work,
  the enterprise's own AI-governance publishing channel.
- **The unwind timing.** When the withdrawal takes effect and
  what the enterprise will do in the intervening period to
  honour outstanding commitments.
- **Continued willingness to engage.** The enterprise remains
  in dialogue with the body's convenors and with other
  signatories on the underlying subject matter; withdrawal is
  from the specific commitment framework, not from the
  substantive conversation.

The statement is one page, sometimes two. Longer statements
signal the enterprise is uncomfortable with the decision;
shorter statements signal the enterprise is dismissive of the
code. One page, five components, is the discipline.

### The internal debrief

After the withdrawal is public, the head runs a debrief with
the CxO peer layer and the audit-committee chair. What did the
enterprise learn from the sign-on decision that would improve
future sign-on decisions? What did the withdrawal cost the
enterprise (headcount for the unwind, external reputation,
customer conversations)? What is the register entry that
carries this history for the head's successor?

The debrief is a governance artefact. Withdrawal is common
enough at enterprise scale that the head's successor will
face the same class of decision. The debrief is the record
that improves the successor's decision.

## Scenario 3 — Publicly disagreeing with a regulator's methodology

The third difficult decision is the one where the enterprise
publicly disagrees with a regulator's substantive methodology
on a matter the enterprise cares enough about to disagree
publicly. Examples of the *class* of matter: a regulator's
proposed evaluation methodology for a class of models the
enterprise operates, a regulator's proposed measurement
approach for a specific risk (bias, safety, robustness) the
enterprise's own methodology addresses differently, a
regulator's proposed transparency requirement the enterprise
believes is technically infeasible or counter-productive.

The head does not treat these as ordinary consultation
submissions ([[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 06). The
ordinary submission cycle is where the enterprise's
disagreement enters the record; but a *public* disagreement — a
position paper, a keynote, a media quote — is a different
posture the enterprise has to specifically ratify.

### The threshold decision

Not every disagreement is worth taking public. The head
threshold-tests the disagreement against three questions.

- **Is the enterprise's substantive position actually
  distinctive?** If the enterprise's position is the same as
  the industry-common position ([[mod-104-regulator-
  engagement]] chapter 06's industry-common lens), the
  disagreement is better carried by an industry body's
  submission and the enterprise co-signs. The enterprise
  disagrees publicly under its own name only where the
  substance is distinctive.
- **Is the disagreement substantive enough to warrant the
  relationship cost?** Public disagreement with a regulator's
  methodology costs relationship capital
  ([[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapters 01 through 05
  is the running relationship). If the substance is
  peripheral to the enterprise's operating exposure, the
  cost may exceed the value. The head weighs both.
- **Is the enterprise's alternative technically defensible?**
  Publicly disagreeing with a regulator's methodology
  requires the enterprise to have an alternative that stands
  up under peer and regulator scrutiny. Disagreement without
  a defensible alternative reads as obstruction.

Where the answer to all three is yes, the disagreement is a
candidate for public expression. Where any is no, the
disagreement stays in the ordinary submission channel.

### The framing discipline

Public disagreement with a regulator is framed as *substantive
engagement*, not as opposition. Five discipline points:

- **Speak to the substance, not the regulator.** The
  enterprise disagrees with the methodology, not with the
  regulator's judgement or its motives. The regulator is
  positioned as the counterparty in a shared substantive
  conversation.
- **Cite the enterprise's own work.** The disagreement is
  grounded in the enterprise's own evaluation methodology,
  its own operating experience, its own published research or
  standards-body contribution. Disagreement grounded in "we
  think" is discounted; disagreement grounded in "our
  experience running system class X across N deployments
  shows" is substantive.
- **Offer a specific alternative.** The disagreement includes
  a specific alternative proposal — a modification to the
  methodology, an addition to it, or an exception mechanism.
  Disagreement without a proposal is criticism, and
  criticism absent proposal is what "burns the relationship"
  actually means.
- **Acknowledge the regulator's mandate.** The enterprise
  recognises that the regulator has the authority to make
  the final decision, that the enterprise will comply with
  the final rule, and that the enterprise's input is offered
  in support of the regulator's process, not as a demand.
- **Coordinate with peer disagreement.** Where peer
  enterprises share the enterprise's substantive position,
  the enterprise coordinates so that the disagreement is not
  read by the regulator as isolated obstruction. Where the
  enterprise is genuinely alone in its substantive position,
  the head explains internally why the enterprise still
  believes the position is right — a principled-lens
  disagreement is legitimate but rare.

### The follow-up discipline

Public disagreement is not a one-time move. After the
disagreement is on the record — in a position paper, in a
consultation submission, in a keynote — the head runs a
follow-up cadence with the regulator:

- **The direct-engagement update.** Within a defined window
  after publication (four to eight weeks), the head requests a
  direct engagement with the regulator's counterpart — a
  private meeting or call — to walk through the disagreement
  in more detail than the public form allowed. The head is
  not lobbying against the rule; the head is offering the
  enterprise's substance in a form the regulator can absorb
  operationally.
- **The methodology contribution.** If the enterprise's
  alternative methodology is technically substantive, the
  enterprise offers to contribute the methodology's
  technical documentation to the regulator's process — under
  an appropriate confidentiality arrangement, coordinated
  with GC.
- **The ongoing engagement surface.** The head continues to
  attend the regulator's public workshops, respond to the
  regulator's RFIs (chapter 04), and participate in the
  standards-body work the regulator draws on. The
  disagreement on one methodology does not close the head's
  standing engagement across the surface.

The head who runs the follow-up discipline turns a public
disagreement into a substantive contribution the regulator
absorbs (or does not, but with awareness). The head who
publishes the disagreement and then goes silent turns the
disagreement into obstruction the regulator files away.

## The chapter's compression

The three scenarios differ in the ratification, the artefact,
and the follow-up, but the underlying discipline is the same:
substantive rationale, non-adversarial framing, preservation of
the ordinary surface, and coordinated internal alignment before
public disclosure. A head who has run one scenario disciplined
can run the others; a head who has run none has to run the
first live and hope for the best.

The exercise for this chapter (exercise 05) rehearses one
scenario in each family so the head has muscle memory before
the live event.

## Summary

- The difficult external decisions — declining to sign a
  voluntary code, withdrawing from a code the enterprise had
  signed, and publicly disagreeing with a regulator's
  methodology — are what determine whether the enterprise's
  external posture is coherent under pressure or performative
  under pressure.
- The general disagreement discipline is four moves —
  substantive naming, non-adversarial framing, preservation
  of the ordinary surface, and internal coordination before
  external disclosure — and applies across the three
  scenarios with variation.
- Declining to sign has a substantive rationale (one of five
  families), a pre-prepared public-facing artefact (one-
  paragraph statement plus explanatory paper if warranted),
  and a monitoring commitment for revisit.
- Withdrawing from a code has a trigger from the sign-on
  register's condition set, an unwind plan (last cycle,
  handoffs, public timing, customer-facing communication),
  a five-component public statement, and an internal debrief.
- Publicly disagreeing with a regulator's methodology passes
  a three-question threshold (substantive distinctiveness,
  relationship-cost warrant, technically defensible
  alternative), follows a five-point framing discipline
  (substance over regulator, cite own work, offer alternative,
  acknowledge mandate, coordinate with peers), and runs a
  follow-up discipline (direct-engagement update, methodology
  contribution, ongoing engagement).
- The enterprise's difficult-decision discipline preserves
  the running regulator relationships in
  [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapters 01 through 05 and
  the running voluntary-code portfolio in chapter 01 of this
  module. Disagreement on matter A does not close engagement
  across the surface.
- Chapter 06 turns to the shape lessons the enterprise draws
  from frontier-lab external positioning — Anthropic RSP,
  OpenAI Preparedness Framework, Google DeepMind FSF — where
  the difficult-decision discipline has been rehearsed at
  frontier scale in ways the enterprise can learn from
  without copying wholesale.
