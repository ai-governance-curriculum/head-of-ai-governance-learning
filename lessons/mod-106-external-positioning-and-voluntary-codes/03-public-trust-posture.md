# The Public Trust Posture

## Motivation

The head of AI governance is a public figure. Not because the
head sought publicity, but because the role's substance is
inherently public: the enterprise's regulatory postures land in
public dockets, the voluntary-code sign-ons land on public
press releases, the standards-body seats land in public
membership rosters, and the audit-committee visibility of the
governance program is a matter public shareholders read about
in the annual report. The head's *public trust posture* is
what those readers see when they look for the enterprise's AI-
governance leadership.

That posture is not the enterprise's marketing surface. The
marketing surface is what the enterprise says about its own AI
products; the public trust posture is what the head says about
the enterprise's AI governance. The two are related, but the
distinction matters — a head who is quoted saying nice things
about the enterprise's chatbot is off-role, and the enterprise
loses the trust-building surface. A head who is quoted saying
substantive things about how the enterprise decides which
systems to build and which to retire is on-role, and the
enterprise gains the trust-building surface.

The failure mode this chapter opens with is the head who has no
public posture at all — no speaking track record, no position
papers under the head's name, no board seat on an industry
body, no relationship with the trade press or the general
business press. That head is a private figure operating in a
public role, and when the enterprise faces a public trust
event — an incident, a regulatory action against a peer that
raises questions, a landing rule the enterprise disagrees with
— the head has no accumulated credibility to spend. The head's
first appearance is the incident briefing, and the audience is
seeing the head for the first time under adverse conditions.
The audience discounts what a stranger says.

The opposite failure mode is the head who has too much of a
public posture — a personal brand that has drifted away from
the enterprise, a speaking calendar that Communications is
scrambling to coordinate around, a media presence that reads as
the head auditioning for the next role. That head is on-role in
name but off-role in effect, and the enterprise loses the
signal a proportionate public posture would send.

This chapter builds the deliberate posture between the two
failure modes. Chapter 01 covered voluntary-code sign-on;
chapter 02 covered standards-body participation. Both feed the
public posture: sign-ons and standards seats are public artefacts
the head then either speaks to or does not. The posture is the
head's decision about what to make of the artefacts.

## Core concept — substance ownership, delivery ownership

The load-bearing distinction in this chapter is the split
between substance and delivery.

The **substance** — what the enterprise says about its AI
governance in public — is the head's ownership. Communications
does not decide the enterprise's position on Article 55 GPAI
obligations. The CFO does not decide the enterprise's position
on the pace of the NIST AI RMF's next revision. The CMO does
not decide the enterprise's position on the interpretation of
Colorado SB 24-205. Those positions are the head's, coordinated
with the CxO adjacencies in [[mod-101-role-scope-and-leadership-
shape]] chapter 06 and with GC per
[[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 07.

The **delivery** — how the substance reaches the public — is
Communications' ownership. Which speaking invitation the head
accepts, which venue matches which message, how the head is
briefed for a specific interview, what the visual identity of a
position paper is, how the position paper is launched (blog
post, LinkedIn, press outreach), how the head is trained for
on-record media, and how a press incident affecting the
enterprise's governance posture is coordinated across the
executive team — all of that is Communications' craft.

The head who tries to own delivery becomes a bad
Communications leader and a distracted head. The head who lets
Communications own substance becomes a bad head — the
enterprise's public governance positions drift toward what is
easy to communicate rather than what the enterprise actually
believes and can defend. The discipline is that both roles
respect the boundary.

The joint operating agreement between the head and the CMO /
Chief Communications Officer is the artefact that keeps the
boundary in place. It parallels the head-and-GC joint operating
agreement in [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 07 and the
head-and-CFO agreement referenced in the sign-off flow in
chapter 01 of this module. The head, the CCO, and the CEO ratify
the agreement; the audit-committee chair sees it.

## The public trust portfolio

The head's public posture is a portfolio of five surfaces. Each
surface has an operating cadence, an accepted range of the
head's time, and a specific value proposition to the enterprise
and to the head's own credibility.

### Public speaking

The head speaks in public — at industry conferences, at
regulator convenings that admit industry speakers, at trade
association meetings, at academic events, and (rarely) at
general-audience venues. Not all of these are the same.

- **Industry-convened venues.** Trade association annual
  meetings, sector-specific AI governance conferences (banking-
  sector AI risk conferences, health AI conferences, defence AI
  governance venues, etc.), and cross-industry AI governance
  conferences. The head is speaking to peers; the audience
  knows the domain; the substance can go deep. Communications
  coordinates the invitation, the head runs the substance.
- **Regulator-convened venues.** NIST public workshops
  ([[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 06 and chapter 02
  of this module), EU AI Office public events, sector-regulator
  industry-day events, AISI public technical events. The head
  is speaking on-record in a venue the regulator is watching.
  The head's substance has to be settled position, coordinated
  with GC and with the level-50 architect, and delivered under
  the same discipline as an examination room appearance.
- **Academic and civil-society venues.** Universities, think-
  tank convenings, civil-society AI conferences. The audience
  is more sceptical of enterprise motives; the head is expected
  to engage substantively with critical perspectives; the
  benefit is a more informed and credibility-building
  positioning.
- **General-audience venues.** Business press conferences, tech
  conferences with mixed audiences, general-audience media. The
  head accepts these sparingly; the return is often less than
  the coordination cost, and the risk of off-message delivery
  is higher. Communications' judgement drives most of these
  decisions.

The head's speaking cadence is a Communications-coordinated
calendar. The default is a *bounded, deliberate* cadence — a
small number of substantive appearances per year at venues
where the head's presence advances a specific position — not
an open acceptance rate. The head who accepts every invitation
is a head who is on the road; the head on the road is not
running the program.

### Position papers

The head authors, or co-authors under the head's name, position
papers on specific AI-governance topics. Not the enterprise's
marketing white paper on why the enterprise's product is good;
the head's substantive position on a specific question the
enterprise's leadership team has settled a view on.

A position paper is one to five pages, published on the
enterprise's AI-governance channel and often co-published with
a peer body (industry association, standards body, academic
institution), and named to the head. It has a takeaway the head
would defend at a Senate hearing or at a national-body meeting.

Position papers are rare and considered. The head does not
publish a position paper monthly; the head publishes when the
enterprise has a position that has taken deliberate work to
form and that will be useful to the field. A position paper
that is content marketing dressed as substantive contribution is
a credibility incident waiting to be caught.

The internal process to author a position paper is the same
seven-step shape as the voluntary-code sign-on (chapter 01):
attachment analysis, cost estimate (Communications' publishing
cost plus the head's time), appetite check on any risk-of-
disagreement-with-peer positions the paper takes, legal
exposure read from GC, public-positioning read from
Communications and Government Affairs, the head's
recommendation, and sign-off. The sign-off room is typically
the head, GC, the CCO, and (for high-visibility papers) the
CEO. The audit-committee chair sees the papers on the annual
external-engagement briefing.

### Industry-body board seats

The head sits on the board or leadership committee of one or
more industry bodies — the sector's trade association, the
cross-sector AI governance associations (BSA | The Software
Alliance, ITI, Chamber of Digital Commerce, the equivalents in
other jurisdictions), or the multi-stakeholder bodies (PAI, FMF
where the enterprise is a member — see chapter 01).

Board seats are meaningful commitments. A board seat that shows
up on a public roster commits the head to attendance,
substantive contribution, and shared responsibility for the
body's positions. The head does not accept a board seat unless
the head is going to do the work — a nominal seat is worse than
no seat, because the seat is visible.

The head's board-seat portfolio is a Government Affairs and CxO
peer-coordinated decision. Government Affairs owns the
enterprise's overall industry-body strategy and knows which
bodies matter for which regulatory-adjacent purposes. The head
takes the seats that are governance-substantive; Government
Affairs takes the seats that are policy-lobbying-substantive
(and either the head of Government Affairs or a delegate holds
those). The two portfolios coordinate but are separately owned.

### Media strategy

The head's on-record media presence — quotes in Financial
Times / Wall Street Journal / Bloomberg / Reuters coverage of
AI regulation, deeper interviews with trade press (Politico,
Axios, MIT Technology Review, and the equivalents in other
markets), podcast appearances on serious AI-policy podcasts, and
occasional broadcast appearances (rare) — is Communications-
coordinated end-to-end.

The head's discipline in media is *stay on substance, stay on
role*. The head does not comment on the enterprise's revenue or
its share price. The head does not comment on live litigation
or examinations. The head does not opine on peer enterprises'
governance postures. The head speaks to the enterprise's
governance program, to the head's read of the public regulatory
landscape, and to the head's positions in publicly-filed
material (position papers, consultation submissions,
standards-body work).

Every on-record media interaction goes through the same three
prep steps:

- **Briefing** — Communications assembles the interview
  parameters, prior related coverage, and the reporter's known
  positions.
- **Substantive prep** — the head, with the level-50 architect
  where technical detail is at stake, works up the answers to
  the substantive questions expected. The head is *practising*,
  not reading; media that reads as scripted-corporate is media
  that gets discounted.
- **Post-interview capture** — Communications logs what the
  head said, on what topics, and cross-references it to the
  archive of the enterprise's public positions. Discrepancies
  between what the head said and prior public position trigger
  a review before the story publishes if possible, and a
  correction / clarification protocol if not.

Off-record and background media happen only under a formal
Communications framework, and the head defaults to on-record
unless there is a specific Communications-approved reason
otherwise. Off-record with a reporter the head does not know is
a category error the head does not make.

### The enterprise's own AI-governance publishing channel

The enterprise's own website hosts an AI-governance publishing
channel — a landing page for the head's positions, the
enterprise's transparency reports, the enterprise's voluntary-
code sign-ons (chapter 01), the enterprise's standards-body
participation summary (chapter 02), and the enterprise's
regulator-facing filings (Article 72 filings, US AISI
disclosures, and equivalents where appropriate).

This channel is the head's own artefact. It is the archive a
future reader (Communications' hypothetical of the future
regulator, the future journalist, the future customer's
procurement lead — the four cold readers from
[[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 06) consults to form a
view of the enterprise's AI-governance posture over time.

The channel is Communications-managed for delivery (site design,
SEO, accessibility) and head-owned for substance (what goes on
it, how each item is framed, what the takeaway of each is).
Additions to the channel go through the same one-page
recommendation and CxO sign-off routing as any other public
posture item. The channel is *not* the enterprise's marketing
site's AI page; the two live at different URLs and speak in
different registers.

## The annual public-trust plan

Every year, the head and the CCO co-author a one-page annual
public-trust plan. The plan lays out:

- The **calendar of intended surfaces** — which speaking
  venues the head expects to accept, which position papers the
  head expects to publish, which industry-body board seats the
  head is committing to, which media relationships the head is
  cultivating.
- The **budget** — the head's time (measured in days per
  quarter, honestly) and the Communications resource commitment
  (event coordination, position-paper editorial, media
  training, and channel maintenance).
- The **positioning arc** — the theme the head's substance
  builds through the year. A head who takes on a specific set
  of related positions across the year — on GPAI governance, on
  measurement of AI safety practice, on the AIMS discipline, on
  the head-and-GC boundary as an operating pattern — builds a
  cumulative substantive presence. A head whose speaking
  calendar is a random walk builds nothing.
- The **coordination points** with the CEO's own public
  posture, with the CTO's technical public presence, with
  Government Affairs' policy-lobbying presence, and with GC's
  litigation-adjacent silence discipline. The head does not
  say things the CEO is about to say; the head does not opine
  on matters GC has said the enterprise will not comment on
  publicly.

The plan is a working artefact, not a rigid script. Events
change the plan; a peer's incident that shifts the public
conversation makes some planned appearances more urgent and
others less relevant. The plan is what the head and the CCO
adjust against, not a plan the head defends by executing when
the circumstances have moved.

## The response-to-events discipline

Half of the head's public posture is planned (the calendar
above); the other half is reactive — the head is asked to
comment on an event the enterprise did not plan for. A peer
enterprise suffers a serious AI incident. A regulator publishes
enforcement material with facts resembling the enterprise's
own. A landing rule the enterprise had watched but not
publicly commented on becomes front-page news. A civil-society
report criticises the enterprise's governance program by name.

The head's discipline in reactive events has three moves.

- **The pause.** No public statement until the head, GC, and
  Communications have jointly settled the enterprise's
  position. The head does not fill a Communications-created
  silence unprompted; the head fills a substantive silence with
  a settled position.
- **The layered response.** The response is layered — a first
  short statement (Communications delivers, head substance),
  followed by a fuller statement on the enterprise's channel if
  the event merits (head authors, Communications delivers),
  followed by a substantive position paper if the event opens
  a durable question the enterprise's leadership should engage
  (head authors, chapter-03 process).
- **The peer coordination.** Where the event involves peers
  (an incident at another enterprise, a regulator action
  affecting the sector), the head coordinates with peer heads-
  of-role via the industry-body channels and the standards-
  body network the head has built. Peer coordination is not
  collusion; it is a legitimate discipline to avoid the
  enterprise's response contradicting an industry-common
  position without deliberate reason. GC watches this
  coordination for antitrust exposure, per the standing GC
  boundary.

The response discipline is what makes the head's ordinary
public presence a *load-bearing* asset when events break —
the head is a known voice with an established substantive
posture, and the enterprise's response is heard as continuous
with that posture rather than as manufactured for the event.

## What the head does not confuse

Three category errors the head does not make:

- The head's public posture is not the enterprise's marketing.
  The head does not sell the product; the head speaks to the
  governance. The two are on different URLs, in different
  registers, with different executives.
- The head's public posture is not the head's personal brand.
  The head's speaking track record accumulates over the head's
  career; the head is not building a personal brand at the
  enterprise's expense. When the head leaves the enterprise,
  the enterprise-role posture ends and the head starts fresh
  in the next role. A head who has been building a personal
  brand rather than a role-shaped posture leaves an
  enterprise that has to rebuild from zero and takes with them
  a brand that has confused the enterprise and the head.
- The head's public posture is not GC's opinion. The head
  does not opine on legal questions in public. When a
  reporter asks whether a specific practice is lawful, the
  head's answer names the enterprise's practice and says GC
  is the enterprise's authoritative voice on legality. This
  is the same discipline as
  [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 07, transposed to
  the public forum.

## Summary

- The head is a public figure by virtue of the role, not by
  choice. The public trust posture is what a reader looking
  for the enterprise's AI-governance leadership sees.
- The head owns substance; Communications owns delivery. The
  joint operating agreement between head and CCO, ratified by
  the CEO, is the artefact that keeps the boundary in place.
- The posture is a portfolio of five surfaces — public
  speaking (industry, regulator-convened, academic / civil
  society, general audience), position papers (rare and
  considered), industry-body board seats (governance-
  substantive, coordinated with Government Affairs), media
  strategy (Communications-coordinated end-to-end, always on
  substance), and the enterprise's own AI-governance
  publishing channel (head substance, Communications
  delivery).
- Position papers run through the same seven-step process as
  voluntary-code sign-on (chapter 01), with sign-off routing
  to head, GC, CCO, and CEO for high-visibility papers.
- The head's on-record media discipline is three prep steps
  every time — briefing, substantive prep, post-interview
  capture — with defaults on-record and off-record only under
  a formal Communications framework.
- The annual public-trust plan is a one-page artefact the
  head and CCO co-author, covering surface calendar, budget,
  positioning arc, and coordination points with CEO / CTO /
  Government Affairs / GC.
- The reactive-event discipline is a pause, a layered response,
  and peer coordination — with GC watching peer coordination
  for antitrust exposure.
- The head does not confuse public posture with marketing,
  with personal brand, or with legal opinion. Chapter 04
  turns to the RFI and RFP response discipline that runs
  adjacent to the public posture; chapter 05 to the hardest
  public postures — declining, withdrawing, and disagreeing
  publicly with a regulator.
