# The Annual Maturity Assessment

## Motivation

The maturity model in chapter [[02-authoring-the-maturity-model]]
is a document. The annual maturity assessment is the discipline
that turns the model into a *plot* the board and audit committee
read once a year — and, more importantly, that turns the plot
into a multi-year *trajectory* the audit committee ratifies and
that the CFO carries into the investment planning cycle.

An assessment run poorly is worse than an assessment not run.
The head who reports a score without a rigorous scoring
discipline, or a score without independent challenge, or a
score that has drifted quietly upward year over year without
evidence of program improvement, has produced a *plot* — but
the plot is decoration. When a certification-body auditor or a
regulator later tests the score against the evidence, the score
either survives or the head's credibility does not.

This chapter is the assessment discipline. The four moves —
self-assessment, second-line challenge, third-line audit input,
external benchmarking — combine to produce a plot the audit
committee ratifies as the basis for the following year's
investment. Chapter [[04-roi-reporting-to-cfo]] carries the
ROI framing that the trajectory anchors on; chapter
[[06-difficult-measurement-conversations]] rehearses the
conversations when the plot regresses or a peer overtakes.

The failure mode this chapter opens with is the head who runs
the assessment as a solo exercise, produces the plot from the
level-50 architect's scoring alone, and presents it to the audit
committee as "the assessment". That plot has one point of view,
no independent challenge, and no peer context. It is not an
assessment; it is a *self-report*. The chapter is the discipline
that makes it an assessment.

## Core concept — the four voices of a defensible assessment

A defensible maturity plot carries four distinct voices. Each
voice contributes something the other three cannot. Missing any
of them leaves a specific class of gap the discipline is
designed to close.

- **The self-assessment voice.** The head's function scores
  itself against the model. This is the primary voice — the
  head's function knows its own operation and evidence base
  better than any external party. The gap this voice cannot
  close on its own is *bias*: the function scoring itself has a
  standing interest in the scores being defensible.
- **The second-line challenge voice.** The CRO's function, or
  the head of enterprise risk management, challenges the self-
  assessment scores. This is independent within the enterprise
  but not independent of the enterprise. It closes the gap of
  self-serving scoring by pressing the head's function on
  evidence that would not withstand a peer's read.
- **The third-line audit input voice.** The chief audit
  executive (CAE) provides assurance on the assessment — not
  necessarily rescoring, but testing that the evidence set
  behind the scores is sound. This closes the gap of institutional
  bias by adding a voice that is independent of both the head
  and the CRO.
- **The external benchmarking voice.** Peer institutions in
  the enterprise's sector — and, where the enterprise is willing,
  a benchmarking consortium or peer-exchange group — provide
  the context that the enterprise's plot cannot generate from
  inside the enterprise. This closes the gap of *relative*
  position: a level-4 score means one thing when peers are at
  level 2 and something else when peers are at level 5.

The four voices are complementary. The head runs all four.
Skipping any of them produces a plot that is legible from one
angle only, and audit committees under stress read plots from
multiple angles.

## The annual assessment calendar

The assessment sits on the AIMS operating calendar
([[mod-102-running-the-program]] chapter 02) at a fixed point in
the year. Typical placement is Q4 — early enough that the
outputs feed the next year's objectives and risk-treatment plan
refresh in Q1, late enough that the current year's evidence base
is substantially complete.

A worked calendar for a Q4 assessment:

- **Month –4 (early Q3).** Model-version review. The level-50
  architect confirms the model the assessment will score against
  — axes in scope, tier definitions, sector overlays, target
  levels. Material changes ratified at the AI Governance Council
  and, where the changes touch the audit committee's line of
  sight, ratified at the audit committee.
- **Month –3.** Self-assessment window opens. The level-50
  architect begins scoring per axis and per cell against current
  evidence. The level-15 analyst runs the evidence-completeness
  pipeline that supports the scoring
  ([[01-kri-kpi-portfolio]] metric 8).
- **Month –2.** Self-assessment draft complete. The head reviews;
  the draft goes to the second-line challenge process.
- **Month –1.** Second-line challenge closes. The head packages
  the challenged draft for third-line CAE assurance and, in
  parallel, closes the external-benchmarking exchange.
- **Assessment month.** Audit-committee deep-dive presentation
  and ratification of the trajectory
  ([[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 02).
- **Month +1 (early Q1).** Trajectory into next year's
  objectives and risk-treatment plan
  ([[mod-102-running-the-program]] chapter 02); trajectory into
  the budget cycle ([[mod-113-budget-vendor-p-and-l-and-investment-case]]).
- **Month +6 (mid-year).** Mid-year trajectory read. Lighter,
  focused on evidence accumulation against the year's planned
  moves rather than full re-scoring.

Every step is on the calendar. Every step has a named owner.
The assessment is not an event the head runs when there is time;
it is an operating discipline with a fixed cadence.

## The self-assessment discipline

The self-assessment is the primary voice, and the head owns its
discipline.

**Evidence-anchored scoring.** Every score at level 3 or above
requires linked evidence — a document under document control, a
system-of-record record, or a KPI/KRI run from
[[01-kri-kpi-portfolio]]. A score claimed without evidence is a
level 2 by default. This is not a stylistic choice; it is what
allows the CAE and the external auditor to test the score. A
score the head cannot point at evidence for is a score the head
cannot defend.

**Level-50 architect scores; head reviews; head does not
override.** The scoring is the architect's craft, not the head's.
The head's job is to review the scoring for consistency across
axes and to challenge the architect on scores that appear to
depart from the model's tier definitions. What the head does
*not* do is override a level-4 score to level-5 because the head
believes the operation is better than the evidence supports. That
override is the failure mode that turns the plot into
decoration.

**Down-moves are as diagnostic as up-moves.** An axis or a cell
that scored level 4 last year and level 3 this year is not a
failure of the assessment; it is a signal that either the model
changed (in which case the change is minuted at the model-version
review), the evidence base thinned (in which case the operating
discipline is failing on that cell and the head owns the
diagnosis), or the operating quality actually regressed (in
which case the head owns the reason). The assessment names which
of the three explains the down-move, and the audit committee
reads the down-move accordingly.

**Draft scores stay editable until second-line challenge
closes.** The self-assessment does not go to the audit committee
as authored. It goes through second-line challenge, third-line
input, and external benchmarking first — each of which may move
scores. The head resists the pull to freeze the draft early
because early-frozen scores create a defensive posture during
challenge.

## The second-line challenge process

The CRO's function — or the enterprise's operational-risk
function, depending on how second line is structured — challenges
the self-assessment before it goes to the audit committee. The
challenge is a specific process, not a courtesy read.

**What the challenge tests.** The second-line reviewer tests
whether the evidence linked from each level-3+ score is *the*
evidence the score requires, and whether the tier definition is
being applied consistently across axes. The reviewer does not
independently score the enterprise; the reviewer challenges
scores the self-assessment produced.

**What the challenge output looks like.** For each axis, a set
of confirmed scores, contested scores (with the reviewer's
alternative and reason), and evidence gaps (scores the reviewer
would confirm if additional evidence were produced). The
contested and gap sets are the head's queue for pre-audit-
committee resolution.

**Who resolves contested scores.** The head resolves, with the
architect and the second-line reviewer in the room. The
resolution either produces the additional evidence to hold the
original score, or the score moves to the reviewer's alternative.
The resolution is minuted. A contested score that goes to the
audit committee unresolved is a discipline break — the audit
committee should not be adjudicating scoring disputes in the
room.

**Second-line does not sign off on the plot.** Second line
challenges; second line does not authorise the plot. The
authoriser is the head, with audit-committee ratification. The
distinction preserves the second-line's independence for the
next year's challenge.

## The third-line audit input

The chief audit executive (CAE) contributes independent
assurance on the assessment. The nature of the assurance is a
deliberate choice, and the head consults the CAE on which nature
is appropriate.

- **Assurance on the evidence base.** The CAE tests, on a
  sample basis, that the evidence linked from level-3+ scores
  is present, appropriately controlled, and consistent with the
  score claim. This is the lightest CAE engagement and is
  appropriate when the assessment is stable and the CAE's audit
  plan has other priorities.
- **Assurance on the scoring methodology.** The CAE tests that
  the scoring methodology — the model, the tier definitions, the
  application of the definitions — is defensible. This is the
  moderate engagement and is appropriate when the model has
  changed materially or when the head is establishing the
  assessment discipline for the first time.
- **Full audit engagement.** The CAE conducts a formal audit of
  the assessment, per the CAE's audit standards (IIA IPPF), and
  the audit output is a formal opinion. This is the heaviest
  engagement and is appropriate when the assessment is under
  external regulatory or examination scrutiny, or when the
  audit-committee has specifically requested it.

CAE independence is load-bearing. The CAE does not set scores;
the CAE assures the evidence and methodology. See
[[mod-102-running-the-program]] chapter 04 for the underlying
third-line boundary discipline.

The CAE's output is a written statement — an assurance
memorandum, an audit report, or an opinion — that becomes part
of the assessment package presented to the audit committee.

## External benchmarking against peer institutions

The self-assessment, second-line challenge, and CAE input are
all *internal* to the enterprise. The external benchmarking
voice is what makes the plot relative rather than absolute. A
level-4 score on GOVERN 2 means something in the abstract; it
means something specific when set against peer scores.

**The peer set the head benchmarks against.** The peer set is
sector-specific and named. Typical composition:

- Three to five peer institutions in the same sector and of
  comparable scale — global systemic banks, tier-one insurers,
  large integrated health systems, comparable public-sector
  bodies, frontier labs, comparable tech platforms.
- Industry-body benchmarking exchanges where they exist and the
  enterprise is a member — sector trade associations (SIFMA
  banking benchmarking, ISDA, AHIP, PhRMA, and sector
  equivalents) sometimes run private AI-governance benchmarks
  for their memberships.
- Certification-body-facilitated benchmarks — some ISO/IEC 42001
  certification bodies publish anonymised aggregate maturity
  data across their certified populations
  ([[mod-102-running-the-program]] chapter 04 for the
  certification-body relationship).
- Public disclosure — peers' voluntary code signatory reports
  (see [[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]]
  chapter 01), frontier-lab public safety frameworks and
  associated disclosure cadences, and sector-body public
  positions.

The head does not select the peer set casually. The peer set is
the enterprise's own strategic framing of who it competes with,
who it is compared to by regulators and investors, and who it
positions against publicly. The audit committee ratifies the
peer set annually so the benchmarking is not the head's own
private definition of "peer".

**What the benchmarking exchange looks like operationally.**
Formal peer-exchange forums are rare and slow to form. In their
absence, the head runs bilateral exchanges with peer heads on a
"my scores in exchange for yours, under NDA" basis. The
exchanges are careful — the substance is score comparison, not
program-detail disclosure. The head does not share the
enterprise's evidence base or its target trajectory in the
exchange; the head shares the current plot and the
methodology-level context.

**How benchmarking output enters the assessment.** The
benchmarking output is a per-axis peer-position note — "the
enterprise is at level 3 on GOVERN 2; the peer set is at level
2–3, with one peer at level 4 driven by a recent regulatory
action". The head does not adjust scores based on the
benchmarking; the head reports the position and lets the audit
committee read the relative signal. The relative signal informs
the trajectory (where the enterprise chooses to lead, match, or
lag the sector) but it does not move the score itself.

**Where benchmarking is unavailable, the head names the gap.**
Some sectors do not have functional peer-exchange forums, and
some peer relationships do not permit the kind of exchange the
head would like. The assessment names the benchmarking gap
explicitly rather than fabricating a peer read. A benchmarking
note that reads "no functional peer benchmarking available for
this axis in the enterprise's sector; the head is engaging with
[named forum] to establish a benchmarking exchange for the
following year" is defensible; a note that fabricates a peer
position is not.

## The output pack — what the audit committee reads

The assessment produces a discrete pack the audit committee
reads at the deep-dive session ([[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]]
chapter 02). The pack has:

- **A per-axis maturity plot** with current-year scores per
  cell, prior-year scores for delta visibility, and the
  ratified two-year target overlaid.
- **A year-over-year delta commentary**, coloured — improvements
  in one colour, regressions in another, holds in a third.
  Regressions are named specifically with the diagnosis (model
  change, evidence thinning, or operating regression).
- **The composite view**, as a summary read *after* the per-axis
  view, with the explicit interpretation note the model requires
  (per [[02-authoring-the-maturity-model]] composition rules).
- **The two-year trajectory** — where the enterprise commits to
  move, per axis, with target levels and target-level
  rationales (including deliberate under-investment).
- **The cost-of-move table** — for each planned move, the
  resource investment (people, technology, external assurance,
  training and certification, external audit) required. Feeds
  the budget cycle in
  [[mod-113-budget-vendor-p-and-l-and-investment-case]] and
  chapter [[05-program-cost-management]] of this module.
- **The peer-benchmarking read** per axis, with the peer set and
  the position note.
- **The independent-challenge and CAE assurance outputs** — the
  second-line-challenge summary, the CAE assurance memorandum
  or audit report, and any external assurance provider output
  as applicable.
- **The prioritisation recommendation** — the two or three moves
  the head recommends the enterprise prioritise in the coming
  year, with the specific rationale. Audit committees read this
  section first.
- **The assumptions and limitations section** — a candid
  statement of what the assessment does not measure, what the
  scores mean and what they do not mean. Audit committees trust
  assessments that acknowledge limits.
- **The specific asks of the audit committee** — endorsement of
  the trajectory, approval of the resource requests, ratification
  of any material model-version changes. The asks are phrased so
  the minute-writer can capture them verbatim.

The pack is a document, not a slide deck. It is packaged as a
pre-read that the committee reads before the session, so the
session is spent on discussion rather than presentation.

## Feeding the trajectory into board reporting and multi-year investment planning

The trajectory the audit committee ratifies is the multi-year
investment plan for the AI-governance program. Two flows carry
the trajectory forward.

**Board reporting flow.** The board sees the maturity plot
annually, via the audit committee's board update
([[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 01),
with the trajectory line overlaid on the current plot. The
board reads the delta from prior year and the position against
the trajectory; the board does not typically ratify at the plot
level (the audit committee does that) but the board reads the
overall shape and holds the trajectory in mind as it authorises
the head's program.

**Multi-year investment planning flow.** The cost-of-move table
feeds directly into the multi-year budget in
[[mod-113-budget-vendor-p-and-l-and-investment-case]]. Each
planned trajectory move is a line item in the budget with an
identified funding source, a named owner, and a delivery date.
The finance business partner ([[05-program-cost-management]])
carries the line items into the budget cycle; the head defends
them at the finance committee and at the CFO's own budget
challenge.

The trajectory is not aspiration. It is a set of specific,
budgeted moves the audit committee has ratified and the CFO has
funded. When the head later reports on the trajectory's
progress, the report reads against a specific commitment set,
not against a vague "we are getting more mature".

## What happens when the assessment reveals regressions or under-performance

The assessment will, at some point, produce a plot the head
would rather not present. An axis regressed year-over-year. A
target for the current year was missed. A peer overtook on a
dimension the enterprise had positioned as its strength. The
discipline for the pack is the same as for a strong assessment:
report the fact, name the owner, describe what has been done
and is being done, state the ask.

The rehearsal for the room is in chapter
[[06-difficult-measurement-conversations]]. What the
assessment pack does specifically:

- Names the regression or under-performance in the delta
  commentary — no burying it in a footnote.
- Provides the diagnosis (why it happened) with candour — the
  audit committee has stronger tolerance for candid diagnosis
  than for evasive explanation.
- Provides the corrective in the trajectory — what the head is
  doing about it, whether the corrective is funded, and what
  the head is asking the committee for.
- Names the specific resource ask if additional investment is
  required to close the gap.

An assessment that reports only improvements is an assessment
the audit committee will not trust. An assessment that reports
the harder facts alongside the improvements is one the
committee reads with confidence.

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**Solo self-assessment.** The head runs the assessment without
second-line challenge, third-line input, or external
benchmarking, and presents the plot as authoritative. The plot
is legible from one angle only. The remedy is the four-voice
discipline.

**Peer-benchmarking fabrication.** The head reports a peer
position without a source for the position — inferred from
public disclosure, guessed from industry-conference talk,
extrapolated from a single conversation. Peer benchmarking with
fabricated peer scores is the credibility failure the head
cannot walk back if a peer later publishes and the enterprise's
reported comparison is wrong. The remedy is naming the
benchmarking gap explicitly where no defensible peer read is
available.

**Trajectory drift.** The audit committee ratifies a
trajectory. The following year, the head reports against a
different trajectory — quietly re-baselined, with the year's
performance measured against the new trajectory rather than
the ratified one. The audit committee will notice. The remedy
is that trajectory changes are minuted and ratified at the
committee before the annual assessment, not surfaced after
the fact.

**Assessment as narrative.** The head reports the assessment as
a set of paragraphs rather than as a plot with scored cells.
Narrative flatters the head (the head has an opinion about
every axis) but does not carry a trajectory. The remedy is the
per-axis plot and the tier-anchored scoring discipline.

## Summary

- The assessment is a discipline, not an event. Four voices
  combine to make the plot defensible: self-assessment (head's
  function), second-line challenge (CRO's function), third-line
  audit input (CAE), and external benchmarking (peer
  institutions in sector).
- The assessment sits on the AIMS operating calendar with a
  fixed placement (typically Q4) so the outputs feed the next
  year's objectives, risk-treatment plan, and budget cycle in
  Q1, and with a mid-year trajectory read at the six-month
  mark.
- Self-assessment is evidence-anchored — every level-3+ score
  requires linked evidence — and the level-50 architect scores
  while the head reviews without overriding.
- Second-line challenge is a structured challenge process with
  confirmed, contested, and gap outputs; contested scores are
  resolved before the audit committee, not in the room.
- Third-line CAE input is one of three depths — evidence
  assurance, methodology assurance, or full audit — chosen
  deliberately with the CAE, and CAE independence is preserved
  (CAE does not set scores).
- External benchmarking is against a named, audit-committee-
  ratified peer set. Where benchmarking is unavailable, the
  head names the gap rather than fabricating a peer read.
- The output pack has per-axis plots, delta commentary, the
  two-year trajectory, the cost-of-move table, peer
  benchmarking, second-line and CAE outputs, prioritisation
  recommendation, assumptions and limitations, and specific
  asks. The pack is a document, not a slide deck.
- The trajectory feeds board reporting and multi-year
  investment planning as a set of specific, budgeted commitments
  — not aspiration.
- Regressions and under-performance are reported with candid
  diagnosis and named correctives; the discipline is the same as
  for strong assessments.
- Four anti-patterns: solo self-assessment, peer-benchmarking
  fabrication, trajectory drift, assessment-as-narrative.
- Chapter [[04-roi-reporting-to-cfo]] turns to the ROI framing
  that the trajectory anchors on; chapter
  [[05-program-cost-management]] carries the cost discipline.
