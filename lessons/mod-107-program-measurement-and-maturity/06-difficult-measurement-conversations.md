# The Difficult Program-Measurement Conversation

## Motivation

Chapter [[01-kri-kpi-portfolio]] built the KRI/KPI portfolio.
Chapter [[02-authoring-the-maturity-model]] built the maturity
model. Chapter [[03-annual-maturity-assessment]] ran the
assessment. Chapter [[04-roi-reporting-to-cfo]] built the ROI
pack. Chapter [[05-program-cost-management]] built the cost
discipline. Each chapter equips the head to *produce* the
numbers. This chapter equips the head to *stand behind* the
numbers when they land badly.

Because they will. A KPI or KRI will go red. An ROI target will
be missed. A peer institution will pass the enterprise on a
maturity axis the enterprise had positioned as its own strength.
The head does not have to like these moments; the head has to be
practised for them. A head who has rehearsed the difficult
program-measurement conversation walks into the room and
delivers the fact, locates ownership, states the corrective, and
holds under challenge. A head who has not rehearsed narrates,
diffuses ownership, and loses the room.

This chapter is the rehearsal. It draws on the general
difficult-conversation pattern in
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 06 and
specialises it for the three program-measurement scenarios: the
red KPI to the board, the missed ROI target to the CFO, and the
maturity-tier peer overtake. It is not a script. Scripts fail on
contact with the committee. The pattern is a shape.

## Core concept — the shape that survives every difficult measurement conversation

The general pattern in
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 06 is
five moves: name the fact in one sentence, locate ownership,
state what has been done and is being done, state the ask, hold
under challenge. The pattern holds for program-measurement
conversations with one specialisation: the fact is *always* a
number the room can inspect, and the head has to be prepared
for the number to be re-derived from source data in front of
them.

Three failure modes specific to program-measurement
conversations that the pattern is designed against.

- **Redefinition-under-fire.** A metric is red; the head arrives
  with a "refined definition" that puts the metric back to green.
  The audit committee will notice on the first occurrence and
  every subsequent metric the head reports will be suspect. The
  pattern refuses redefinition-under-fire; the metric is what it
  is and the definition sheet from
  [[01-kri-kpi-portfolio]] has not moved.
- **Averaging-through-red.** A composite metric masks a red
  component; the head presents the composite and hopes the
  committee reads the composite rather than the components. The
  audit committee reads the components. The pattern refuses
  averaging-through-red; the components are on the page even
  when the composite is green.
- **Explaining-by-attribution.** The head explains a red number
  by pointing to external drivers — a market shift, a peer's
  incident, a regulator's announcement — without locating
  ownership inside the enterprise. The audit committee reads
  this as the head deflecting. The pattern names the enterprise's
  own decisions and non-decisions as the cause where they are
  the cause.

## Scenario A — the red KPI to the board

The situation. A KPI or KRI from
[[01-kri-kpi-portfolio]] has entered the red band. The head is
delivering the number to the board or audit committee at the
next reporting cycle.

The scenario is not "the number moved into red because the
threshold is wrong". If the threshold is wrong, that is the
CRO's re-calibration conversation, not the difficult board
conversation. This scenario is "the number is red because the
program is genuinely behind on the underlying dimension".

### Move 1 — name the fact

The head opens with one declarative sentence stating what the
number is, where it sits against the threshold, and the
timeframe.

Examples:

- *"The Q3 control-testing pass rate on the AIMS Annex A
  controls was 74%, below the amber-to-red threshold of 80%
  this committee ratified in the December risk-appetite update,
  in the third-party evidence-collection control cluster."*
- *"The regulator-response latency KRI for October was 14
  business days median, against the enterprise's internal
  buffer of 10 and the regulator's 15-day floor, driven by the
  three concurrent requests from [regulator] on [scope]."*
- *"The Q4 evidence-completeness rate for tier-1 third-party AI
  vendors was 82% against the 95% target, with the gap
  concentrated in the four vendors named in the appendix."*
- *"The AIA turnaround time at Q3 close was 47 business days
  median, against the 30-day target, with the backlog
  concentrated in the customer-facing generative-AI systems
  onboarded in Q2."*

The head does not preface. The head does not sequence into
context. The head does not soften. The number is the fact and
the fact is the first sentence.

### Move 2 — locate ownership

The second sentence names who owns the number and what the
ownership covers. This is the specialisation of the general
pattern for measurement conversations: the ownership is the
*measured function*, not the head's function generically.

- *"Accountability for the control-testing pass rate is the
  level-50 senior AI governance architect on the control-set
  authorship side, and the second-line risk-oversight team on
  the testing side, both reporting to me; I am the accountable
  executive for the KRI and its threshold breach."*
- *"Accountability for the regulator-response latency is me
  jointly with the General Counsel on the correspondence path;
  the escalation was to me directly on October 12 when the
  three concurrent requests landed."*
- *"Accountability for the tier-1 third-party evidence-
  completeness rate is the third-party risk-oversight lead on
  my team, jointly with the enterprise procurement lead for the
  contract-refresh cadence."*

The head does not diffuse ownership across "the team", "the
function", or "the industry generally". The head does not blame
the vendor, the regulator, or the customer. The head names the
enterprise's own accountable role — often themselves — and
describes the boundary of the accountability.

### Move 3 — state what has been done and what is being done

Concise past and present tense. The head reports the specific
actions the enterprise has taken since detection and the
specific actions in flight now.

- *"Since detection at the September month-end run, we have
  identified the specific evidence gaps in the third-party
  control cluster, contacted each of the four vendors with a
  remediation deadline of November 15, opened a level-50
  architect review of whether the control-set specification
  itself needs adjustment, and put the recovery to green on the
  December risk-committee agenda."*
- *"We have re-sequenced the regulator-response workload, freed
  additional legal time from the General Counsel's office,
  filed the three responses inside the regulator's floor with
  three days to spare, and re-committed to the internal buffer
  for the November cadence."*
- *"We have segmented the AIA backlog by system criticality, put
  the seven customer-facing generative-AI systems ahead of the
  general queue with an accelerated review protocol, engaged an
  external reviewer for capacity augmentation for the balance
  of Q4, and reforecast the median turnaround for January
  reporting."*

The committee is not asking whether the response is perfect.
The committee is asking whether *something is happening* and
whether that something is being run by someone accountable.
Move 3 answers both.

### Move 4 — state the ask

The specific decision the committee is being asked to take, or
the position the committee is being asked to note as taken.
Phrased so the minute-writer can capture it verbatim.

- *"I am asking the committee to note the KRI breach, to
  endorse the remediation approach, and to note that recovery
  to green is on the December risk-committee agenda with the
  underlying evidence set."*
- *"I am asking the committee to note the latency breach for
  October, to note the return to inside the internal buffer for
  November, and to note the request for one additional level-25
  program-manager FTE for the regulator-response function in
  the following year's budget cycle."*
- *"I am asking the committee to note the tier-1 evidence gap,
  to endorse the escalation of the four named vendors to the
  contract-refresh cadence, and to approve the specific request
  for CFO contingency draw of [amount] for the accelerated
  AIA reviewer capacity."*

Silence about the ask is the ambiguity failure mode. If no
committee decision is requested, the head says so explicitly:
*"I am asking the committee to note the position; no committee
decision is requested at this time."*

### Move 5 — hold under challenge

The board or audit committee will press. The specific challenges
that arise in a red-KPI conversation:

- **"Has this happened before?"** The head answers with the
  metric's trend line — how many quarters in the amber band
  before red, whether the red is a first occurrence or a
  recurrence. The trend line was in the pre-read; the head is
  answering with a specific reference to it.
- **"Is this the wrong appetite?"** The head answers with the
  appetite calibration history — who set the threshold, when,
  on what analysis, and whether the current breach signals the
  appetite is mis-calibrated or the operation is genuinely off
  pace. If the appetite is genuinely wrong, the head says so
  and escalates to a CRO-led re-calibration conversation; the
  head does not use the challenge to move the threshold in the
  room.
- **"Are you resourced to recover?"** The head answers with the
  specific resource picture — what headcount is deployed
  against the recovery, what the head is asking for
  incrementally, and what the head is not asking for and why.
- **"What signals should we be watching?"** The head names the
  specific KPIs and KRIs the committee should track between now
  and the next reporting cycle to know whether recovery is on
  pace. The list is short and specific.
- **"Are other KPIs going to go the same way?"** The head
  answers on the composition — whether the underlying driver
  of this breach affects other metrics and what the head is
  doing pre-emptively.

The head does not use the challenge to defend the metric. The
head uses the challenge to demonstrate the head has thought
through the questions the committee is asking. If the head does
not know an answer, the head says so and commits to the
follow-up: *"I do not know the specific driver at that depth; I
will confirm with the level-50 architect and provide the answer
to the secretariat by close of business."*

## Scenario B — the missed ROI target to the CFO

The situation. The head's ROI pack from
[[04-roi-reporting-to-cfo]] projected an avoided-loss figure
that has, on backtest, come in below plan. Typically the incident-
cost-avoidance component: the model predicted a certain expected
avoided loss based on incident-frequency assumptions; the actual
incident-frequency was higher or the incident-cost was higher
or both, and the modelled avoidance did not materialise.

The scenario is not "the ROI model was overclaiming and is being
walked back" (though that is a distinct scenario the head has
to rehearse elsewhere). This scenario is "the model was
defensibly authored, and the outcome was worse than the model
predicted".

### Move 1 — name the fact

The head opens with the specific delta.

- *"The FY24 incident-cost-avoidance component of the ROI
  pack was modelled at [amount]. Actual incident cost for FY24
  is [amount], of which [amount] would have been counted as
  avoided under the model. The delta against the model is
  [amount], approximately [percent] under."*
- *"The FY24 deal-enablement component was modelled at [amount]
  in enabled revenue on the named RFP pipeline. Actual enabled
  revenue is [amount], with [n] of the named deals not
  closing in year. The delta against the model is [amount],
  approximately [percent] under."*

The head does not sequence into an explanation. The fact is the
number and the delta.

### Move 2 — locate ownership

The head names the model owner and the model boundary.

- *"The ROI model was co-authored by me and the finance
  business partner assigned to the AI-governance program. The
  incident-frequency input was drawn from [source]; the cost-
  per-incident input was drawn from [source]; the mitigation-
  efficacy attribution was mine. Accountability for the model
  is joint on the modelling craft and mine on the substantive
  inputs."*
- *"The deal-enablement model was co-authored with the finance
  business partner and the sales operations lead. The named-
  deal input was drawn from the RFP intake register; the
  attribution to AI-governance credentials was jointly
  determined. Accountability for the substantive input is mine
  and the sales operations lead's; the modelling craft is the
  finance business partner's."*

The head does not blame the finance business partner or the
sales operations lead. The head does not blame the model for
"being modelled". The head names the ownership boundary
explicitly.

### Move 3 — state what has been done and what is being done

- *"Since the backtest was run at year-end close, we have
  identified the specific incidents that drove the actual cost
  above the modelled projection — the two Q2 incidents in
  [scope] that were not in the modelled incident-class
  distribution. We have revised the model's incident-class
  taxonomy to include the class, revised the frequency and
  cost assumptions with the finance business partner, and the
  FY25 model reflects the revision transparently. The revised
  FY25 avoided-loss projection is [amount]."*
- *"Since Q3 forecast, we have re-worked the deal-enablement
  attribution model with the sales operations lead — the two
  deals that did not close were in a segment where the AI-
  governance differentiator was not the determinative factor,
  and the attribution logic did not capture that nuance. The
  revised model narrows the attribution to the segments where
  AI-governance credentials are demonstrably determinative,
  and the FY25 projection is [amount]."*

The head reports the revision transparently. The head does not
present the FY25 projection as if the FY24 miss did not happen;
the head reports the miss, the diagnosis, the model revision,
and the revised projection together.

### Move 4 — state the ask

- *"I am asking the CFO to note the FY24 delta, to endorse the
  model revision, and to accept the revised FY25 projection as
  the basis for the FY25 budget discussion. I am not asking
  for a budget adjustment on the basis of this miss; the
  program cost is unchanged and the case for the program is
  unchanged."*
- *"I am asking the CFO to note the deal-enablement delta, to
  endorse the attribution-model narrowing, and to note the
  FY25 sales-pipeline coordination adjustment we are running
  with the sales operations lead to sharpen the model's
  predictive quality."*

The head does not use the missed target as an occasion to
request more budget. That is a discipline break the CFO reads
as opportunism. The ask is that the CFO ratifies the revised
model as the basis for the following cycle; the budget
conversation is separate.

### Move 5 — hold under challenge

CFO challenges typically:

- **"Was the model always overclaiming?"** The head answers
  with the model's authorship history and the backtest history.
  If prior-year backtests were clean and this year's miss is
  the first, the head says so. If this is the second consecutive
  miss on the same component, the head acknowledges the
  pattern and describes the correction.
- **"Should we be reducing the AI-governance budget by the ROI
  delta?"** The head answers on the program's cost-benefit —
  the program's total ROI (across the three components) against
  its total cost, and the risk-adjusted view. A missed
  incident-cost-avoidance figure does not reduce the case for
  the program; it reduces the modelled ROI by the specific
  delta.
- **"Do we trust the deal-enablement model at all now?"** The
  head answers on the anchored component — the specific deals
  that did close subject to specific AI-governance conditions,
  independent of the modelled attribution to broader trust-
  margin uplift. Anchored evidence survives the model
  challenge; illustrative evidence does not.
- **"What is your confidence in the revised FY25 projection?"**
  The head answers honestly. A revised model with backtest-
  informed assumptions is more defensible than the prior
  model, but it is not certain. The head states the confidence
  caveat as it is on the model, not as the CFO would prefer to
  hear it.

The head does not defend the model against the finding. The
head owns the miss, presents the correction, and moves the
conversation to the following cycle.

## Scenario C — the peer overtake on a maturity tier

The situation. The annual maturity assessment from
[[03-annual-maturity-assessment]] shows a peer institution has
moved ahead of the enterprise on a maturity axis or subcategory
where the enterprise had positioned as strong. The head is
presenting the assessment to the audit committee and expects the
peer position to come up.

The scenario is not "we are behind peers generally". If the
enterprise is behind on multiple axes and the trajectory is off
pace across the board, that is a broader conversation about the
program's overall investment. This scenario is the specific
case: on one dimension the enterprise had claimed leadership,
a peer has passed the enterprise, and the audit committee will
notice.

### Move 1 — name the fact

The head names the specific dimension, the peer position, and
the enterprise's position, all explicitly.

- *"On the MEASURE axis of the NIST AI RMF Playbook, the
  enterprise scored level 4 at the FY24 assessment; the peer-
  benchmarking exchange shows two of the five sector peers now
  scoring level 4.5–5 on the same axis, up from level 3.5–4 at
  the FY23 exchange. The enterprise has not moved on this axis
  in the interval."*
- *"On the ISO/IEC 42001 clause 9 maturity, the enterprise
  scored level 4 at the FY24 assessment; three of the five
  sector peers are now scoring level 4.5+ on their clause 9,
  driven primarily by the automation-of-monitoring investment
  peers have made and the enterprise has not."*

The head does not soften. The head does not sequence into
"it's not really an overtake because..." The number is the
number and the peer's position is the peer's position.

### Move 2 — locate ownership

- *"Accountability for the enterprise's MEASURE-axis position
  is the level-50 senior AI governance architect who authored
  the enterprise's evaluation-suite maturity trajectory, and
  me as the accountable executive for the trajectory the audit
  committee ratified in FY23. The trajectory did not
  contemplate a peer move at the pace peers have moved; that
  reading is mine to own."*
- *"Accountability for the clause 9 position is the level-50
  architect on the monitoring-automation investment case and
  the FY23 audit-committee decision to prioritise the
  MEASURE-axis moves over the monitoring-automation
  investment; both were my recommendations, ratified in the
  FY23 trajectory."*

The head does not name the peer as having overinvested, or
having overclaimed, or having done something the enterprise
would not do. The head owns the enterprise's own trajectory
decision.

### Move 3 — state what has been done and what is being done

- *"Since the peer position was surfaced in the September
  benchmarking exchange, we have re-opened the MEASURE-axis
  trajectory decision with the level-50 architect; we have
  scoped the specific evaluation-suite investment that would
  move the enterprise from level 4 to level 4.5 in FY25 and
  to level 5 by FY26; and we have the cost-of-move figure at
  [amount] year one and [amount] year two on the FY25 budget
  request."*
- *"Since the peer clause 9 position was surfaced, we have
  scoped the monitoring-automation investment specifically —
  which controls would be automated, at what tooling cost,
  with what staff redeployment — and we have the trajectory
  moves on the FY25 assessment prep for audit-committee
  ratification."*

The head reports specific corrective actions. Not "we will
consider" or "we will study"; specific moves with cost
attached.

### Move 4 — state the ask

- *"I am asking the committee to note the peer position, to
  endorse the revised trajectory that moves the enterprise
  from level 4 to level 5 on MEASURE across the FY25–FY26
  horizon, and to authorise the FY25 incremental investment of
  [amount] for the evaluation-suite work. The alternative — a
  deliberate under-investment posture with the trajectory
  holding at level 4 — is a defensible posture the committee
  can also ratify; I am recommending the move, not the hold."*

The head presents both options — the move and the deliberate
under-investment — and recommends one. The head does not
present the move as if under-investment is not a defensible
alternative; the discipline in
[[02-authoring-the-maturity-model]] permits both, and the head
respects that discipline in the room.

### Move 5 — hold under challenge

Audit-committee challenges typically:

- **"Why did you not anticipate this move?"** The head
  answers honestly. Peer trajectories can shift; the previous
  year's benchmarking exchange gave one signal, and the peers
  moved faster than the trajectory contemplated. The head does
  not pretend to have anticipated a move that was not
  anticipated.
- **"Have the peers been more thoughtful than we have?"** A
  legitimate challenge, and the head answers with the specific
  investment path — the peers made a specific investment (the
  evaluation-suite tooling, the monitoring automation) and the
  enterprise made a different specific investment (whatever
  the enterprise prioritised instead). The comparison is
  between investment choices, not between capabilities.
- **"Are we going to be behind on other axes too?"** The head
  answers with the peer-benchmarking read across axes — where
  the enterprise leads, where it matches, where it lags. The
  full picture is more informative than the single-axis
  finding.
- **"What is the reputational implication?"** The head answers
  on the disclosure surface — whether the peer's move is
  publicly disclosed, whether the enterprise's position is
  publicly disclosed, and whether the peer-benchmarking
  exchange itself is discreet. The reputational implication is
  bounded by disclosure surface.

The head does not defend the enterprise's position against the
peer's position. The head owns the peer overtake, presents the
corrective, and moves the audit committee to the trajectory
decision.

## Language discipline — what the head does and does not say

Four language habits the head holds across all three scenarios.

**Present the fact in a way that survives quotation.** Every
sentence the head speaks in the room could end up in a
transcript, a minute, a leaked email, or a subsequent
regulator engagement. If the head would not stand by the
sentence when read back three years later, the head does not
say it. Specifically for measurement conversations: the head
does not diminish a metric that has been red for two quarters
by calling it "the kind of thing that fluctuates"; the head
does not describe a peer overtake as "the peer benchmarking
methodology being what it is". Both diminishments will surface
as evasive later.

**Do not diminish; do not aggrandise.** The metric is the
metric. Do not soften a red KRI with "though this is well
within the range of typical AI-governance program variance";
that is either factual and boring or hopeful and misleading,
and either way it does not belong in the room. Equally, do not
call an ROI miss "catastrophic" if it is a modelled component
undershooting; the language is disproportionate and the
committee will read the disproportion as loss of composure.

**Attribute where attribution is fair.** If the incident-cost
overshoot was driven by two specific incidents in a specific
scope, name the specific incidents (with appropriate
confidentiality controls). If the peer overtake was driven by
a specific peer investment the enterprise did not make, name
the specific investment. Selective attribution reads as spin;
comprehensive attribution reads as ownership.

**Cite the definition, not the interpretation.** When a
committee member asks "what is this metric measuring", the
head reads from the definition sheet in
[[01-kri-kpi-portfolio]]. Not from memory. Not with editorial
softening. The definition sheet is the head's contract with
the committee about what the metric means, and citing it in the
room reinforces the discipline.

## After the meeting

The difficult program-measurement conversation continues after
the meeting closes. The disciplines from
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 06
apply, with three measurement-specific additions.

- **Update the metric-definition sheet if the meeting revealed
  a gap.** If a committee member asked a question the
  definition sheet did not answer, the sheet is updated ahead
  of the next reporting cycle. Not to change what the metric
  measures; to clarify what the sheet says about it.
- **Update the model documentation if the meeting revealed a
  gap.** If an ROI-model challenge revealed an assumption not
  captured in the model documentation, the documentation is
  updated. The next quarter's finance-business-partner review
  reads against the updated documentation.
- **Debrief the level-50 architect and the finance business
  partner on how the discussion went.** The architect and the
  finance business partner authored the numbers with the head;
  they hear how the numbers landed, so the next cycle's
  authoring benefits from the committee's read.

## Rehearsal — how the head gets ready

The head does not walk into a difficult measurement
conversation cold. Two rehearsal practices before the meeting.

**The murder-board.** The head convenes a small group — the
level-50 architect, the finance business partner, ideally the
CRO or a trusted peer — for a rehearsal of the presentation.
The group's job is to press the head on every question the
committee might ask, in the language the committee would use.
The head practises the moves. The group flags any move where
the head deflected, diminished, or overreached.

**The pre-brief with the committee chair.** For a material
red-KPI or missed-ROI conversation, the head briefs the audit
committee chair or the risk committee chair (whichever is the
natural adjacency) before the committee meeting. The brief is
not to campaign for a specific outcome; the brief is to give
the chair the substance in private so the chair can shape the
committee conversation and so the head does not surprise the
chair in the room. Chairs who are pre-briefed on difficult
conversations manage the room; chairs who are surprised do
not.

Neither rehearsal is optional for a material scenario. The
head who arrives at a difficult program-measurement
conversation without rehearsal is not being courageous; they
are being under-prepared, and the committee reads the under-
preparation.

## Anti-patterns the head does not permit

**The pre-emptive re-baselining.** The head anticipates a red
number and quietly re-baselines the KPI definition so the
number reports as green. When the committee later notices the
definition change (they will), the head loses the credibility
that would have carried the difficult conversation. The
remedy is the version-controlled definition sheet and the
principle that definition changes are minuted and ratified,
not made in advance of a report.

**The buried-red pattern.** The head reports the composite as
green and buries the red component in an appendix. The audit
committee reads appendices; more importantly, the audit
committee reads the delta between the composite and the
components, and the buried-red pattern reads as opacity. The
remedy is per-component reporting on the page, not in the
appendix.

**The apology-in-place-of-substance.** The head opens with an
apology or an extended acknowledgment of "regret" without
delivering the substance the committee is there for. Apologies
consume airtime that should be spent on facts, ownership, and
correctives. The remedy is: the head can apologise briefly in
the meeting's opening remarks if the situation warrants; the
head does not confuse the apology for the substance.

**The over-reliance on external explanation.** The head
explains every miss by naming external drivers — regulator
behaviour, peer moves, market shifts, incident patterns "the
whole industry is seeing". Selective external attribution is
reasonable; consistent external attribution reads as the head
being passive. The remedy is naming the enterprise's own
decisions as a first-class explanation whenever they are a
first-class explanation.

**The reactive-only posture.** The head appears in front of
the audit committee only when there is a red number to
explain. The committee reads the head as reactive rather than
strategic. The remedy is the standing programme-effectiveness
cadence in [[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] —
the head appears in the routine cadence as well as the
difficult cadence, so the difficult cadence is not the only
lens the committee has on the head.

## Summary

- Difficult program-measurement conversations follow the
  general five-move pattern from
  [[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 06:
  name the fact, locate ownership, state what has been and is
  being done, state the ask, hold under challenge.
- Three measurement-specific failure modes to fail-safe
  against: redefinition-under-fire, averaging-through-red,
  explaining-by-attribution.
- Three rehearsed scenarios: the red KPI to the board, the
  missed ROI target to the CFO, the peer overtake on a
  maturity tier the enterprise had positioned as its
  strength. Each scenario has its own five-move rehearsal.
- Language discipline: survive quotation, do not diminish, do
  not aggrandise, attribute fairly, cite the definition rather
  than the interpretation.
- After the meeting: update the metric-definition sheet or the
  model documentation if the meeting revealed a gap, and
  debrief the level-50 architect and finance business partner
  on how the discussion went.
- Rehearsal: the murder-board with the architect, the finance
  business partner, and a trusted peer; the pre-brief with the
  natural committee chair. Neither is optional for a material
  scenario.
- Five anti-patterns: pre-emptive re-baselining, buried-red,
  apology-in-place-of-substance, over-reliance on external
  explanation, and the reactive-only posture.
- The chapter closes the module. The head who has authored the
  portfolio, the model, the assessment, the ROI story, and the
  cost discipline — and who has rehearsed the difficult
  conversations against each — has built the program-
  measurement backbone the audit committee, the CFO, and the
  board rely on.
