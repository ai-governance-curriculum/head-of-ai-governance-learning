# Difficult Incident Conversations

## Motivation

The head of AI governance who has run the incident bridge
well — held the substantive seat, closed the classification
call inside the first ninety minutes, commissioned the
containment posture, signed the Article 73 filing on the
running clock — will still, over the arc of a serious
incident, have to run a small number of conversations that
the bridge cannot help with. The CEO wants a five-minute
readout before deciding whether to phone the audit-committee
chair tonight. The full board convenes out-of-cycle two days
in because the incident is the second occurrence of a class
of risk the enterprise did not carry on its register, and
the board needs to hear the honest account from the head
personally. The national competent authority reads the
Article 73 filing and notes that awareness was established
on the Monday but the filing arrived on the Friday; the
follow-up call is scheduled for Tuesday morning and it will
be the head who takes it. A specialist trade journalist has
been briefed on the incident by an anonymous source and has
asked for a technical interview; Communications has decided
the enterprise will participate and the head is the named
interviewee.

None of these are bridge conversations. Each is one-to-one
or small-group. Each is under time pressure, often under
public or regulator scrutiny, and each shapes the
enterprise's downstream exposure more than any single
decision the bridge itself made. The failure mode this
chapter defends against is the head who is technically
competent inside the bridge and who fumbles the conversation
outside it. Three shapes of that failure recur. The **CEO
briefing that opens open technical questions** — the head
walks into the CEO's office with a running commentary on
what the model might have done and no recommendation on
whether to convene the audit committee, and the CEO makes
the enterprise-level call under weaker AI-substantive
framing than the head could have delivered. The **regulator
follow-up that over-shares** — the head, wanting to
demonstrate transparency, volunteers detail beyond what the
filing carried and beyond what GC advised, and hands the
national competent authority material for a broader inquiry
than the filing alone would have opened. The **media
appearance the head is unprepared for** — Communications
puts the head in front of a specialist journalist because
the CCO is unavailable, the head has not rehearsed the
coordinated position with Legal, and the head speculates on
a technical detail that contradicts the enterprise's public
statement.

Each failure is a positioning error, not a knowledge one.
The head has the substance. What the head does not always
have is the disciplined shape for delivering it under
pressure to a counterpart whose response the head cannot
control. This chapter is the shape.

## Core concept — rehearsed shape, cold-run under pressure

The four conversations this chapter walks through are not
inventions. They follow the disciplined shape of the board
decision meetings authored in
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] chapter 04
and the regulator engagement discipline authored across
[[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapters 06 and 07. The
briefing shape is the same across peace-time and incident-
time: named participants, named purpose, an opening frame
the head owns, a bounded set of substantive questions the
counterpart is likely to ask, a discipline for answering
each, a set of moves the head does not make, and a closing
frame that lands the hand-off cleanly. What is different
under incident conditions is that the shape has to be
rehearsed cold. Peace-time briefings run on preparation
time — the head reads the register, drafts the note, walks
the flow with the delegate, and enters the room with the
shape fresh. Incident-time briefings run on fatigue,
distraction, competing calls on the head's attention,
political weather the head cannot forecast, and information
the head learns thirty seconds before the door opens. The
shape has to be *muscle memory* rather than *fresh
preparation* if it is to hold.

Rehearsal is where the shape becomes muscle memory. The
rehearsal discipline is treated at the end of this chapter
and composes with the drill programme covered in
[[01-incident-command-shape-at-leadership-scope]] and with
the calibration-from-public-incident-data discipline in
[[07-calibration-from-public-incident-data]]. Every one of
the four conversations below is a conversation the head has
run in a rehearsed setting, with a rehearsed counterpart,
before running it live. The head who runs the CEO briefing
for the first time under an actual serious-incident clock
is the head who will discover — under maximum cost — that
the shape does not survive first contact with real
pressure.

The four conversations are not exhaustive. The head may
also brief the enterprise all-hands with the CEO, brief a
customer-council chair, brief a strategic-investor
representative, brief a sector-body board on which the head
sits in a personal capacity. What the four cover is the
substantive surface where positional accountability is
highest and where the head's presence, framing, and language
have the largest downstream effect on the enterprise's
exposure. Once the shape of the four is muscle memory, the
head can extend it to the adjacent variants without
authoring a new shape from scratch.

## Conversation 1 — Briefing the CEO on a serious incident

### Participants and purpose

Two people in the room, most of the time. The CEO and the
head. Occasionally the General Counsel is on the line where
a legal-characterisation question is unresolved; occasionally
the CISO is on the line where the operational-severity call
is contested with the AI-substantive severity call. The
Chief of Staff to the CEO may be in the room to take the
record. The audit-committee chair is not in the room; the
audit-committee chair is one of the outputs of the meeting.

The purpose of the meeting is not to update the CEO. The
purpose is for the CEO to make the enterprise-level
decisions the incident requires: whether to phone the
audit-committee chair tonight, whether to convene an out-of-
cycle full-board briefing inside one business day, whether
to pre-notify the national competent authority before the
Article 73 filing lands, whether the CCO or the CEO or an
external-counsel-fronted statement is the right public
posture, whether the enterprise activates its crisis-
communications retainer, whether legal privilege is asserted
around the incident-response workstream in a specific way.
Every one of these is a CEO-level call the head does not
make. The head's job is to arrive with the substance the CEO
needs to make them.

### The opening frame

The head opens with four sentences. The first names the
incident class in one line — "we have a jailbreak-driven
downstream-harm event in the customer-support assistant,
class 5 on the internal taxonomy, currently at Sev-2
operational severity and Sev-1 on regulatory exposure". The
second names the containment status — "containment is held;
the endpoint has been throttled, the affected capability is
disabled, incident-time evaluation is running to bound the
affected population". The third names the disclosure-
trigger assessment — "Article 73 is triggered; the two-day
clock started this morning at 09:14; the filing team is
composing against the pre-built template". The fourth names
the specific decision the head is bringing to the CEO —
"the decision I need from you in this meeting is whether
the audit-committee chair is notified out-of-cycle
tonight". The fourth sentence is the load-bearing move; it
tells the CEO what the meeting is *for* before the CEO has
to infer it from context.

The head does not open with a narrative reconstruction of
the incident. The narrative is available in a one-page
briefing note that the head hands to the CEO or has already
sent; the note carries the DFIR timeline extract, the
AI-substantive annotation, the containment log, and the
draft communications posture. The CEO reads the note if the
CEO chooses; the head does not read it aloud.

### The three-to-five substantive questions the CEO is likely to ask

The CEO will ask a small number of questions. The head has
rehearsed the answer to each, with the head's confidence
level attached to each answer.

**"How bad is this."** The head answers in enterprise-level
terms, not technical ones. Bad means: how many customers or
affected individuals; how much operational disruption; how
much regulatory exposure; how much reputational exposure;
how much financial exposure the head can bound at this
point in the incident. The head answers each dimension with
the head's current best estimate, the head's confidence
level, and the time by which the estimate will firm up. The
head does not say "we do not know yet" without immediately
naming when the enterprise will know.

**"Do we tell the board tonight."** The head brings a
recommendation. Not a menu. The audit-committee-chair
notification threshold from
[[mod-103-board-and-audit-committee-reporting]] cluster 3
is the reference; the head has already applied it and
arrives with a yes or no. The head names the reasoning in
one sentence — "yes, because this event materialises a
class of risk the audit committee last discussed six months
ago, and the pattern is that the chair prefers to hear
material events from us on the day rather than in the next
pack". The CEO overrides the head's recommendation or
endorses it; either way the CEO decides in the meeting.

**"Do we say anything publicly."** The head brings a
recommendation composed with the CCO's holding-statement
posture, per the coordination interface with Communications
from [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] chapter 05
<!-- needs-research: verify mod-109 chapter number for CCO
coordination interface --> and per the public-trust posture
from [[mod-106-external-positioning-and-voluntary-codes]]
chapter 03 <!-- needs-research: verify mod-106 chapter
number for public-trust posture -->. The head does not
draft the public statement in the meeting; the head names
whether the enterprise's pre-agreed posture applies and
whether the CCO should be activating the holding-statement
library.

**"When do we file with the regulator."** The head names
the running clock, names the filing team's current progress
against the pre-built template, and names the expected sign-
off window. Where the filing is on a two-day clock, the
head names the specific hour by which the filing will be
transmitted. The CEO does not need to approve the filing —
the head signs — but the CEO needs to know when the
enterprise's exposure moves from private to regulator-
visible.

**"What could still go wrong."** The head names the
downside surface honestly. Containment could fail; the
incident-time evaluation could reveal a larger affected
population than the current estimate; the filing could
trigger a broader regulator inquiry than the filing itself
addresses; the customer-facing statement could be picked
up by a specialist journalist and turned into a wider
story. The head names each with an assessed likelihood and
the mitigation the head is running against it. Naming the
downside surface here — before the CEO discovers it later —
is the discipline that preserves the CEO's trust in the
head across the incident's arc.

### What the head does not do

The head does not bring open technical questions to the
CEO. The head does not walk into the office and ask "is
this a jailbreak or a training-data leakage event, we are
not sure". Open technical questions belong on the bridge,
with the AI-substantive team and the CISO's DFIR lead. The
head arrives at the CEO's office with the head's current
best classification, the head's confidence level in it, and
a plan for how the classification will firm up.

The head does not narrate the technical detail beyond what
the CEO asks for. The CEO does not need to know what a
retrieval-augmented-generation pipeline is; the CEO needs
to know that a customer-support assistant emitted outputs
outside sanctioned behaviour, that the enterprise contained
the emission within thirty-eight minutes of detection, and
that the affected-customer population is bounded at
approximately X. Technical narration is the head's ego
speaking; the CEO reads it as noise.

The head does not perform certainty the head does not have.
Where the head does not know, the head says so, names when
the head will know, and names the follow-up cadence. The
head who performs certainty at the CEO briefing and
retracts it two hours later is the head whose next CEO
briefing on the next incident starts from a weaker
position.

The head does not lobby the CEO on decisions the CEO owns.
If the CEO overrides the head's recommendation — decides
not to notify the audit-committee chair tonight, decides
not to activate the holding-statement, decides to have
external counsel front the public-facing statement rather
than the CCO — the head registers the position and
executes. Lobbying against a CEO decision inside the
briefing wastes the CEO's time and models bad hierarchy
discipline for the rest of the incident response. If the
head believes the CEO's decision is materially wrong, the
head raises it in a subsequent one-to-one, not in the room
where the decision was made.

### The closing frame and hand-off

The head closes the meeting with three lines. First, the
head restates what the CEO decided in the meeting — "you
have decided to phone the audit-committee chair at 20:00
tonight, to hold public disclosure to the pre-agreed
holding statement until the filing lands, and to have
Communications and Legal on standby for the two-hour
window after the filing". The restatement is the head's
protection against reconstructed memory later; if the CEO
recalls the decisions differently in a subsequent meeting,
the restatement is the head's record. Second, the head
names the next briefing time — "I will be back in this
office at 14:00 tomorrow with the filing-decision meeting
outcome and the updated affected-population estimate".
Third, the head hands off — "in the meantime the CISO's
bridge holds; my delegate is seated; you can reach me on
the incident-priority channel". The head leaves.

## Conversation 2 — Briefing the full board on a systemic failure

### Participants and purpose

The full board of directors, in a scheduled or out-of-cycle
sitting. The CEO chairs (or the board chair, per the
enterprise's governance shape). The head presents on the
AI substance; the CEO presents the enterprise-wide posture.
The General Counsel is present for interpretive questions.
The Chief Financial Officer is often present because
financial exposure is one of the board's fiduciary lenses.
The audit-committee chair and the risk-committee chair
carry particular interest but do not chair the sitting.

The purpose of the briefing is not the same as the
audit-committee-chair briefing at incident-open. That
briefing was operational — the chair needs to know the
event has happened, the containment status, and the
disclosure trajectory. The full-board briefing at this
stage of the incident is a *fiduciary* briefing on a
systemic failure. The specific case this chapter treats is
the one where the incident is the second (or third)
occurrence of a class of risk the enterprise did not carry
on its register — where the honest reading is that the
enterprise had a class-of-risk blind spot and the board's
oversight has to see it directly. The board needs the
account of what the class of failure is, why the
enterprise did not carry it, and what changes the head is
commissioning through the learning cycle from
[[05-post-incident-learning-cycle-sponsorship]].

### The opening frame

The head opens with a directly framed sentence: "the
incident this board is being briefed on is the second
occurrence in fourteen months of a class of AI-specific
failure — evaluator drift feeding through into silent
production quality collapse — that the enterprise's AI
risk register did not carry as a distinct class until the
first occurrence and did not carry with adequate control
coverage until this second occurrence." The sentence is
long and it is deliberate. It names the class of failure,
it names the recurrence, it names the register gap, and it
names the control-coverage gap. The board hears the
enterprise-level shape of the failure in one sentence
before any narrative reconstruction.

The head then names what the briefing will cover — the
class of failure, the enterprise's history with it, the
control changes commissioned through the learning cycle,
the timeline for the changes, and the residual risk the
board is asked to note. The head names what the briefing
will not cover — the operational containment (already
briefed to the audit-committee chair and the CEO), the
regulator filing (in flight, on the pre-agreed timeline),
the public-communications posture (owned by the CCO with
CEO sign-off). Naming what the briefing does not cover
prevents the board's questions from drifting into
operational detail the head has already resolved
elsewhere.

### The three-to-five substantive questions the board is likely to ask

**"How did the class of risk not get on the register."** The
head answers honestly. Where the register was authored
before the class was well-understood in the sector, the
head says so and cites the timeline. Where the class was
understood but the head's predecessor (or the head
personally) made a judgement call not to carry it as a
distinct row because it was folded into a parent risk, the
head says so and does not defend the judgement call as
correct in retrospect. Where the class was carried but at
a residual score that did not warrant the control
coverage it now clearly warranted, the head says so and
names the specific reason the score was set where it was.
The board can absorb a truthful account of past judgement
that turned out wrong; the board cannot absorb a defence
that reads as face-saving.

**"What is different about this incident from the first
occurrence."** The head names the delta with precision.
Same class of failure, materialised in a different system
under different conditions, with a different affected
population and a different regulatory exposure surface.
The head names what the enterprise did after the first
occurrence — the RCA that ran, the control changes that
were commissioned, the register update that was made —
and names honestly why those changes did not prevent the
second occurrence. If the answer is that the first-
occurrence RCA stopped at proximate cause and the systemic
finding was not surfaced, the head names it. If the
answer is that the control was commissioned but the
implementation across all in-scope systems was incomplete,
the head names it. The board reads the head's naming as
evidence of the learning-cycle discipline the head is now
committing to run at greater depth.

**"What are you commissioning now to prevent the third
occurrence."** The head names the specific changes with
named dates. The learning cycle from
[[05-post-incident-learning-cycle-sponsorship]] is the
framework; the specific commissioning is the head's
personal commitment. The head names the RCA charter (with
the specific question the RCA will answer), the target
completion date, the risk-register revision the head
expects the RCA to produce, the control-library update the
head is commissioning from the senior AI governance
architect, the policy revision the head is commissioning,
and the audit-committee-visible closure date. The head
does not name dates the head cannot defend; missing a
named date to the board is more damaging than committing
to a later date at the outset.

**"What resource do you need from us."** The board asks
this question when the board judges the incident as a
signal that the AI governance function is under-resourced
for the class of risk the enterprise now knows it carries.
The head has a rehearsed answer, composed with the CFO in
peace time per the CFO coordination interface from
[[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] chapter 03
<!-- needs-research: verify mod-109 chapter number for CFO
coordination interface -->. The answer is specific:
additional headcount at a named level with a named target
start, additional external evaluator capacity through a
named vendor, additional third-party assessment cadence on
a named surface, additional audit-committee-visible
reporting cadence. The head does not invent the ask in the
meeting; the ask is drawn from the multi-year hiring plan
authored in [[mod-108-leading-the-governance-function]]
chapter 02 <!-- needs-research: verify mod-108 chapter
number for multi-year hiring plan --> and from the ROI
modelling and cost-management discipline in
[[mod-107-program-measurement-and-maturity]].

**"Is anyone accountable."** The board asks this question,
in some form, on any incident with a systemic-failure
framing. The head's answer is disciplined. Accountability
for the enterprise-level failure sits with the head — the
head is the accountable executive for the AI governance
function; the head does not deflect. Accountability for
specific operational failures upstream of the AI
governance function's oversight (a product team that
declined to adopt a control on cost grounds; a data
science team that did not surface an evaluator regression;
a vendor that did not disclose a material model change)
is named honestly. The head does not scapegoat. The head
also does not use "accountability" language as a
performative admission; the head names what will change
in the head's own posture (a review cadence the head will
run personally rather than delegate; a control the head
will sign off on personally at a specified threshold) as
evidence that the accountability is operational, not
rhetorical.

### What the head does not do

The head does not defend past decisions the head did not
own. Where the register gap or the control gap dates from
before the head's tenure, the head names the timeline
factually and does not construct a defence of prior
judgement the head has no basis to defend. The board reads
constructed defences as evidence of positional insecurity
and it damages the head's standing for subsequent
briefings on the same class of risk.

The head does not defend past decisions the head did own,
where the incident is evidence they were wrong. The head
names the decision, names the reasoning at the time,
names what the enterprise now knows that revises the
reasoning, and names the change. Owning a wrong judgement
in the head's own tenure is the discipline that preserves
the head's credibility for the next judgement.

The head does not perform contrition beyond what the
substance warrants. Performative contrition — "I am
deeply sorry, this should not have happened, we let you
down" — is a communications register the board reads as
theatre. What the board wants is substantive account and
specific commitment. The head runs a substantive register
throughout: this is what happened, this is why, this is
what changes.

The head does not narrow the account to the specific
incident. The board's fiduciary lens is systemic; the
board wants to know what the incident says about the
class of risk, about the AI governance function's
maturity, about the enterprise's residual exposure to
isomorphic incidents. The head lifts the account to the
class-of-risk level early and holds it there. Where the
board asks operational detail, the head answers briefly
and returns to the systemic frame.

The head does not commit on behalf of other functions
without their prior alignment. If the changes the head
commissions require CISO co-operation, product-team
adoption, or CFO budget allocation, the head names that
the commitment is conditional on the coordination in
question — coordination the head has already opened with
each function's head per
[[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]. The head does not
promise a change that the head's peers have not agreed to
deliver.

### The closing frame and hand-off

The head closes the briefing with three lines. First, the
head restates the specific commitments the head has made
in the meeting — the RCA charter, the target completion
dates, the resource ask, the audit-committee-visible
closure date. Second, the head names the next board-
visible touchpoint — the audit committee's next scheduled
sitting, where the head will present the RCA finding and
the artefact updates per the learning-cycle discipline.
Third, the head hands the sitting back to the CEO or the
board chair, who closes the item and moves the board to
its next agenda. The head does not linger.

## Conversation 3 — Briefing a regulator on a late or incomplete filing

### Participants and purpose

The head and the named counterpart at the national
competent authority (or the sector supervisor, or the AI
Office where a GPAI dimension is in play, per
[[03-article-73-filing-at-incident-time]] and
[[04-us-serious-incident-disclosure-map]]). The head's
Regulator Relations lead is on the call as coordinator
and record-keeper. The General Counsel is on the call
where the legal-characterisation dimension is unresolved
or where the conversation touches privilege. The head's
senior AI governance architect is on standby, off the
call, to provide technical detail the head requires
mid-conversation.

The purpose of the call is not the filing itself. The
filing was submitted; the regulator has read it; the
regulator has scheduled the follow-up because the filing
carried a gap the regulator's letter names. The specific
case this chapter treats is the one where the filing was
late — awareness was established on Monday, the two-day
clock ran to Wednesday, and the filing arrived Friday —
or where the filing was substantively incomplete against
the seven-question filing-defence discipline from
[[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] chapter 02
<!-- needs-research: verify mod-104 chapter number for the
seven-question filing-defence discipline -->. The
regulator will not open with the gap; the regulator will
work through the filing content first. The head has to be
prepared for the gap question when it arrives, usually
mid-call.

### The opening frame

The head opens by acknowledging the meeting and the
context. Two sentences. "Thank you for scheduling this
follow-up on our Article 73 filing dated [date]. I
understand the purpose is to walk through the filing
content, the questions the filing raised for your team,
and any additional context I can provide." The head does
not lead with the gap. The head does not lead with an
apology. The regulator sets the agenda for the call; the
head follows the regulator's structure.

Where the regulator opens with the gap — "before we begin,
we noted the filing arrived two days past the Article
73(3) clock, and we would like to understand the
circumstances" — the head responds directly. Not
defensively. The head names the gap in factual terms:
"awareness was established at 09:14 on Monday when the
incident bridge classified the event; the Article 73(3)
clock therefore ran to 09:14 Wednesday; the filing was
transmitted at 16:22 Friday. The gap is two days and
seven hours." The head does not round, does not soften,
does not qualify the numeric account. Then the head names
the cause, the immediate corrective action, and the
systemic corrective action, in that order. The regulator
reads factual precision as evidence of institutional
seriousness.

### The three-to-five substantive questions the regulator is likely to ask

**"Why was the filing late."** The head names the cause
directly. The specific cause matters, and the head does
not disguise it. Where the cause was that the
classification meeting did not converge inside the
timeline pressure — because the head or the GC needed
additional time to resolve the Article 3(49) sub-
categorisation, or because the awareness-date evidence
stack was contested internally — the head says so. Where
the cause was operational — the pre-built filing template
was not up to date with the current version of the
regulation, the sign-off routing broke down because a
named signatory was on leave and the pre-authorised
delegate had not been briefed on the specific incident —
the head says so. Where the cause was that the enterprise
made a judgement call to file under a more-onerous
interpretation and needed the additional hours to compose
the more-onerous filing, the head says so and names the
alternative interpretation the enterprise ruled out.

**"What immediate corrective action have you taken."** The
head names the specific operational change. Not "we will
review our processes". A specific change: the pre-built
template has been updated to the current regulation
version and the update has been ratified through the head's
signature; the pre-authorised delegate list has been
refreshed and the delegates have been briefed on the
current template; the classification-meeting convention has
been amended to include a mandatory escalation-to-CEO
trigger at the twelve-hour mark of the Article 73(3)
clock. The corrective actions are the ones the head has
already commissioned; they are not aspirations.

**"What systemic corrective action have you taken."** The
head names the learning-cycle output. The RCA on the
filing-lateness event has been chartered; the target
completion is [date]; the RCA will produce a control-
library update on the incident-time filing discipline and
a policy revision on the classification-meeting cadence.
The head names the specific artefact updates the head
expects the RCA to produce and the audit-committee-visible
closure date. The learning-cycle discipline from
[[05-post-incident-learning-cycle-sponsorship]] is the
framework; the regulator does not need to hear the
framework, but the regulator needs to see the head's
personal commitment to run it at depth.

**"How do we know this will not happen again."** The head
answers with the specific evidence the head will produce.
The head names the exercise cadence — the incident-time
filing rehearsal from Exercise 03 of
[[mod-104-regulator-engagement]] <!-- needs-research:
verify mod-104 exercise numbers and specifically that
Exercise 03 is the incident-time filing rehearsal --> and
the cross-function incident coordination rehearsal from
this module — that the enterprise runs on the [cadence];
the head names the specific evidence artefact the head
will provide to the regulator on request (the exercise
after-action reports, the delegate briefing logs, the
template-version ratification records). The head does not
promise "we will do better". The head names the specific
evidence.

**"Is there anything else about this incident we should
know."** The regulator's closing question, usually. The
head answers honestly. Where there is a related event, a
near-miss adjacent to the filed incident, a systemic
observation the head has arrived at through the incident
that the regulator would benefit from knowing, the head
names it — bounded, factual, without volunteering
detail beyond what the head has already coordinated with
GC. Where there is nothing further, the head says so
clearly. The head does not fill the silence with
elaboration; see the universal discipline on silence
below.

### What the head does not do

The head does not over-explain. The over-explaining
anti-pattern from [[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]]
chapter 06 <!-- needs-research: verify mod-109 chapter
number for the over-explaining anti-pattern in GC
coordination --> applies to the regulator conversation as
directly as it applies to the CEO conversation. Every
additional sentence the head volunteers is a sentence the
regulator can read as material to a broader inquiry. The
head answers what the regulator asked, in the compass the
regulator set, and stops.

The head does not under-explain. The mirror-image anti-
pattern is the head who answers in monosyllables or in
formulaic phrases that read as evasion. "We are looking
into it." "That is a matter for our internal review."
"We are not in a position to comment further on that."
Each of these reads to the regulator as the enterprise
holding material back. The regulator's follow-up letter
opens a formal investigation on that basis.

The head does not speculate beyond the enterprise's
confirmed account. The regulator may ask a question the
head has not yet resolved internally. The head names the
uncertainty — "the incident-time evaluation is still in
flight; the current bounded estimate of the affected
population is [X], with a confidence interval of [Y]; the
firmer estimate will be available on [date] and we will
communicate the update to your team through Regulator
Relations". Speculating past the enterprise's confirmed
account is the fastest route to a subsequent conversation
in which the regulator asks the head to reconcile the
speculation with the later firmer account.

The head does not breach privilege. Where the head's
answer would require characterising the substance of GC's
legal advice, or the internal deliberations of the head
and GC on the legal characterisation, the head defers to
GC on the call. GC either answers, defers, or asserts
privilege explicitly per the engagement-versus-legal-
opinion boundary from [[mod-104-regulator-engagement]]
chapter 07 <!-- needs-research: verify mod-104 chapter
number for the engagement-versus-legal-opinion boundary
-->. The head does not walk past privilege because the
head wants to appear transparent; the appearance costs the
enterprise substance.

The head does not commit corrective action on the call
that the head has not authorised internally. If the
regulator asks whether the enterprise will commit to a
specific control the regulator would like to see, the
head names what the enterprise has already commissioned,
names the process by which the head reviews further
corrective actions (the RCA and the learning cycle), and
names when the enterprise will communicate any additional
commitments. The head does not adopt corrective actions
on the fly to close down the regulator's line of
questioning.

### The closing frame and hand-off

The head closes with three lines. First, the head restates
the specific commitments the head has made on the call —
the corrective actions taken, the corrective actions
commissioned, the evidence artefacts the head will
provide, the follow-up communication dates. Second, the
head names the channel for follow-up — "Regulator
Relations will provide the exercise after-action reports
and the delegate briefing logs by [date] through the
standing correspondence channel; any further inquiries
from your team route through the same channel and I will
personally respond within [timeframe]". Third, the head
thanks the regulator for the conversation. The head does
not offer to reconvene without a specific reason; the
head does not extend the call. The call ends on the
regulator's terms.

## Conversation 4 — Briefing the media in coordination with Communications

### Participants and purpose

The head is almost never the on-record spokesperson for
an AI incident. The CCO is, or the CEO is, per the
enterprise's crisis-communications convention. The head
briefs the spokesperson — walks the spokesperson through
the AI-substantive account, the containment status, the
disclosure trajectory, the anticipated technical questions
and the coordinated answers — and steps out. The
spokesperson delivers.

The exception is the small number of cases where the head
is publicly named and asked to speak. Three common shapes.
A **specialist technical press interview** — a trade
publication or a specialist journalist with an AI beat
requests an interview with the head personally, on the
basis that only the head has the technical fluency the
interview requires; Communications assesses that
participation is net-positive for the enterprise's public-
trust posture and names the head as interviewee. A
**regulator-sponsored public briefing** — the national
competent authority, or a sector supervisor, or an AI
safety institute convenes a public briefing on an incident
class the enterprise's incident is one instance of, and
asks the enterprise to send a substantive representative;
the head is the natural attendee. A **parliamentary
committee open session** — a legislative committee holds
an open evidence session on an AI-related matter that
touches the enterprise's incident, and the head is named
as a witness or asked to appear alongside the CEO or
CCO. In each case the head is on-record.

The purpose of the appearance is to preserve public trust
through a disciplined factual account. Not to defend the
enterprise; not to promote the enterprise's AI programme;
not to advocate the head's personal views on AI policy.
Factual account, coordinated with Communications and
Legal, held under public scrutiny.

### The opening frame

The head opens with the coordinated frame that
Communications and Legal have pre-cleared. Not the head's
personal framing. The pre-cleared frame is one to three
sentences that name the incident in the enterprise's own
language, name what the enterprise has done about it, and
name the head's specific role in the account. Example
shape: "the enterprise experienced a class-5 AI incident
on [date], reported to the [national competent authority]
under Article 73 within the statutory window, with
containment held from [time] and a customer-facing
statement issued on [date]. My role today is to walk
through the technical dimension of the incident and to
answer questions on the enterprise's AI-substantive
response." The frame is deliberately narrow. The head
does not open with technical detail; the head opens with
the coordinated positioning statement.

Where the head is on a regulator-sponsored panel or a
parliamentary committee open session, the head's opening
statement is longer — typically two to five minutes,
pre-drafted and cleared with Communications and Legal.
The head does not depart from the cleared statement
under public pressure. Departing from the cleared
statement is the fastest route to a subsequent
conversation in which Communications and Legal have to
retract the departure.

### The three-to-five substantive questions the counterpart is likely to ask

**"What actually happened."** The head answers with the
coordinated factual account, at a level of technical
detail Communications and Legal have pre-cleared. The
head names the class of incident in accessible language;
the head names the outcome; the head names the
containment; the head names the disclosure. The head does
not go beyond the pre-cleared level of detail. Where the
journalist or the committee member presses for further
detail, the head declines factually — "the further
technical detail is captured in the incident-response
record and is being shared with the [national competent
authority] as part of the follow-up correspondence; the
enterprise's public account is the one I have provided".

**"Was this predictable."** The head answers honestly and
narrowly. Where the class of failure is one the sector
had discussed and the enterprise had considered, the head
says so and names the specific reason the enterprise's
control posture did not cover it in the specific incident.
Where the class of failure is genuinely novel — outside
the sector's current understanding — the head says so
and does not construct a retrospective account that
implies predictability. Speculating on predictability
beyond the enterprise's confirmed account is the head's
personal opinion, and the head's personal opinion is not
on-record in this conversation.

**"What are you doing to prevent recurrence."** The head
names the learning-cycle output, at the level Comms and
Legal have cleared. The specific artefact updates (the
RCA, the risk-register revision, the control-library
update, the policy revision, the board-facing summary)
from [[05-post-incident-learning-cycle-sponsorship]] are
the substantive answer; the coordinated positioning is
the delivery register. The head does not commit to
specific control designs on-record; the head names that
the learning cycle is under way, names the audit-
committee-visible closure date, and defers control-
specific detail to the standing enterprise reporting.

**"Do you take personal responsibility."** The head is
asked some version of this question in most on-record
appearances. The head answers with substance, not
performance. The head is the accountable executive for
the AI governance function; the head owns the AI
governance function's role in the incident; the head is
running the learning cycle personally. The head does not
narrate feelings — "I am devastated", "this keeps me up
at night" — because the media register reads narrated
feelings as evasion of substantive commitment. The head
names the substantive commitment: this is what I am
doing; this is by when.

**"What do you say to affected users / customers /
citizens."** The head answers in the enterprise's cleared
voice, which typically is a version of the CCO's
customer-facing statement. The head does not extend the
statement, does not add personal apology beyond what the
enterprise has published, and does not commit remedial
action beyond what the enterprise has coordinated with
Legal and Comms. The head names the channel through
which affected parties are being contacted directly if
the enterprise has one, and defers to the CCO's public
account for the enterprise-wide response.

### What the head does not do

The head does not speculate beyond the enterprise's
confirmed account. Any speculation — on cause, on
predictability, on the class of risk in the sector, on
the adequacy of current regulation, on what the head
would do differently — is on-record and durable.
Speculation that turns out wrong becomes the story the
next media cycle runs.

The head does not breach legal privilege, contract, or
ongoing investigation. Where an answer would require
disclosing GC's legal advice, characterising a supplier's
performance in a way that touches the supplier contract,
or commenting on the substance of an active regulator
investigation, the head declines directly — "that is a
matter I cannot comment on because [it is subject to
legal privilege / it is the subject of an active
regulator investigation / it touches confidential
supplier arrangements]; the enterprise's public account
is the one I have provided". The head does not attempt
to hint at the answer while declining to state it; the
hint is a partial disclosure and it will be treated as
the substantive answer.

The head does not step outside the coordinated position
with Communications and Legal. Where the counterpart asks
a question the head has not rehearsed the coordinated
answer to, the head defers — "that is a question I would
like to answer through the enterprise's coordinated
response rather than in this format; Communications can
provide a substantive follow-up through the standing
media channel". Deferring to the coordinated response is
not evasion; it is the discipline that keeps the
enterprise's public account coherent across every
subsequent media interaction.

The head does not advocate personal views. The head's
personal views on AI policy, on the appropriate design of
AI regulation, on the merits of the enterprise's own
strategic decisions, on other enterprises' incidents, are
not on-record. The head is present in the head's official
capacity; the personal capacity is off-limits for the
duration.

The head does not perform delivery. See the universal
discipline below on owning the substance rather than the
delivery. The head is not the enterprise's crisis-
communications professional; the head does not compete
with the CCO's delivery register. The head speaks in the
head's substantive register — precise, factual, bounded
— and lets Communications carry the delivery.

### The closing frame and hand-off

The head closes with the coordinated closing statement
that Communications and Legal have pre-cleared, if the
format supports one. For a technical press interview,
the closing is typically one sentence naming the channel
for follow-up questions — "further questions from your
publication route through Communications on the standing
media contact channel and will receive a substantive
response within [timeframe]". For a regulator-sponsored
public briefing or a parliamentary open session, the
closing is often a formal thank-you to the convening body
and a statement of the enterprise's continuing engagement
with the process. The head does not linger, does not
take questions after the formal close, and does not
speak to journalists off-record on the way out. The
appearance ends on the coordinated terms.

## Three universal disciplines

Three disciplines carry across all four conversations. Each
is easier to state than to hold, and each is the specific
place where the head who has run the substantive work well
fails on delivery.

### Own the substance, not the delivery

The head is the substantive expert on the AI dimension of
the enterprise's exposure. The head is not the enterprise's
spokesperson, not the enterprise's storyteller, not the
enterprise's crisis-communications professional. Those
roles belong to Communications, to the CEO, to the crisis-
communications retainer the enterprise may engage under
[[mod-109-cross-enterprise-leadership]] chapter 05
<!-- needs-research: verify mod-109 chapter number for CCO
coordination and crisis-communications retainer -->.

The temptation, under public scrutiny, is for the head to
compete on delivery — to attempt polish, to attempt
narrative pacing, to attempt emotional register. The
temptation is misplaced. The counterpart (the CEO, the
board, the regulator, the journalist) is not evaluating
the head on delivery. The counterpart is evaluating the
head on substantive precision. A head who delivers a
technically precise, bounded, factual account in a plain
register is credible; a head who delivers a polished
narrative that is technically imprecise is not.

The discipline in practice: speak in the head's own
substantive register. Precise verbs. Numeric detail where
the head has confidence in it. Named uncertainty where
the head does not. Short sentences. No metaphors the head
has not rehearsed with Communications. No rhetorical
flourishes. Substance is the head's contribution;
delivery is someone else's.

### Do not fill silence

Silence in the room is not the head's problem to solve.
Counterparts use silence as a diagnostic. The CEO asks a
question, the head answers, the CEO does not respond for
five seconds. The regulator asks a question, the head
answers, the regulator writes something down and does
not respond for ten seconds. The journalist asks a
question, the head answers, the journalist looks at
their notes and does not respond for fifteen seconds.

The head's temptation is to fill the silence with
elaboration. "To add to that" or "what I meant to say
was" or "further to my previous point". Each additional
sentence the head volunteers into the silence is a
sentence the counterpart did not ask for and can now
narrow with a follow-up question. The narrowing question
is one the head has not prepared for, because the head
volunteered the material the narrowing rests on.

The discipline in practice: answer, stop, wait. The
silence is the counterpart's. If the counterpart wants
more, the counterpart asks. If the counterpart moves on,
the head's answer was sufficient. Filling silence is
the single most common on-record error the head makes,
and it is the error with the largest downstream cost
because it opens material the head did not have to open.

### Name what you do not know

The head who claims certainty on a fact the head does
not have is one incident away from a durable credibility
loss. In every one of the four conversations, the
counterpart will ask a question the head does not yet
know the answer to. The right move is not to construct a
plausible-sounding answer; the right move is to name the
uncertainty explicitly.

The discipline in practice: name the uncertainty, name
when the enterprise will know, name the follow-up
cadence. "The affected-population estimate is currently
bounded at [X] with confidence interval [Y]; the firmer
estimate will be available on [date]; I will communicate
the update through [channel]." The three-part answer is
what the counterpart wants; the plausible-sounding guess
is what the counterpart accepts in the moment and holds
the head to later.

Uncertainty owned is not weakness. The head who names
uncertainty at the CEO briefing on Day 1 and returns on
Day 2 with the firmer estimate is the head whose Day 3
briefing is trusted. The head who claims certainty on
Day 1 and revises on Day 2 has lost the counterpart's
trust for the arc of the incident and, often, for the
arc of the head's tenure. Uncertainty hidden is
weakness; uncertainty owned is discipline.

## The rehearsal discipline

The four conversations are rehearsed. The rehearsal is
what turns the disciplined shape into muscle memory. The
head who has run the CEO briefing three times in a
rehearsed setting, with a rehearsed counterpart, runs the
live briefing under fatigue and pressure with the shape
intact. The head who has never rehearsed the shape runs
the live briefing on improvisation and rediscovers under
maximum cost that improvisation does not survive first
contact with real pressure.

The rehearsal cadence has three tiers.

**Annual rehearsal.** The four conversations are rehearsed
in a bounded annual exercise, typically one calendar day
in aggregate, held during a peace-time window when the
head and the head's peers can commit the attention. The
annual rehearsal covers the full shape of each of the
four conversations, run against a scenario the exercise
designer selects from the calibration-from-public-
incident-data discipline in
[[07-calibration-from-public-incident-data]] so that the
scenario is realistic and specific to the enterprise's
current risk surface. The annual rehearsal is where the
head, the head's delegate, and the head's peers
(especially the CCO, the CCO's incident-communications
lead, and the Regulator Relations lead) rehearse the
coordination between the four conversations.

**Quarterly rehearsal.** A single conversation is rehearsed
each quarter, on a rotating basis, in a shorter window
(typically two hours). The quarterly rehearsal is where
the head keeps the specific shape of each conversation
sharp — the opening frame, the question set, the
disciplines, the closing frame — without the coordination
overhead of the full annual rehearsal. The quarterly
rehearsal is also where the head's delegate rotates into
the primary seat so that the delegate's rehearsal count
tracks the head's.

**On-demand rehearsal.** The head runs a specific
rehearsal on demand when a specific conversation is
imminent. If the head has scheduled a CEO briefing for
tomorrow morning on a live incident, the head runs a
thirty-minute cold rehearsal of the CEO briefing shape
that afternoon with the head's chief of staff or the
head's delegate playing the CEO. The on-demand rehearsal
is not a substitute for the annual and quarterly
rehearsals; it is the acute-phase tuning that a
rehearsed foundation makes possible.

The counterpart in each rehearsal is not the head. The
head cannot rehearse against the head. Four options for
counterpart depend on which conversation and which
tier:

- **A peer head-of-role.** For the CEO briefing and the
  board briefing, a peer head from a different function
  (the CISO, the CCO, the CRO) plays the counterpart. The
  peer's fluency with the CEO's or board's actual
  register is what makes the rehearsal realistic.
- **An external coach.** For the board briefing, an
  external executive coach with sector-specific fluency
  runs the counterpart role. The coach's neutrality is
  what makes it possible to run rehearsals that would be
  socially difficult inside the enterprise.
- **A former regulator.** For the regulator conversation,
  a former senior official from the relevant national
  competent authority or sector supervisor — typically
  engaged through a specialist advisory retainer — plays
  the regulator. The former regulator's fluency with the
  actual style of a regulator follow-up call is
  irreplaceable.
- **A media-training professional.** For the media
  appearance, a specialist media-training firm — often
  the same firm the CCO engages for the enterprise's
  crisis-communications retainer — runs the counterpart
  role. The media-training professional's fluency with
  specialist-press interview technique, parliamentary-
  committee questioning, and regulator-sponsored panel
  format is what the head cannot substitute for
  internally.

The rehearsal is scored against the disciplined shape.
Did the head hold the opening frame; did the head answer
the counterpart's substantive questions with the
discipline the shape prescribes; did the head hold the
"does not do" list; did the head close with the shape's
closing frame. Scoring is done by the counterpart, by
the head's chief of staff or the head's delegate observing
the rehearsal, and by the head self-scoring in a short
after-action note. The scoring is captured; a rehearsal
that surfaces a specific weakness in the head's shape
becomes an input to the next tier's rehearsal.

Rehearsal composes with calibration. The scenario for
each rehearsal is selected from the public-incident-data
sources treated in
[[07-calibration-from-public-incident-data]] — the AI
Incident Database, the OECD.AI incidents monitor, the
MIT AI Risk Repository, sector-specific incident lists
where they exist. Rehearsing against a scenario drawn
from public incident data is what keeps the head's
rehearsal calibrated to the actual class of incident the
enterprise's peers experience, rather than to the head's
personal imagination of what an incident might look
like.

Rehearsal extends to the delegate. The pre-nominated
delegate from
[[01-incident-command-shape-at-leadership-scope]] —
typically the senior AI governance architect at level 50
— rehearses each of the four conversations on the same
cadence the head does. The rehearsal is not a nice-to-
have; it is what allows the delegate to run the CEO
briefing or the regulator follow-up call on the day the
head is unavailable without the delegate's presence
being the delegate's first. The head's succession
obligation is that the delegate is never first-time on
any of the four conversations.

## Summary

- Incidents produce a small number of conversations the
  head personally runs under time pressure and public
  scrutiny. These are not bridge conversations; they are
  one-to-one or small-group where the head's presence,
  framing, and language directly shape the enterprise's
  exposure. The failure mode is the head who is
  technically competent on the bridge but fumbles the
  briefing outside it.
- Four conversations are treated in this chapter:
  briefing the CEO on a serious incident; briefing the
  full board on a systemic failure; briefing a regulator
  on a late or incomplete filing; briefing the media in
  coordination with Communications.
- Each conversation has a disciplined shape: named
  participants and purpose, an opening frame the head
  owns, a bounded set of substantive questions the
  counterpart is likely to ask with a discipline for
  answering each, an enumerated list of moves the head
  does not make, and a closing frame that hands off
  cleanly.
- In the CEO briefing the head arrives with the AI-
  substantive account, the containment status, the
  disclosure-trigger assessment, and a recommendation
  (not a menu) on the specific enterprise-level decision
  the meeting exists to close. Open technical questions
  belong on the bridge, not in the CEO's office.
- In the board briefing on a systemic failure the head
  names the class of failure directly, names why the
  enterprise did not carry it, names the specific
  changes commissioned through the learning cycle with
  named dates, and names accountability substantively.
  The head does not defend past decisions the head did
  not own; the head does not perform contrition beyond
  what the substance warrants.
- In the regulator follow-up on a late or incomplete
  filing the head names the gap in factual terms, the
  cause, the immediate corrective action, and the
  systemic corrective action from the learning cycle.
  The head does not over-explain and does not under-
  explain; the head does not breach privilege; the head
  does not commit corrective actions on the fly.
- In the media appearance the head is on-record only in
  the small number of cases where Communications and
  Legal have coordinated the position and named the head
  as the substantive representative. The head speaks in
  the enterprise's coordinated voice, does not
  speculate beyond the confirmed account, does not
  breach privilege or contract or active investigation,
  and does not advocate personal views.
- Three universal disciplines carry across all four:
  own the substance, not the delivery; do not fill
  silence; name what you do not know. Each is easier to
  state than to hold, and each is where the head who
  has run the substantive work well fails on delivery.
- Rehearsal is what turns the disciplined shape into
  muscle memory. The rehearsal cadence has three tiers:
  annual (full shape, one day, all four conversations),
  quarterly (single conversation, rotating), and on-
  demand (acute-phase tuning before a specific live
  conversation). Counterparts are peer heads-of-role,
  external coaches, former regulators, and media-
  training professionals — never the head. Rehearsals
  are scored against the disciplined shape and
  calibrated against public incident data from chapter
  07.
- The delegate rehearses each of the four conversations
  on the same cadence the head does, so that the
  delegate's presence at any of them on the day the
  head is unavailable is not the delegate's first.
